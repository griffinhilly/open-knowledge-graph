## Flags
- domains/history/economic-social-history/agricultural-revolution-neolithic.md, Questions item 4: "several large domesticable mammals (cattle, horses, pigs, sheep, goats)" in the Fertile Crescent. Horses were domesticated on the Eurasian steppe, not in the Fertile Crescent.
- domains/history/economic-social-history/agricultural-revolution-neolithic.md, Questions item 0: "the Andes (potatoes, ca. 3500 BCE)". Most estimates put potato domestication several thousand years earlier (roughly 8000-5000 BCE). Also Core Idea says "About 10,000 years ago" while the Explainer says "About 12,000 years ago"; minor inconsistency.
- domains/history/economic-social-history/ancient-trade-networks.md, Explainer: "predictable monsoon winds: northeast in summer, southwest in winter". By wind-naming convention the summer monsoon is the southwest monsoon (blowing toward the northeast). The question answer phrases it correctly as sailing direction; the Explainer reads as wind names and is reversed.
- domains/history/economic-social-history/atlantic-slave-trade-economics.md, Core Idea: "(roughly 1500-1800)" conflicts with the Explainer's "Between 1500 and 1875"; the trade continued well past 1800 (Brazil to 1850, Cuba to the 1860s). Core Idea also says "it was an aberration — that free labor is more productive"; this is a contested claim stated as fact (Fogel and Engerman argued slave plantations were efficient).
- domains/history/economic-social-history/bretton-woods-system.md, Core Idea: "you cannot have a gold standard, fixed exchange rates, and independent monetary policy simultaneously ... (the impossible trinity)". The trilemma is fixed exchange rates, free capital mobility, and independent monetary policy, as the file's own last question states.
- domains/history/economic-social-history/bretton-woods-system.md, Explainer: quote "the most important economic decision since FDR" attributed to George Shultz; could not confirm the attribution.
- domains/history/economic-social-history/consumer-society-emergence.md, Explainer: "Consumer society ... did not exist before the 20th century." Historians (e.g. McKendrick et al., The Birth of a Consumer Society, 1982; de Vries on the "industrious revolution") locate a consumer revolution in 18th-century Britain and the Netherlands. Stated too absolutely.
- domains/history/economic-social-history/cotton-economy-slavery.md, Explainer: "its production rested entirely on enslaved labor". Cotton was also grown by free farmers (e.g. India, later Egypt); "entirely" is too strong. Core Idea calls the 19th century "early modern capitalism"; the period is modern.
- domains/history/economic-social-history/demographic-transition-history.md, Explainer: "Global population, which had been roughly stable at 1-2 billion for centuries". World population first reached 1 billion around 1800 and 2 billion around 1927; it was well below 1 billion for all earlier centuries. Also Questions item 2 explanation: "(for Africa) was used as forced labor through slavery" garbles the demographic-transition point.
- domains/history/economic-social-history/digital-economy-history.md, Questions item 1 (MC answer): ARPANET "was funded by DARPA to enable resilient communications". ARPANET's stated purpose was resource sharing among research computers; the nuclear-survivability motive belongs to Paul Baran's earlier RAND work and is a common conflation.
- domains/history/economic-social-history/enclosure-movement.md, Questions item 4 answer and Explainer: "Robert Allen found that enclosed farms did achieve higher agricultural productivity — they adopted crop rotation ... faster than open-field farms". Allen's Enclosure and the Yeoman (1992) argued the opposite on productivity: open-field farmers adopted many improvements, and enclosure's main effect was redistribution to landlords via rents. The rent finding is right; the productivity attribution is not.
- domains/history/economic-social-history/energy-transitions-history.md, Questions item 0 explanation: mine flooding "which James Watt's steam engine (1769) helped solve". Newcomen's engine (1712) was the one first used to pump mines; Watt improved efficiency. The Explainer has it right.
- domains/history/economic-social-history/environmental-history-overview.md, Questions item 1 (true-false, answer true): the Mesopotamian salinization-and-decline thesis (Jacobsen and Adams 1958) has been substantially challenged (e.g. Powell 1985); presenting it as settled fact is questionable.
- domains/history/economic-social-history/factory-system-labor.md, Explainer: "The 10 Hours Act (1847) limited textile workers to 10-hour days". The act limited women and young persons; adult men were covered only indirectly.
- domains/history/economic-social-history/famine-history.md, Explainer: Soviet 1932-33 and Chinese 1959-61 famines "were the largest famines in human history". True of the Chinese famine; the Soviet famine is not clearly larger than e.g. the 1876-79 famines in China and India. Explainer also says Bengal 1943 food was "available and being exported"; Sen's argument rests on purchasing power, not exports.
- domains/history/economic-social-history/globalization-history.md, Questions item 3 explanation: "or offshore domestically (moving production to another country but keeping it in-house)" is self-contradictory wording; probably meant "or offshore in-house".
- domains/history/economic-social-history/gold-standard-history.md, Explainer: the gold standard "depended on unique conditions — British hegemony, limited capital mobility". The 1870-1914 era had high capital mobility, as the same Explainer says two paragraphs earlier ("Capital flowed freely across borders").
- domains/history/economic-social-history/great-depression-causes-responses.md, Questions item 1 explanation: Smoot-Hawley "helped turn an American recession into a global depression". Many economic historians (e.g. Irwin, Eichengreen) judge its macroeconomic effect modest relative to monetary causes; stated too strongly. Explainer: "the Civilian Conservation Corps employed 300,000 young men" reads as a total; about 3 million served over its life.
- domains/history/economic-social-history/hanseatic-league-trade.md, Questions item 1: "Dried and salted cod (stockfish)". Stockfish is air-dried and unsalted; salted dried cod is klippfisk. Questions item 4 answer credits the Hansa with spreading marine insurance and "the Hamburg and Lübeck Borse"; marine insurance developed in Italy, Hanseatic merchants were notably slow to adopt it, and the Hamburg exchange dates from 1558, after the League's peak.
- domains/history/economic-social-history/history-of-banking.md, Explainer: "Their invention of the letter of credit, along with developments in double-entry bookkeeping (attributed to Luca Pacioli, 1494)". The Medici did not invent bills of exchange or letters of credit (Italian merchants used them from the 13th century), and Pacioli described double-entry bookkeeping, which was already in use; he did not originate it. Also Core Idea says banking "emerged in medieval Europe" while the Explainer places the earliest banks in ancient Mesopotamia.
- domains/history/economic-social-history/history-of-education.md, Questions item 3 explanation: "Title IX of the Higher Education Act (1972)". Title IX is part of the Education Amendments of 1972, not the Higher Education Act (option D repeats the error).
- domains/history/economic-social-history/history-of-insurance.md, Questions item 3: typed true-false but phrased as an open question ("What strategic goal motivated it?") with answer false; malformed item. Its explanation also says "Germany avoided the social revolution that Marx had predicted", overlooking the 1918-19 German Revolution. Explainer lists "the 1906 San Francisco earthquake" among "major shipping losses".
- domains/history/economic-social-history/history-of-money.md, Core Idea: money "emerges when communities need a medium of exchange beyond barter" conflicts with the file's own Questions item 0 and Explainer, which reject the barter-origin story. Questions item 3: typed true-false but phrased as an open question; malformed. Questions item 1 explanation attributes "flying money" (feiqian) to the Song; it was a Tang-era instrument. Explainer: "Bitcoin lost 80% of its value in 2022"; the calendar-2022 drop was about 65% (about 77% from the November 2021 peak).
- domains/history/economic-social-history/history-of-taxation.md, Core Idea: "regressive taxes (same rate for all)". A single rate on income is proportional; a regressive tax takes a smaller share from higher incomes, as the Explainer correctly says. Questions item 3: typed true-false but phrased as an open question with answer false, while the explanation argues there is a difference; malformed.
- domains/history/economic-social-history/industrial-revolution-economic-impact.md, Questions item 3: typed true-false but phrased as an open question ("What was that mechanism?") with answer true; malformed.
- domains/history/economic-social-history/inequality-historical-patterns.md, Explainer: Rome's estimated Gini of "around 0.43-0.50 — comparable to highly unequal modern societies like Brazil or South Africa". South Africa's Gini is about 0.63 and Brazil's about 0.52, so the comparison overstates. Core Idea: "Global inequality has not clearly trended ... (increasing global inequality)"; most estimates (e.g. Milanovic) show global interpersonal inequality falling since around 1990-2000, mainly from growth in China and India.
- domains/history/economic-social-history/informal-economy-history.md, Questions item 3: typed true-false but phrased as an open question; malformed. Questions item 4 answer: "UK and California courts ... both ruled Uber drivers are employees". The UK Supreme Court (2021) ruled they are "workers", a distinct intermediate category. Questions item 1 explanation cites the 289-day registration figure from The Mystery of Capital; it comes from de Soto's The Other Path (1986).
- domains/history/economic-social-history/labor-movement-history.md, Questions item 1 answer: Haymarket "killing a police officer ... Eight people died total". Seven police officers and at least four civilians died. It also says the Knights of Labor launched the May 1886 strike; Knights leadership opposed it. Explainer, Homestead: "nine workers and seven Pinkertons were killed"; the usual count is seven workers and three Pinkertons. Questions item 3 is a true-false item whose answer ("true") is a contested evaluative claim.
- domains/history/economic-social-history/medieval-manorial-economy.md, Questions item 0: the correct option says output rose "by about 50%", while its explanation computes "a roughly 33% increase in productive land". Explainer: "By 1400 in England, serfdom had largely disappeared"; it declined through the 15th century and was largely gone around 1500. Explainer treats serfs as a class below villeins; in English usage the terms are largely synonymous. Questions item 4 explanation repeats the Allen productivity attribution flagged under enclosure-movement.
- domains/history/economic-social-history/mercantilism-and-colonialism.md, Explainer: VOC "became the world's first joint-stock company"; the English East India Company (1600) and earlier companies were joint-stock, the VOC is usually called the first publicly traded or permanent joint-stock company. Explainer: Potosí poured "perhaps 40,000 tons" into the economy, while Questions item 0 gives 40,000 tons as all American silver reaching Europe 1500-1800. Explainer: Meiji Japan "used protective tariffs"; unequal treaties capped Japan's tariffs until tariff autonomy in 1911.
