## Flags

### Suspected factual errors in existing text
- bessel-functions.md, Explainer: "Y_ν(x) ... diverges logarithmically as x → 0". The divergence is logarithmic only for ν = 0; for ν > 0, Y_ν blows up like x^(−ν).
- complex-roots-oscillatory-solutions.md, Explainer: "The imaginary part β sets the angular frequency of oscillation — how many complete cycles occur per unit of x". Angular frequency is radians per unit x; cycles per unit x is β/(2π).
- convolution-theorem.md, Explainer: "The solution to y″ + p(t)y′ + q(t)y = g(t) can often be written as y(t) = ∫₀ᵗ h(t − τ)g(τ) dτ". The h(t − τ) form needs constant coefficients (time invariance) and zero initial conditions; with p(t), q(t) the kernel is not a function of t − τ.
- differential-equations-intro.md, Explainer: "Your prerequisite of integration by parts is already a direct solving technique: some first-order equations can be solved by separating variables". Mixes up integration by parts and separation of variables.
- exact-differential-equations.md, Q3 (true-false, answer false): "can generally be made exact by multiplying through by an appropriate integrating factor". For a smooth equation an integrating factor exists locally in theory; what fails is finding it in closed form. The question conflates existence with findability.
- first-order-linear-odes.md, Q2 (true-false, answer true): "with P(x) > 0, the homogeneous solution always decays to zero". False as stated: P(x) = 1/x² on [1, ∞) has a bounded integral, so e^(−∫P) tends to a nonzero limit. The explanation's claim that ∫P grows without bound is also wrong in general.
- fourier-series-definition.md, Q0: answer is 2 ("(2/L) ∫ f cos(πx/L) dx"), but the explanation says the standard formula is (1/L) ∫ (option 1). With the a₀/2 convention in the Core Idea, the answer should be 1.
- fourier-series-definition.md, Q1: "A Fourier series typically converges to f(x) at most point where f is defined" marked false. For piecewise smooth f it does converge to f at all but finitely many points per period; the item is garbled ("most point") and its key is doubtful.
- fourier-series-definition.md, Explainer: "twice that frequency (the first harmonic)". Usual convention: the fundamental is the first harmonic, twice it is the second harmonic; the next sentence's "a₂ ... second harmonic" is inconsistent with this.
- frobenius-method.md, Explainer: "if r₁ = r₂ or r₁ - r₂ is a positive integer, the second solution involves ln(x - x₀)". When r₁ − r₂ is a positive integer, a log term may or may not appear; Q0's explanation in the same file states this correctly.
- laplace-transform-of-derivatives.md, Explainer: "y'' + 3y' + 2y = eˡ" is a typo for eᵗ.
- laplaces-equation.md, Explainer: "a Dirichlet or Neumann boundary condition on a closed boundary uniquely determines the solution". Neumann data fix the solution only up to an additive constant and need a compatibility condition.
- laplaces-equation.md, Explainer: "The contrast with the wave equation (your prerequisite)". The wave equation is not a listed prerequisite.
- legendre-equations.md, Explainer: "if n is a non-negative integer, the recurrence terminates". Only the series of the same parity as n terminates; the other stays infinite. It also calls the method at an ordinary point "the Frobenius method".
- phase-portraits-linear-systems.md, Explainer: "if not (a Jordan block), trajectories spiral mildly before straightening". An improper (degenerate) node has curved trajectories but no spiraling; trajectories do not wind around the origin.
- power-series-solutions.md, Explainer: "the simple harmonic oscillator with non-constant forcing — have variable coefficients". Forcing does not make the coefficients variable. Q1 also has a typo "e₊".
- repeated-roots-reduction-of-order.md, Explainer: "you end up with w'' = 0 (after substitution), so w is constant". It should be v'' = 0 (that is, w' = 0).
- resonance-and-damping.md, Explainer and Q3: "until at critical damping the response has no resonance peak at all". The amplitude peak vanishes once c² ≥ 2km (damping ratio 1/√2), before critical damping.
- resonance-and-damping.md, Explainer: Tacoma Narrows (1940) given as "the classic engineering illustration of resonance". It is usually explained as aeroelastic flutter, not simple forced resonance.
