## Flags

### Suspected factual errors in existing text
1. separability-topology.md, Q1: answer says ℝ^ℝ with the product topology "is not separable." By the Hewitt–Marczewski–Pondiczery theorem, a product of at most continuum many separable spaces is separable, so ℝ^ℝ is separable. All four options are then separable, so the question has no correct answer as written.
2. product-topology-construction.md, Core Idea: "products of Hausdorff/compact/connected spaces are Hausdorff/compact/connected (by Tychonoff)". Tychonoff covers compactness only. The Hausdorff and connected results are separate theorems.
3. product-topology-construction.md, Q3 explanation, last sentence: "the box topology is strictly coarser." It is strictly finer, as the same explanation says earlier.
4. regularity-t3-axiom.md, Core Idea: "regularity is preserved under continuous images (unlike T₂)". False. Every space is a continuous image of a discrete space, which is regular. Neither regularity nor T₂ survives continuous images.
5. quotient-maps-and-identification.md, Q2 explanation: "there are open sets in S¹ whose preimages are not open in [0,1)". f is continuous, so open sets have open preimages. The failure goes the other way: some non-open sets (such as the image of [0, 1/2)) have open preimages. The same explanation calls the circle topology "finer"; it is coarser than the quotient topology.
6. quotient-maps-definition.md, Core Idea: "the torus is ℝ² with 'opposite edges identified'". It should be the unit square [0,1]² (or ℝ²/ℤ²).
7. quotient-maps-definition.md, Q4 answer: "a stronger one would require q to be an open map". A finer topology on Y would make q discontinuous; it has nothing to do with openness of q. The Explainer and the Q4 explanation also say a coarser topology "would declare some set U closed even though q⁻¹(U) is open"; it should be "not open".
8. separation-axioms.md, Q3 explanation: in the Sorgenfrey plane "the two axes are disjoint closed sets". The axes meet at the origin. The standard witness is the rational and irrational points of the antidiagonal x + y = 0.
9. separation-axioms.md, Explainer: the complement of {x} "is the intersection of all open sets containing each other point". It is the union.
10. separation-axioms.md, Q0: T₂ is called "sufficient (and necessary)" for unique sequential limits. It is not necessary. The cocountable topology on an uncountable set has unique sequential limits but is not Hausdorff.
11. separation-axioms-t3-regular.md, Explainer: Urysohn construction "U_r ⊆ closure(U_s) whenever r < s". It should be closure(U_r) ⊆ U_s. urysohns-lemma.md Explainer has a similar slip ("U(r) ⊆ U(s)", where the proof needs the closure of U(r) inside U(s)).
12. Hierarchy written backwards as inclusions: regular-spaces.md Q2 ("T1 ⊂ T2 ⊂ T3 ⊂ T4"), separation-axioms-t3-regular.md Q3, regularity-t3-axiom.md Q4 and Explainer ("T₂ ⊂ T₃ ⊂ T₄"). As classes of spaces it is T₄ ⊂ T₃ ⊂ T₂ ⊂ T₁, and only with T₁ assumed.
13. urysohn-metrization-theorem.md, Core Idea and urysohns-lemma.md Explainer: "second-countable normal space is metrizable" needs T₁ (or Hausdorff). The two-point indiscrete space is second-countable and normal but not metrizable. The Q4 answer also says the countable family "lands in ℓ²(ℕ)". That needs scaling (for example fₙ/n), since (1, 1, 1, …) is not in ℓ².
14. urysohns-lemma.md, Q2 explanation: "Normal spaces form a proper subclass of Hausdorff spaces". This fails without T₁ (the Sierpiński space is normal and not Hausdorff).
15. tietze-extension-theorem.md, Explainer: "one characterization of metrizable spaces is that they are normal". Metrizable implies normal, but normality does not characterize metrizability. The same paragraph also says Urysohn metrization needs "normality combined with second-countability"; the standard hypothesis is regular (T₃) + second-countable.
16. van-kampen-theorem.md, Explainer (torus paragraph): "U ∩ V is a circle (simply connected intersection gives a free product ...)". U ∩ V is an annulus with π₁ = ℤ, not simply connected. It also says U deformation-retracts to "the boundary square (a loop aba⁻¹b⁻¹)". The punctured torus retracts onto the wedge of two circles a ∨ b, and the commutator is the image of the puncture's boundary. Q1's explanation repeats the "boundary square" wording.
17. path-connected-spaces.md, Explainer: "all manifolds you encounter in calculus are path-connected". Disconnected manifolds exist (for example, the two-branch hyperbola). The true statement is that connected manifolds are path-connected.
18. topological-manifolds-introduction.md, Q4 answer: the sphere, torus and projective plane are said to be spaces "that cannot be embedded in any Euclidean space without distortion or self-intersection". S² and T² embed in ℝ³, and every manifold embeds in some ℝᴺ.
19. simply-connected-spaces.md, Explainer: the Poincaré conjecture "concerns which 3-manifolds are simply connected and topologically equivalent to the 3-sphere". The conjecture says every simply connected closed 3-manifold is homeomorphic to S³.
20. sequential-compactness.md, Q2 explanation: "sequentially compact spaces that are not compact (uncountable products can exhibit this)". Uncountable products show the other direction (compact but not sequentially compact). The standard sequentially compact, non-compact example is [0, ω₁).
21. sequential-compactness-metric-spaces.md, Explainer: "Uncountable products with the product topology (Tychonoff spaces)". "Tychonoff space" means completely regular Hausdorff. The Core Idea says "infinite products"; countable products of sequentially compact spaces are sequentially compact, so it should say uncountable. Arzelà–Ascoli is also stated as "sequentially compact iff uniformly bounded and equicontinuous", where the standard statement is relative compactness (closure compact).
22. product-topology.md, Core Idea: "generated by the basis of boxes (products of open sets from each factor)". For infinite products this describes the box topology. The product topology restricts only finitely many factors.
23. subspace-topology.md, Explainer: "the unique topology on A for which the inclusion map ι: A → X ... is continuous". It is not unique (the discrete topology also works); it is the coarsest. It also says "{0} is open in A if A has the discrete subspace topology". For A = [0,1], {0} is not open, so the sentence is confusing.
24. topological-spaces-definition.md, Q2 explanation: "Any set X carries at least two topologies". This fails for sets with at most one element, where discrete and indiscrete coincide.
