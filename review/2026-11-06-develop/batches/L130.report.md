## Flags

Suspected factual errors in existing text:
- thomas-hobbes-social-contract.md, Core Idea: "Hobbes argued for absolute monarchy as the only guarantee of order". Hobbes allowed the sovereign to be one person or an assembly and preferred monarchy; the file's own Explainer says "one person or assembly". The two sections contradict each other.
- thomas-hobbes-social-contract.md, Explainer: "God barely enters the argument" (and Q3 explanation "without significant appeal to religion"). Parts III and IV of *Leviathan* are given over largely to religion and the Christian commonwealth. Overstated.
- spanish-inquisition-religious-authority.md, Core Idea: the Inquisition "investigating ... the expulsion of Jews and Muslims". The expulsions were royal decrees, not Inquisition proceedings. Wording is muddled.
- spanish-inquisition-religious-authority.md, Explainer: "limpieza de sangre ... emerged alongside Inquisition activity". Purity-of-blood statutes predate the 1478 founding (Toledo statute of 1449). Chronology is loose.
- treaty-of-westphalia.md, Core Idea: "establishing that rulers could determine religion within their territories" and "system of independent nations". Westphalia fixed a normative year (1624) that limited rulers' power to change their subjects' religion, and "nations" is anachronistic. The file's own Explainer also adopts the "Westphalian myth" critique, which sits uneasily with the Core Idea's "origin of the modern international state system".
- state-centralization-sovereignty.md, Core Idea: Westphalia "established the system of independent states" and "consolidated nation-states". This is the Westphalian-myth framing that treaty-of-westphalia.md warns against, and "nation-states" is anachronistic for c. 1700.
- thirty-years-war.md, Explainer: "codified the principle that sovereign states had the right to determine their own internal affairs — including religion". Same Westphalian-myth and normative-year issue as above.
- separation-of-powers-doctrine.md, Explainer: lists "courts could strike down laws" among the tools the 1787 system gave. Judicial review is not stated explicitly in the Constitution; it was established in practice by Marbury v. Madison (1803). Minor.
