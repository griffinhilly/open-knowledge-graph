## Flags

Suspected factual errors in existing text:

1. globalization-and-labor.md, Core Idea: "trade with low-wage countries reduces wages for the abundant factor in those countries (low-skilled labor in developed nations)". Low-skilled labor is the scarce factor in developed nations. Stolper-Samuelson says trade lowers the real return of the scarce factor and raises that of the abundant factor. The Explainer and question 0 state it correctly.
2. search-and-matching-models.md, Explainer: "When aggregate demand is strong, firms post many vacancies and unemployment is low (northeast of the curve). When demand is weak ... (southwest of the curve)." With unemployment on the horizontal axis and vacancies on the vertical, high V and low U is the upper left (northwest). Low V and high U is the lower right (southeast).
3. minimum-wage-economics.md, Explainer: Cengiz et al. (2019) "found that the number of jobs paying just below the new minimum increased by about the same amount as the number paying at the new minimum". The finding is that jobs below the new minimum fell (missing jobs) by about as much as jobs at or just above it rose (excess jobs). "Increased" should be "decreased".
4. lifecycle-labor-supply.md, Core Idea: "the U-shaped lifecycle earnings profile". The lifecycle earnings profile is hump-shaped (inverted U), as other files in the course describe (rising, then flattening).
5. lifecycle-labor-supply.md, Explainer: "taxi drivers work more hours on rainy days (when wages per hour are temporarily high due to demand)". This is contested. Camerer et al. (1997) found drivers quit earlier on high-wage days (negative elasticity); Farber later disputed that. It should not be stated as settled fact.
6. lifecycle-labor-supply.md, Explainer: US female labor force participation "57% in 2000". The BLS figure for 2000 is about 60%; about 57% matches around 1990. Worth checking.
7. labor-unions.md, Core Idea: "declined ... since the 1970s, from about 35% to under 10% in the US private sector". The roughly 35% figure is the mid-1950s peak (the Explainer says "1950s"). Private-sector density in the 1970s was well below 35%.
8. intergenerational-mobility.md, Core Idea: the IGE "measures the correlation between parents' and children's earnings". The IGE is a regression elasticity, not a correlation; they differ when inequality changes across generations. Also, the question 0 explanation's "a parent at the 90th percentile produces a child expected at the 70th percentile" uses rank-rank logic, which applies to the rank-rank slope, not the log-earnings IGE.
9. informal-labor-markets.md, question 2 explanation: "mobile payment-based social insurance (Kenya's M-Pesa)". M-Pesa is a mobile money transfer service, not a social insurance scheme. Also minor: the Core Idea gives 50-90% of non-agricultural employment, while question 0 says 60-80% of total employment.
10. unemployment-theory.md, question 1 explanation: the US NAIRU fell "to below 4% in the 2020s". The common official estimates (for example CBO's noncyclical rate) for the 2020s sit around 4.4%, not below 4%. Worth checking.
11. gender-wage-gap.md, Explainer: "countries with generous parental leave ... show smaller child penalties. This suggests that the penalty reflects institutional ... structures". Kleven and coauthors' later work on Austrian reforms found parental leave and childcare expansions had little effect on child penalties. Cross-country variation lines up more with gender norms. Softening this claim may be warranted.
12. discrimination-economics.md, Explainer: the Oaxaca-Blinder residual is "often interpreted as an upper bound on discrimination". It can be biased either way, for example downward if controls such as occupation are themselves shaped by discrimination. Minor. The new misconception text describes it as approximate, consistent with the file's own "approximate".
