## Flags

### Suspected factual errors in existing text
- sequential-characterization-continuity.md, Explainer para 3: "Therefore: if f fails the sequential condition, f is discontinuous." The argument above it proves the reverse implication (discontinuous ⇒ fails the sequential condition, i.e. sequential ⇒ continuous). The stated conclusion is the contrapositive of the forward direction, not what the paragraph shows.
- uniform-continuity-compact-sets.md, Core Idea: "a failure of uniform continuity produces a non-convergent sequence with no convergent subsequence, violating Bolzano-Weierstrass." The proof uses Bolzano-Weierstrass to get a convergent subsequence and then reaches a contradiction with continuity at the limit; it does not produce a sequence with no convergent subsequence.
- uniform-continuity-compact-sets.md, Explainer last para: "The Riemann integral's definition requires that continuous functions on [a, b] can be uniformly approximated by step functions." The definition does not require this; it is a consequence used in the integrability proof.
- uniform-convergence-functions.md, Core Idea: "guarantees that limits can be exchanged with derivatives and integrals." False for derivatives: sin(nx)/n → 0 uniformly on ℝ, but the derivatives cos(nx) do not converge to 0 (e.g. at x = 0). Derivative interchange needs uniform convergence of fₙ'. Integral interchange also needs a bounded interval.
- uniform-convergence-preserves-continuity.md, Core Idea: "justifying when lim can be exchanged with continuity and derivatives." Same derivative problem. Explainer last para ("Uniform convergence is the sufficient condition that makes these swaps legal", including d/dx) repeats it.
- uniform-convergence-preserves-continuity.md, Explainer para 2: "This is not a pathological edge case; it is the generic behavior when convergence is non-uniform." False: non-uniform convergence of continuous functions to a continuous limit is common (nx·e^(−nx) on [0, ∞)).
- uniform-convergence.md, Explainer para 2: says xⁿ "on [0, 1)" converges to "the function that is 0 on [0, 1) and 1 at 1"; the point 1 is not in [0, 1). Q1 explanation calls option C "a consequence (discontinuous limits)", but on [0, 1) the limit 0 is continuous.
- uniform-convergence.md, Explainer para 3: "∫ limₙ fₙ = limₙ ∫ fₙ" under uniform convergence is stated without the bounded-interval hypothesis (fails on [0, ∞) for fₙ = 1/n on [0, n]).
- weierstrass-m-test.md, Explainer para 3: after ∑xⁿ/n² converges uniformly on [−1, 1], "you can ... pass differentiation through the sum". Uniform convergence of the series does not justify this; the derivative series ∑xⁿ⁻¹/n diverges at x = 1.
- weierstrass-m-test.md, Q4 explanation: "∑ fₙ(x) = x^n on [0,1] converges pointwise but not uniformly." ∑xⁿ diverges at x = 1, so it does not converge pointwise on [0, 1].
- taylors-theorem-remainder.md, Explainer para 3: "the weight (x−t)^n concentrates near x." (x − t)ⁿ is largest at t = a and vanishes at t = x, so the weight concentrates near a.
- taylors-theorem-remainder.md, Explainer para 4: "This is exactly how computational software evaluates sin x, cos x, and eˣ — by truncating their Taylor series." Doubtful as stated; math libraries typically use argument reduction plus minimax or other tuned polynomial/rational approximations, not plain Taylor truncation.
- supremum-infimum.md, Q5 explanation: "level sets of continuous functions" are given as sets with suprema but no maxima. Level sets of continuous functions on ℝ are closed, so when bounded and nonempty they attain their supremum.
- weierstrass-approximation-theorem.md, Q5 answer: "uniform approximation over all of ℝ fails for any bounded function." False for constant functions; it fails for bounded functions that are not within ε of a constant.
- weierstrass-approximation-theorem.md, Explainer para 4: says polynomials give density "because they separate points and contain constants, and those two properties alone drive the density." Stone–Weierstrass also needs the family to be a subalgebra (closed under multiplication) on a compact space. The same paragraph lists Taylor polynomials and splines as justified by the theorem; Taylor polynomials need differentiability, and splines are piecewise polynomials.
- supremum-and-infimum.md, Core Idea: "For any bounded set S" omits "nonempty"; the empty set is bounded but has no supremum in ℝ.
