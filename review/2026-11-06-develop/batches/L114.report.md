## Flags

### Suspected factual errors in existing text
1. medieval-frankish-kingdoms-merovingian.md, Core Idea: "Their territories eventually encompassed much of present-day France, Germany, and northern Italy." The Lombards held northern Italy during the Merovingian period. Frankish rule there began with Charlemagne's conquest in 774.
2. medieval-gender-family-kinship.md, Q0 and Explainer: coverture is applied to "12th-century France." Coverture is an English common-law doctrine. French customary law varied, and the claim that wives "could not hold property" is overstated for many regions.
3. medieval-guild-system-crafts.md, Core Idea: "membership was restricted and hereditary." This is overstated. The Explainer itself says mastership became *increasingly* hereditary late in the period.
4. medieval-guild-system-crafts.md, Explainer and Q3: "the labor union movement that replaced them retained many of the guild's core functions." Most historians reject direct continuity. Craft guilds were associations of masters (employers), not workers. The quality-signal thesis in Q0 and Q1 is also presented as settled, but it is contested in the literature.
5. medieval-heresy-albigensian-waldensian.md, Q2 and Explainer: the Albigensian Crusade is called "the first crusade launched against Christian communities within Europe." This is debatable, because Innocent III had already called a crusade against Markward of Anweiler in 1199. The claim that Waldensians "accepted orthodox Catholic doctrine" also fits only the early movement, since later Waldensians rejected purgatory and oaths.
6. medieval-holy-roman-empire-formation.md, Explainer: "In 962 ... the Holy Roman Empire was born." The name "Holy Roman Empire" is later (12th–13th century). The Core Idea's "Italian city-states" is anachronistic for the 10th century.
7. medieval-inquisition-religious-enforcement.md, Core Idea: "established in the 12th century." Papal inquisitors date from the 1230s, under Gregory IX. Explainer and Q0: "Only the relapsed ... were relaxed to the secular arm." Impenitent, obstinate heretics who refused to recant were also handed over for execution.
8. medieval-ireland-medieval-dynasties.md, Explainer and Q1: "the Pale" is described as a feature of the early conquest. The term dates from the late 15th century. Around 1250–1300, Anglo-Norman lordship covered much of the island, including large parts of Munster. The phrase "Hibernis ipsis Hiberniores" is attributed to "contemporaries," but it comes from later writers.
9. medieval-jewish-diaspora.md, Core Idea: "moneylending, crafts, and trade (occupations forbidden to Christians)." Crafts and trade were not forbidden to Christians. Only lending at interest was prohibited.
10. medieval-jewish-expulsion-conversion.md, Explainer: "Suleiman reportedly welcomed them." The Ottoman sultan in 1492 was Bayezid II, and the anecdote is attributed to him. Suleiman reigned 1520–66. Also, "Judenhut" is a hat, not a badge, and Lateran IV required distinctive dress without specifying a badge. "Ghettos" (Core Idea) is anachronistic before 1516.
11. medieval-kingship-divine-authority.md, Q0 and Explainer: "Holy Roman Emperor Henry IV" in 1076. Henry was crowned emperor only in 1084, so in 1076 he was king. "Sign Magna Carta": John sealed it. The phrase "divine right" is an early modern concept.
12. medieval-law-courts-justice.md, Q3: ordeal is called "entirely legitimate," with no contemporary doubt. Some medieval churchmen criticized ordeals (e.g., Agobard of Lyon; the Paris theologians around Peter the Chanter).
13. medieval-medicine-humoral-theory.md, Explainer and Q1: "Galen's texts were treated as authoritative scripture." This is overstated. Medieval physicians, such as al-Razi in his Doubts about Galen, openly criticized Galen.
14. medieval-monastic-intellectual-centers.md, Explainer: Alcuin is called "a single learned monk." He was a deacon and was probably not a professed monk. Bede is said to have written "entirely from sources he assembled in his monastery's library." He also used informants and documents copied for him in Rome. "Secular universities" is misleading, because medieval universities were clerical institutions.
15. medieval-monasticism-communities.md, Q2: the answer is "false" for "economic power and political influence were unintended consequences." The explanation conflates self-sufficiency (a design goal) with wealth and political power. Many historians would treat the statement as broadly true, so the item is ambiguous. Q3: the recovery of Aristotle came largely through Arabic and Greek translations, not through Western monastic copying.
16. medieval-mysticism-spiritual-experience.md, Q1: distractor A ("Women were considered more prone to demonic deception") states a real medieval view (discernment-of-spirits literature, e.g., Gerson). This makes the item ambiguous.
17. medieval-normans-conquest-1066.md: "Harold Godwinson seized the English throne." Harold was accepted by the English magnates and crowned. Q2 says William "distributed land in scattered parcels" as deliberate policy, which is debated. The Explainer stresses Norman-introduced administration, but late Anglo-Saxon England already had shires, sheriffs, writs and the geld.
18. medieval-pilgrimage-routes-networks.md, Q2 and Explainer: the Romanesque style is said to have followed pilgrimage corridors "closely." This "pilgrimage church" thesis is debated. The participant figure ("hundreds of thousands ... over the medieval period") is unknowable. In 1098 the Fatimids, not the Seljuks, held Jerusalem.
19. medieval-portuguese-kingdom-formation.md, Explainer: "establishing a school of navigation at Sagres" is generally considered a myth. "Castile and Aragon continued fighting southward for another two centuries" does not fit Aragon, whose peninsular reconquest ended by the 1240s–60s. The grant of Portucale is usually dated 1095–96, not 1093.
20. medieval-reliquaries-relic-cults.md, Q1 and Explainer: body-part reliquaries are said to hold the matching body part. Art historians note that the shape often did not match the contents.
21. medieval-sacramental-theology-eucharist.md, Explainer and Q3: Luther's view is labelled "real presence through faith." Luther held a real presence that did not depend on the recipient's faith, and "through faith" is closer to Calvin.
22. medieval-scholastic-method-logic.md, Core Idea: "thesis, antithesis, and synthesis" is a later (Fichte/Hegel-era) scheme, not the scholastic quaestio. Explainer: "present objections to the answer you will defend (sed contra arguments)." The sed contra is the counter-authority, and the objections open with "videtur quod." Q4 correctly identifies the sed contra as the counter-position.
23. medieval-scottish-independence.md, Q1 and Q2 and Explainer: the Declaration of Arbroath is read as "popular sovereignty." That reading is anachronistic: it was a barons' letter to the pope, and "community of the realm" meant the political elite. In Q1, option A (papal recognition of Bruce) was the letter's actual immediate purpose, which makes the item ambiguous. In Q0, Edward I's claim also rested on historical arguments, not only on Scottish kings holding English land.
24. medieval-serfdom-manorial-lords.md, Explainer: the "great majority" of medieval people are treated as serfs. Many peasants were legally free. "Bargaining power for the first time" is overstated.
25. medieval-urbanization-growth.md, Q0 and Explainer: "Stadtluft macht frei" is a 19th-century formulation. The year-and-a-day rule existed in some charters but was not universal. Q3 presents the push vs. pull debate as settled.
26. medieval-viking-raids-settlement.md, Explainer: "Danelaw (formalized 878 under the Treaty of Wedmore)." 878 was Guthrum's baptism at Wedmore. The boundary was set in the treaty of Alfred and Guthrum (c. 886). The 845 attack on Paris is usually called a raid or sack, while the famous siege was 885–86.
27. medieval-wales-medieval-kingdoms.md, Explainer: "concentric castles (Caernarfon, Conwy, Harlech)." Of these, only Harlech is concentric (Beaumaris is the other classic example). Caernarfon and Conwy are not.
28. medieval-world-definition-periodization.md, Explainer and Q2: the House of Wisdom is called "the intellectual center of the known world." Recent scholarship treats its scale and role as partly mythologized.
