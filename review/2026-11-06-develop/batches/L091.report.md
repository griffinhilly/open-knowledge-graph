## Flags

### Suspected factual errors in existing text
- comparative-global-history.md (Explainer and Q2): "Barrington Moore ... compared how England, France, Germany, Japan, and India". *Social Origins of Dictatorship and Democracy* has England, France, the United States, China, Japan and India as its main cases. Germany and Russia appear only as comparisons in passing. The list leaves out the US and China.
- comparative-global-history.md (Q4 explanation): a global history of the French and Russian Revolutions "would ask how Atlantic abolitionist ideas, commodity markets, and Enlightenment print culture linked them". The 1789 and 1917 revolutions are more than a century apart, so this example of linkage is anachronistic and confused.
- comparative-global-history.md (Explainer): "the rise of African slavery" as part of the Columbian Exchange. Slavery in Africa came well before 1492. What expanded was the Atlantic slave trade.
- enlightenment-historiography.md (Q2 explanation): "Vico did not restore providence; his cycles are human developmental patterns, not divine plans." Vico's *New Science* explicitly gives divine providence a central role. He describes it as a "rational civil theology of divine providence". The cycles are human-made, but providence works through them. Separately, the Core Idea treats Vico as a straightforward Enlightenment figure, which is contested; Berlin, for example, reads him as a critic of the Enlightenment.
- deep-history-approach.md (Q5 answer): "these institutions occupied most of human existence before them". This is garbled and says the reverse of the intended point. The intended point is that these institutions were absent for most of human existence.
- deep-history-approach.md (Q1 explanation): "It cannot tell us about the invention of agriculture (that's archaeological)". Ancient DNA has in fact been central to showing that farming spread into Europe largely through migrating farmers from Anatolia. So it bears directly on option A.
- deep-history-approach.md (Q4 explanation): "cognitively modern for at least 100,000–200,000 years". The timing of behavioral and cognitive modernity is contested, and the figure is stated too firmly.
- annales-school.md (Q3 explanation): "the political narrative tradition ... under historians like Ranke and Michelet". Michelet is a poor example of political-narrative history. Febvre and the Annales founders admired him as a forerunner of total history.
- entangled-histories-approach.md (Explainer, Q1 and Q3 explanations): "Egypt shaped Europe as much as Europe shaped Egypt" and "India affected Britain as much as Britain affected India". These "as much as" claims are unsupported, and they sit uneasily with the file's own point about asymmetry. Also, *histoire croisée* (Werner and Zimmermann) is a specific approach distinct from "entangled history". The file treats the two terms as the same.
- connected-histories-entanglement.md (Explainer, Q2): tea is listed among goods "developed in India" that remade Britain. For most of the period, Britain's tea came from China. Indian (Assam) tea arrived only from the mid-19th century.
- genealogical-method.md (Explainer): family reconstitution is "a technique pioneered by the Cambridge Group". The technique was pioneered by Louis Henry and Michel Fleury in France in the 1950s. The Cambridge Group later adapted it for England.
- genealogical-method.md (Core Idea): Foucauldian genealogy traces "origins of concepts and institutions back to their problematic foundations". This conflicts with the file's own Q3 and Explainer, which say genealogy rejects the search for origins. More broadly, the file joins two unrelated senses of "genealogy". The parallel claimed in Q4 (true-false, answer true) is a forced analogy.
- foucault-genealogy.md (Explainer, Q1): "for most of human history, sodomy was a prohibited act, not an identity". This overgeneralizes Foucault's claim about modern Western legal and medical categories to all of human history, and historians dispute even the narrower version.
- contextualism-historical.md (Q5 explanation): "perlocutionary effect (the intended impact)". In Austin, the perlocutionary effect is the effect actually produced on the hearer, not the intended impact.
- carlo-ginzburg-microhistory.md (Q2, Q5): the "evidential paradigm" is presented as Ginzburg's method in *The Cheese and the Worms* (1976). He named and theorized it in his later essay "Clues" (1979). Applying the term to the book is retrospective. This is minor.
- environmental-history-theory.md (Q2, Explainer): Cronon's *Changes in the Land* is summarized mainly as fire suppression producing dense forest. His central ecological story is clearing, fencing, livestock and soil depletion. Please check whether the emphasis is right.
- gender-history.md (Q1): option A ("Women were actually present in most historical armies") is marked wrong. But 18th-century European armies did include many women as camp followers, sutlers and laundresses. The premise that armies were "entirely male" is also inaccurate.
- gender-history-theory.md (Q2 explanation): the explanation says Scott was "explicitly arguing against" women's history as a recovery lens. She argued for going beyond it, not against it.
- explanation-in-history.md (Q2 explanation): Weber is called "the canonical example of idealist explanation". Weber explicitly disclaimed replacing a one-sided materialist account with a one-sided spiritualist one. This is a common simplification.
- braudel-longue-duree.md (Q1): the 1588 Armada's failure is explained through "Atlantic weather patterns" as longue durée. The specific 1588 storms were event-level weather. Only the general climate and geography belong to the longue durée.
