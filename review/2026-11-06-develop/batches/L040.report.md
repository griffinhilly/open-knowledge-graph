1. distribution-theory-pdes.md, Explainer: "the Fourier transform of the fundamental solution of the Laplacian in 3D, which grows at infinity". The 3D fundamental solution 1/(4π|x|) decays at infinity. The 2D logarithm is the one that grows.
2. fourier-transform-methods-pdes.md, Explainer: "For Schrödinger's equation iu_t = -Δu, the transform gives û_t = i|ξ|²û with solution û = f̂·e^(i|ξ|²t)". Transforming gives iû_t = |ξ|²û, so û_t = −i|ξ|²û and û = f̂·e^(−i|ξ|²t). The sign is wrong.
3. greens-functions-pdes.md, Question 0: the option marked correct is "G(x,y) = -1/(4π|x-y|)", but the explanation says the Green's function for −Δ is +1/(4π|x−y|). The two conflict. The negative sign is right only for Δ, not −Δ.
4. hamilton-jacobi-equations.md, Explainer: "For the equation u_t + ½|∇u|² = 0 with initial data u₀(x) = -|x|, the characteristics emanating from the origin fan out while those from far away converge, and the solution develops a corner ... in finite time." For u₀ = −|x| the characteristics converge right away, and the corner is already present at t = 0. Hopf-Lax gives u = −|x| − t/2. A fan comes from u₀ = +|x|, which is smoothed at once. The example does not show a corner forming in finite time from smooth data.
5. viscosity-solutions.md, Explainer: "If F is degenerate elliptic (non-decreasing in D²u), then F(x₀, u, Dφ, D²φ) ≤ F(x₀, u, Du, D²u) = 0." With D²u ≤ D²φ, a non-decreasing F gives the opposite inequality. The convention that matches the "≤ 0 for subsolutions" definition is F non-increasing in D²u. Also, "convergent numerical schemes automatically produce viscosity solutions" overstates the result. The Barles-Souganidis theorem needs monotone, stable and consistent schemes.
6. variational-methods-pdes.md, Question 2 explanation: "the Poisson equation is the gradient-flow equation for the Dirichlet energy". Poisson's equation is the Euler-Lagrange (critical point) equation. The gradient flow is the heat equation u_t = Δu + f. Also, the Explainer's mountain pass statement leaves out the Palais-Smale compactness condition, and without it the conclusion can fail.
7. maximum-principles-advanced.md, Question 0: "if u is a supersolution with u(x₀) = max u at a boundary point". The Hopf lemma at a maximum applies to subsolutions. The Explainer's version (Lu ≤ 0 with L = −a^{ij}∂ij) is the subsolution case, so the question's wording does not match it.
8. maximum-principles-pdes.md, Explainer: "The strong version is considerably more useful: it gives uniqueness of the Dirichlet problem ... continuous dependence ... and comparison principles". The weak maximum principle already gives all three. The sentence credits the strong version with things it does not need.
9. reaction-diffusion-equations.md, Explainer: "Turing patterns appear in ... chemical reactions (the Belousov-Zhabotinsky reaction)". BZ is the standard example of an oscillating, wave-forming reaction, not of stationary Turing patterns. The first clear experimental Turing patterns came from a different (CIMA) reaction. Also, the Question 1 explanation "The faster-diffusing inhibitor cannot keep up with the slower-diffusing activator" gets the mechanism wrong. The usual account is local activation with long-range inhibition: the inhibitor spreads farther and suppresses activation nearby.
10. schauder-estimates.md, Question 1 explanation: it refers merely measurable coefficients for the non-divergence equation −a^{ij}u_{ij} = f to De Giorgi-Nash-Moser. That theory covers divergence-form equations. The non-divergence counterpart is Krylov-Safonov.

Sufficiency needs strengthened conditions such as strict Legendre plus no conjugate points.

The spatial operator (the Laplacian) is elliptic in all three cases. The types belong to the evolution equations, not to the spatial operator.
