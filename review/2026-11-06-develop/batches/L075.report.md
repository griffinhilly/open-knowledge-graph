## Flags
- domains/earth-and-space-sciences/geophysics/seismic-data-processing-and-filtering.md, Explainer: "After stacking, additional steps address remaining artifacts. **Multiple suppression** ..." Multiple suppression (Radon, predictive deconvolution) is mostly applied before stack; the sequence as written is misleading.
- domains/earth-and-space-sciences/geophysics/seismic-data-processing-and-filtering.md, question 3 explanation: "over-correct (flattening too much, causing NMO stretch artifacts)". Too low a velocity overcorrects so events curve upward; NMO stretch is a separate effect of the correction at far offsets and shallow times, not a symptom of overcorrection.
- domains/earth-and-space-sciences/geophysics/seismic-data-processing-and-filtering.md, Explainer: "common midpoint (CMP) gather — all traces that share the same reflection point". Traces share a midpoint; they share a reflection point only for flat reflectors.
- domains/earth-and-space-sciences/geophysics/seismic-migration-techniques.md, Core Idea: "Time migration assumes constant velocity". Time migration allows velocity to vary with time/depth (the file's own Explainer and questions say so); it assumes no strong lateral variation.
- domains/earth-and-space-sciences/geophysics/seismic-moment-and-magnitude.md, Core Idea and Explainer: "Mw = (2/3)log₁₀(M₀) − 10.7". The −10.7 constant applies to M₀ in dyne·cm; the Explainer gives M₀ in N·m, where the constant is about −6.07. With N·m, Tōhoku's 5×10²² gives Mw ≈ 4.4, not ≈ 9.
- domains/earth-and-space-sciences/geophysics/seismic-moment-and-magnitude.md, Explainer: "a barely-felt magnitude-2 event might have M₀ ≈ 10⁹ N·m ... a factor of 10¹³ larger". Mw 2 corresponds to about 10¹² N·m (10⁹ N·m is about Mw 0); the ratio to Tōhoku is then about 10¹⁰–10¹¹.
- domains/earth-and-space-sciences/geophysics/seismic-moment-and-magnitude.md, Core Idea: "Seismic moment ... quantifies the total energy released". Moment is not energy; radiated energy scales with moment times stress drop over rigidity. Question 1 (0-based) answer "They released equal energy" is therefore wrong: equal M₀ with smaller area and larger slip implies higher stress drop and generally more radiated energy. Suggest rewording the question to ask about moment or magnitude.
- domains/earth-and-space-sciences/geophysics/seismic-network-design-and-station-deployment.md, Explainer: "If all your stations are north of the earthquake (a 180° gap to the south), the east-west and depth positions will be poorly constrained." With stations only to the north, the north-south position is the poorly constrained one (question 0's explanation says so).
- domains/earth-and-space-sciences/geophysics/seismic-ray-theory.md, question 1 explanation: "A low-velocity zone bends rays toward steeper angles (they speed up entering the LVZ...)". Rays slow down entering a low-velocity zone; that is why they bend toward the vertical.
- domains/earth-and-space-sciences/geophysics/seismic-ray-theory.md, Explainer: "A sharp velocity increase (like the Moho ...) produces a discontinuity in the curve". The first-arrival curve stays continuous but breaks in slope, and the full curve shows a triplication; "discontinuity" is misleading.
- subduction-zone-seismic-structure -> seismic-migration-techniques: listed as a soft prerequisite, but migration does not depend on subduction-zone structure; it looks misplaced.
- domains/earth-and-space-sciences/geophysics/seismic-ray-tracing-methods.md, Core Idea: "Rays bend toward the vertical in high-velocity zones and away from the vertical in low-velocity zones." Reversed: entering faster rock, a ray bends away from the vertical (the file's own Explainer and questions say so).
- domains/earth-and-space-sciences/geophysics/seismic-ray-tracing-methods.md, question 4 explanation: "the curved paths are head-wave-like refraction phenomena". Diving (turning) waves in a gradient are not head waves; the comparison is misleading.
- domains/earth-and-space-sciences/geophysics/seismic-reflection-surveys.md, Core Idea and Explainer: CMP processing "groups traces by reflection point". CMP sorting groups by common midpoint; this equals a common reflection point only for flat reflectors.
- domains/earth-and-space-sciences/geophysics/seismic-surface-waves-rayleigh-love.md, Core Idea: surface waves "propagate with minimal attenuation". Their advantage at distance comes from weaker geometric spreading (1/√r vs 1/r, as the file's question 3 says); they travel in the low-Q shallow Earth and are not minimally attenuated.
- domains/earth-and-space-sciences/geophysics/seismic-tomography-velocity-imaging.md: the file contains two `## Questions` YAML blocks; they should be merged.
- domains/earth-and-space-sciences/geophysics/seismic-tomography-velocity-imaging.md, Core Idea: "Ray theory approximates high-frequency seismic wave propagation as straight rays". Rays in ray theory are generally curved; straight rays are a further simplification used in some local studies.
- domains/earth-and-space-sciences/geophysics/seismic-tomography-velocity-imaging.md, Explainer: "a medical CT scan builds an image of the body from X-ray travel times". CT inverts X-ray attenuation, as the file's own true-false question says.
- domains/earth-and-space-sciences/geophysics/seismic-tomography-velocity-imaging.md, question 3 (first block) explanation: "Seismic wave velocity increases with density". At fixed moduli, higher density lowers velocity (v = √(modulus/density)); velocity often correlates with density in rocks because moduli rise faster.
- electrical-resistivity-tomography -> seismic-tomography-velocity-imaging: listed as a soft prerequisite; it is a different method and not needed, though harmless as soft.
- domains/earth-and-space-sciences/geophysics/seismic-velocity-density-relationships.md, Explainer: "If you measure Vp = 6.5 km/s, it could be gabbro, granulite, or eclogite"; questions 1 and 2 say the same for 7.0 km/s. Eclogite Vp is about 8 km/s or more, so it does not fit either value.
- domains/earth-and-space-sciences/geophysics/seismic-velocity-density-relationships.md, question 0 explanation: "partial melt reduces both moduli and effective density — both lower velocity". Lower density on its own raises velocity; melt lowers velocity through the moduli.
- domains/earth-and-space-sciences/geophysics/seismic-velocity-depth-models.md, Explainer: "transition zone (410–660 km), where olivine undergoes pressure-induced phase transitions to denser crystal structures (wadsleyite, then ringwoodite), producing sharp velocity jumps at those depths". Olivine → wadsleyite is at 410 km; wadsleyite → ringwoodite is near 520 km; the 660 km jump is ringwoodite → bridgmanite + ferropericlase (as the subduction file states).
- domains/earth-and-space-sciences/geophysics/seismic-velocity-depth-models.md, question 1: the low-velocity zone is stated as "caused by" partial melting. The cause is debated (temperature near the solidus, water, small melt fractions); "partial melting" as the sole cause is overstated.
- domains/earth-and-space-sciences/geophysics/stress-tensor-inversion-from-earthquakes.md, Core Idea: "Methods like the Michael method assume earthquakes occur on planes optimally oriented for slip". These methods assume uniform stress and slip parallel to resolved shear traction (Wallace-Bott); they do not assume optimal orientation, and they need a range of fault orientations (as the file's questions say).
- domains/earth-and-space-sciences/geophysics/subduction-zone-seismic-structure.md, Explainer: "Some slabs (like Tonga) dive steeply and penetrate deep into the lower mantle." Tonga is often imaged as deflected or partly flattened in the transition zone; worth checking.
- domains/earth-and-space-sciences/geophysics/subduction-zone-seismic-structure.md, question 1 and Explainer attribute the upper DSZ plane to dehydration and the lower plane to unbending. The unbending model accounts for both planes (upper compression, lower extension); the split as written oversimplifies.
- domains/earth-and-space-sciences/geophysics/subduction-zone-structure-and-dynamics.md, Explainer: slabs as the downwelling limb "complementing the hot upwelling limbs at mid-ocean ridges". Ridges are mostly passive, shallow upwellings, as the file's own question 2 explanation says.
- domains/earth-and-space-sciences/geophysics/subduction-zone-thermal-structure.md, question 1 explanation: "The 100–120 km arc-trench gap". The 100–120 km figure is the slab depth beneath the arc, not the horizontal arc-trench gap.
- domains/earth-and-space-sciences/geophysics/thermal-conductivity-and-rocks.md, Core Idea: "fluid-filled pores greatly reduce effective conductivity compared to dry rock". Water-filled pores conduct better than air-filled (dry) pores; the reduction is relative to non-porous rock, as the file's questions say.
- domains/earth-and-space-sciences/geophysics/thermal-time-constant.md, Explainer: "A 1-meter-thick lava flow cools in about a day." With τ = d²/κ and κ = 10⁻⁶ m²/s, 1 m gives 10⁶ s ≈ 12 days.
- domains/earth-and-space-sciences/geophysics/thermal-time-constant.md, question 0 and Explainer: 100 km gives "roughly 100 million years". d²/κ = 10¹⁶ s ≈ 320 Myr; the ~100 Myr figure needs a different prefactor.
- domains/earth-and-space-sciences/geophysics/thermal-time-constant.md, question 3 explanation: "The 100 km lithosphere is 2 × 10⁷× thicker than a 5-meter flow ... (2 × 10⁷)² = 4 × 10¹⁴". 100 km / 5 m = 2 × 10⁴, so the ratio is 4 × 10⁸. Also a 5 m flow has τ ≈ 2.5 × 10⁷ s ≈ 290 days, not "days".
- domains/earth-and-space-sciences/geophysics/thermal-time-constant.md, Explainer: "A volcanic intrusion (a dike or sill) a few meters thick heats its surrounding rock for days to weeks". d²/κ for 3 m is about 100 days; "weeks to months" fits the file's own formula better.
- domains/earth-and-space-sciences/geophysics/thermobarometry-cooling-history.md, question 1 and Explainer: counterclockwise P-T paths described as "characteristic of contact metamorphism near igneous intrusions". Contact metamorphism is mostly near-isobaric heating; counterclockwise paths are usually tied to magmatic arcs or extension with heating before burial.
- thermochronology-and-cooling-ages -> thermobarometry-cooling-history: listed as a hard prerequisite, but thermobarometry depends on mineral equilibria and metamorphic petrology, not on thermochronology; the two are complementary. A metamorphic-facies or phase-equilibria topic would be the natural hard prerequisite.
- domains/earth-and-space-sciences/geophysics/thermochronology-and-cooling-ages.md, question 1 (answer and explanation): the ages 55 Ma at 300 °C and 5 Ma at 75 °C are said to show stagnation followed by "rapid exhumation" at 5 Ma. Two points only give an average of about 4.5 °C/Myr; they cannot show a late acceleration without a lower-temperature or present-day constraint.
- domains/earth-and-space-sciences/geophysics/thermochronology-and-cooling-ages.md, Explainer: "daughter isotopes (or other damage products like fission tracks and helium atoms) diffuse out". Fission tracks anneal (shorten and vanish); they do not diffuse out.
- domains/earth-and-space-sciences/geophysics/thermochronology-and-cooling-ages.md, Explainer: Himalayan exhumation "accelerated dramatically around 10–15 Ma, linked to intensified monsoon erosion". The timing and the monsoon link are debated; stated as settled fact.
