## Flags

### Suspected factual errors in existing text
- enlightenment-natural-rights-theory.md (Explainer, Q2 explanation): "Locke wrote his *Two Treatises* ... meant to justify Parliament's deposition of James II." Most scholars date composition to about 1679–83 (Exclusion Crisis); it was published in 1689 and presented as a defense of 1688. Same claim in john-locke-liberal-political-thought.md: "Locke was writing in the immediate aftermath of the Glorious Revolution" (Explainer, Q2 explanation).
- enlightenment-natural-rights-theory.md (Explainer): the Declarations "echo Locke almost verbatim". This overstates it; "pursuit of happiness" is not Locke's wording.
- english-enclosure-movement.md (Core Idea): "c. 1500-1800" conflicts with the Explainer's 15th-century start and its parliamentary wave of "1750–1850". Also, "dramatically increasing productivity" and "agricultural output per acre increased substantially" are contested in the literature (some economic historians find modest yield gains from enclosure).
- galilean-observation-experimentation.md (Core Idea): "telescopic observations of ... Saturn's rings". Galileo saw Saturn's odd shape but did not identify rings; Huygens did that in 1655. Explainer: *Sidereus Nuncius* "addressed to the educated public, not just academic philosophers". It was in Latin and dedicated to Cosimo II; the Italian-language *Dialogue* (1632) is the public-facing work.
- galileo-telescope-observations.md (Explainer): "Copernicus had been a priest". He was a cathedral canon, and whether he was ordained a priest is uncertain.
- humanist-education-studia-humanitatis.md (Core Idea): lists "grammar, rhetoric, dialectic, poetry, and history". The standard list is grammar, rhetoric, poetry, history and moral philosophy (as the Explainer says). Dialectic belongs to the trivium, not the studia humanitatis.
- jesuit-missionary-education.md (Explainer): "In Japan, Matteo Ricci in China, and José de Acosta in the Americas" is garbled, with no figure named for Japan. "The Jesuits essentially invented the multinational religious institution" overstates it, since the mendicant orders were already international. Q5 explanation: colleges "built in cities where Protestant competition was strongest, not where Catholic populations were most secure" is an unsupported absolute.
- linear-perspective-mathematics-art.md (Explainer): the mirror experiment is misdescribed. In the accepted account, the viewer looked through the hole from the back of the panel at a mirror held in front, which reflected the painted side. Removing the mirror let them compare it with the real Baptistery. The text says "When held up against a mirror, the painting appeared continuous with the actual building behind it."
- locke-epistemology-sensation-ideas.md (Q2): the key says cross-cultural diversity was Locke's "primary evidence". But Locke's best-known argument against innate speculative principles is that "children and idiots" lack them, which is option B. So the item has two defensible answers. The Explainer's claim that the political philosophy "follows directly" from the epistemology is also contested: the works were written largely independently, and the *Treatises* rely on natural law knowable by reason.
- lutheran-theology-sola-fide.md (Core Idea, Explainer, Q1 explanation): these imply Luther made sacraments unnecessary ("confession, penance, and the Eucharist are not necessary for salvation"; "the priest's mediating role collapsed"). Luther kept baptism and the Lord's Supper as means of grace and kept private confession, and Lutheran churches kept ordained clergy.
- machiavelli-political-realism.md (Explainer): "*The Prince* opens with a categorical claim: I will write about things as they actually are". That passage is in chapter 15, not the opening. "*il principe virtuoso*" looks like an invented term.
- mechanical-philosophy-causation.md (Core Idea): says the view "eliminated reference to ... divine intention". This overstates it for Boyle and Newton, who kept divine design central; Boyle defended final causes in natural theology. Explainer: "An acorn falls ... partly because it is heavy (material cause) ... natural tendency ... (final cause)" muddles Aristotle's causes.
- mercantilism-national-wealth.md (Explainer): "Smith coined the term *mercantile system*". The term (système mercantile) was used earlier by the elder Mirabeau and the Physiocrats; Smith popularized it in English. Also, "plantation system ... was a designed solution" and slavery and mercantilism "declined together" (Q5 explanation) are overstated causal claims.
- middle-passage-atlantic-forced-migration.md (Explainer, Q1 explanation): the "tight-packing debate ... resolved in favor of quantity, producing mortality rates" presents a contested link as settled. Quantitative studies have found weak correlation between crowding and mortality.
- military-revolution-gunpowder-warfare.md (Explainer): "advantages over non-European polities that lacked artillery and trace italienne fortifications" ignores the strong artillery of the Ottoman, Mughal and Ming states. The conquest of the Aztec and Inca empires also owed much to disease and Indigenous allies.
- montesquieu-separation-governmental-powers.md (Explainer): "The legislative could impeach the executive" misstates Montesquieu, who held that the legislature should not judge the executive's person (only its ministers). He also called the judicial power "in some fashion null", so the even three-branch picture is anachronistic.
- neoplatonism-renaissance-philosophy.md (Q2, Explainer): this ties Cosimo's funding of Ficino to the 1453 fall of Byzantium. Cosimo's interest is usually traced to the Council of Florence (1438–39) and Gemistos Plethon, and Greek teaching in Florence dates from Chrysoloras in 1397. "Pico ... His student" is inaccurate: Pico was Ficino's associate. The existence of a formal "Platonic Academy" is debated. Botticelli's paintings as "Neoplatonic allegories" is one contested interpretation, stated as fact.
- newtonian-synthesis-physics.md (Q1): the distractor "Newton corrected Kepler's laws, showing they were only rough approximations" is partly true, because Newton showed they hold only approximately. The Core Idea and Explainer attribute "deterministic mechanism" to Newton, but he held that God might need to correct planetary irregularities.
- peace-of-augsburg.md (Explainer, Q4): the Ecclesiastical Reservation is misdescribed as "Church lands converting to Lutheranism after 1552 must return". The Reservation held that an ecclesiastical prince who converted lost his office and lands. The 1552 (Passau) date is a separate normative date for already secularized property. "Never enforced" is overstated, since it was contested in the Cologne War of 1583.
- religious-toleration-theory-practice.md (Explainer, Q1 explanation, Q3 explanation): says Huguenots received "their own law courts" or "separate law courts". The Edict created bipartisan chambers (chambres de l'Édit / mi-parties) within the parlements, not separate Huguenot courts. Q3's wording ("chambers in parlements") is correct.
- renaissance-artistic-patronage.md (Explainer, Q4): "No Renaissance masterwork appeared because an artist felt like making it" is an absolute with counterexamples, such as Michelangelo's late, uncommissioned Pietàs. The genius ideal being purely a "Romantic invention" ignores Vasari's (1550) treatment of Michelangelo as "divine". Q5 explanation calls the period a "pre-print ... world", but printing was widespread after the 1450s.
- renaissance-banking-finance-medici.md (Core Idea): "The Medici family developed ... double-entry bookkeeping" contradicts the file's own Q4 and Explainer. Explainer, Q1: "letter of credit (lettera di cambio)". The lettera di cambio is a bill of exchange, an instrument that also carried exchange-rate profit. Explainer: Pacioli as "a Florentine connection": he was from Sansepolcro and published the *Summa* in Venice. The Explainer also omits the bank's decline under Lorenzo.
- renaissance-classical-literature-revival.md (Explainer): "most of Aristotle's works ... was preserved not in Western libraries". Most of Aristotle was available in Latin by the 13th century. Also, Petrarch and Boccaccio (died 1374/1375) "now ... found scholars arriving" after the 15th-century flight from Constantinople, which is a chronology error.
