## Flags

### Suspected factual errors in existing text
1. baire-category-metric-spaces.md, Explainer: "nowhere dense if its closure contains no open ball — equivalently, every open ball intersects the complement of S." The second clause describes empty interior of S, not of its closure (ℚ satisfies it but is dense). The correct equivalent: every open ball contains a smaller open ball disjoint from S.
2. baire-category-metric-spaces.md, question 4 explanation: "The theorem is false for incomplete spaces, so the condition is necessary." Some incomplete spaces are Baire (e.g. (0, 1) with the usual metric). Completeness is sufficient, not necessary.
3. baire-category-theorem.md, question 2 (true-false, answer true): "at least one Aₙ must contain an open ball." Wrong as stated: the closure of some Aₙ must contain an open ball. Counterexample: ℝ = ℚ ∪ (ℝ \ ℚ), and neither part contains an interval. The explanation itself switches to closures. Answer should be false, or the question should say "the closure of at least one Aₙ".
4. basis-for-topology.md, question 2 explanation: "{(p, q) : p, q ∈ ℚ} is countable (a countable product of countable sets)." ℚ × ℚ is a finite product; countable products of countable sets are generally uncountable.
6. basis-for-topology.md, Explainer: compares a topological basis to a vector-space basis "with minimal redundancy … spanning without collapse." Topological bases are not minimal; the analogy misleads.
7. boundary-set-topology.md, Explainer: "boundary points are limit points of A." Not always: in ℝ, 0 is a boundary point of {0} but not a limit point of it.
8. classification-compact-surfaces.md, Explainer: χ "packages genus and orientability into a single number." χ does not determine orientability (torus and Klein bottle both have χ = 0); the file's own questions say this.
9. closed-sets-definition-examples.md, Explainer: "the limit-point characterization is a consequence that holds only in sufficiently nice spaces" and "beyond metric spaces, you lose the intuition of 'a closed set contains its boundary points'." In every topological space, a set is closed iff it contains all its limit points iff it contains its boundary. Only the sequence characterization needs nice spaces (e.g. first countability).
10. closed-sets-definition-examples.md, question 4 answer: calls defining closed sets via limit points "circular." Limit points are defined from open neighborhoods, so it is not circular; it is an equivalent characterization.
11. closure-interior-and-boundary.md, question 4 explanation: "most topology texts define closure axiomatically and derive interior from it." Most texts start from open sets.
12. closure-interior-boundary.md, question 2 text: "open if and mainly if" (garbled "only").
13. closure-interior-boundary.md, Explainer: "∂A is always a closed set (as the difference of two closed sets)." Conclusion true, reason wrong: ∂A = Ā ∩ (X \ A°), an intersection of two closed sets. The sentence about the three-piece partition is also garbled.
14. closure-operator-topology.md, Explainer: "x is a limit point of A if every open set containing x intersects A." That defines an adherent (closure) point. A limit point needs a point of A other than x.
15. compact-metric-spaces.md, Explainer and question 3: "totally bounded means the space fits inside a large enough ball" and "(0, 1) is totally bounded (it fits in a ball of radius 1)." This confuses bounded with totally bounded. An infinite discrete metric space fits in a ball but is not totally bounded.
16. compact-sets-definition.md, question 2 text: "if and mainly if" (garbled).
17. compact-spaces-open-covers.md, Explainer: "in a compact space, every sequence has a convergent subsequence." False in general topological spaces (e.g. [0,1]^[0,1]). It holds for compact metric spaces.
18. compactness-hausdorff-spaces.md, question 3 explanation: in the topology ∅, {1}, {0,1}, {1,2}, {0,1,2}, "the set {0} is compact … but is not closed." Its complement {1,2} is open, so {0} is closed. {1} would work (complement {0,2} is not open).
19. compactness-hausdorff-spaces.md, Core Idea: puts "finite products of compact spaces are compact" under the Hausdorff heading. This holds without Hausdorff.
20. connected-spaces.md, question 3 text: "if and mainly if nearly every two points" (garbled "only if every").
21. connected-spaces.md, question 4 answer: "disjoint open sets have disjoint closures." False ((0,1) and (1,2)). Also, "between any two open intervals, there is always a point in neither" is false for overlapping intervals. The proof sketch needs rewriting.
22. continuity-topological-definition.md, continuity-topological-spaces.md, continuous-functions-topology.md (questions and Explainers): open maps are called "a strictly stronger condition than continuity." Open maps need not be continuous; the two are independent. Example: the identity from standard ℝ to discrete ℝ is open but not continuous. I did not add a misconception on this to avoid contradicting the Explainers.
23. continuity-topological-definition.md question 1 option D note: says sequential continuity matches continuity "only in metric spaces." It matches in all first-countable spaces. Question 2 text: "maps most open set" (garbled).
24. continuity-topological-spaces.md, Explainer para 2: "f is continuous at x₀ iff for every open V containing f(x₀), the preimage f⁻¹(V) is an open set containing x₀." Pointwise continuity only needs f⁻¹(V) to be a neighborhood of x₀. Question 3 text: "most open set" (garbled).
25. continuous-functions-topology.md, question 2 text: "usually maps open sets" (garbled "always"/plain statement).
26. contraction-mapping-theorem.md, question 3: the premise "a closed, bounded subset of ℝ … not complete" is impossible. The explanation says "a closed subset of an incomplete metric space inherits the incompleteness," which is false ([1/4, 3/4] is closed in (0, 1) and complete).
27. contraction-mapping-topology.md, question 3 text: "for most distinct x, y … nearly every distance" (garbled "all"). Question 0 explanation suggests Brouwer could give option A's "unique" fixed point; Brouwer gives existence only. Explainer: links Nash equilibria to the contraction theorem; Nash existence uses Brouwer/Kakutani.
28. convergence-in-topology.md, question 3 explanation, and countability-axioms-topology.md question 3: sequence characterization of closure fails "precisely" in non-first-countable spaces. Failure can only occur there, but many non-first-countable spaces (Fréchet–Urysohn spaces) still pass.
29. countability-axioms-topology.md, Core Idea: "Second-countable spaces are separable … and metrizable." Second-countable does not imply metrizable (regular Hausdorff is also needed, per Urysohn). The file's own question 1 explanation says so.
30. countability-axioms-topology.md, question 3 explanation: in the cocountable topology "a point is in the closure of every infinite set." Countable sets are closed there; points lie in the closure of every uncountable set.
31. countability-axioms-topology.md, question 0 and Explainer: first countability lets sequences characterize "all topological properties." Compactness is a counterexample (ω₁ is first-countable and sequentially compact but not compact).
32. covering-spaces.md, question 3 explanation: "A covering map is injective only in the trivial case where X̃ = X and p is the identity." Any one-sheeted cover is a homeomorphism, not necessarily the identity. Explainer: "the more of the fundamental group that 'lifts' to a loop in X̃ (rather than becoming contractible)" garbles the lifting criterion.
33. dense-sets-and-nowhere-dense.md, question 1 explanation: closed nowhere dense sets of positive measure exist "in principle (though not in ℝ for standard examples)." Fat Cantor sets in ℝ are standard examples. Question 4 explanation: "a measure-zero set can be non-meager (like ℚ)." ℚ is meager. (Non-meager measure-zero sets do exist, but ℚ is not one.)
34. dense-sets-topology-definition.md, Explainer: "separability makes it possible to work with sequences rather than nets." That is first countability, not separability. Also "The integers-to-reals analogy" should read rationals-to-reals.
