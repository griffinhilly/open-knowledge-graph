## Flags

### Suspected factual errors in existing text

1. golden-rule-capital-accumulation.md, Question 0: the answer key is wrong. The stem gives f'(k*) = 0.06 < δ = 0.10. Because f' falls as k rises, f' < δ means capital is ABOVE the golden rule, which is dynamic inefficiency. The correct option is index 1 ("dynamically inefficient — reducing the savings rate would raise steady-state consumption"), not 2. The explanation text is also self-contradictory ("Dynamic inefficiency ... occurs when f'(k*) < δ in the other direction").
2. golden-rule-capital-accumulation.md, Core Idea: "Most developed economies accumulate capital beyond the golden-rule level". This contradicts the file's own Explainer, which cites Abel, Mankiw, Summers and Zeckhauser (1989) finding developed economies dynamically efficient, that is, below the golden rule.
3. asset-pricing-macro.md, Explainer: "assets whose returns covary negatively with consumption growth (falling when consumption falls) must offer higher expected returns". Falling when consumption falls is POSITIVE covariance with consumption growth (negative covariance with the SDF). The sign is wrong.
4. asset-pricing-macro.md, Question 0 explanation: "they pay off in booms (when household consumption and marginal utility are low)". In booms consumption is high and marginal utility is low.
5. balance-of-payments-capital-flows.md, Explainer: lists "China in the early 2000s" as a fast-growing economy that borrowed from abroad (ran a current account deficit). China ran current account surpluses in the 2000s; the file's own Question 2 says so.
6. consumption-smoothing-intertemporal.md, Explainer: "because marginal utility is concave (each additional dollar of consumption is worth less than the last)". Utility is concave, meaning marginal utility is diminishing; the statement as written is wrong.
7. euler-equation-intertemporal-choice.md, Explainer: "consumption growth ... is ... a decreasing function of the risk aversion parameter σ". This holds only when β(1+r) > 1. When β(1+r) < 1, a higher σ moves growth toward zero from below, so growth increases with σ.
8. household-optimization-consumption-savings.md, Explainer: "If the interest rate rises ... (lower consumption today) ... The household saves more." This states that current consumption falls, which contradicts the file's own Question 2 (net effect ambiguous because of income effects).
9. lifecycle-hypothesis-consumption.md, Core Idea: "saving when young (and earning less)". The Explainer says young workers borrow or save little because income is low; saving peaks in middle age. The Core Idea has this wrong.
10. lifecycle-hypothesis-consumption.md, Question 4 explanation: says the 2001 US rebate evidence "broadly confirms" small consumption responses. Studies of that rebate found sizable spending out of it; the claim looks overstated.
11. human-capital-accumulation.md, Core Idea: "Unlike physical capital, human capital grows through intentional investment". Physical capital also grows through intentional investment; the contrast is false.
12. government-debt-fiscal-sustainability.md, Explainer: Country B (b = 100%, r − g = −2%) "can actually run a primary deficit of 2% of GDP while still seeing its debt ratio fall". With Δb ≈ (r − g)b + d = −2 + 2 = 0, the ratio is stable, not falling.
13. financial-accelerator-mechanism.md, Core Idea: "creating a negative feedback loop". The loop described is self-reinforcing, which is positive feedback.
14. financial-accelerator-mechanism.md, Question 1 stem: "The financial accelerator is symmetric — it amplifies downturns but not expansions." The stem contradicts itself ("symmetric" vs "not expansions"). Rewrite the stem.
15. forward-guidance-central-bank.md, Core Idea: "central banks lower current long-term rates and inflation expectations". At the zero lower bound, forward guidance aims to RAISE inflation expectations (lowering real rates), as the file's own Explainer says.
16. financial-frictions-amplification.md, Explainer and Question 2: "In a frictionless world, a 1% decline in productivity would cause roughly a 1% decline in output ... 2–3% output decline" with frictions. These numbers look invented. Quantitative accelerator models usually report more modest amplification.
17. endogenous-growth-lucas.md, Question 0 explanation: "Solow/Ramsey models, where countries converge to the same steady-state income level regardless of policy differences". In Solow, saving and other policies change the steady-state level; countries converge to the same growth rate, not the same level.
18. expectation-formation-mechanisms.md and inflation-unemployment-tradeoff.md (Question 3): these state that under rational expectations, anticipated policy has no real effects or a credible disinflation is costless. That holds with flexible prices. With sticky prices (the New Keynesian prerequisite of inflation-unemployment-tradeoff), anticipated policy does have real effects. Consider qualifying.
19. government-spending-multiplier.md, Explainer and Question 4 explanation: "The empirical evidence broadly supports larger multipliers at the ZLB and during recessions". The evidence is contested (some prominent studies find no larger multipliers in slack periods), so this is overstated.
20. fiscal-policy-sustainability.md, Question 3 (true-false, answer true): "must immediately run a primary surplus to prevent debt from becoming unsustainable". A primary surplus of (r − g)d is needed to keep the ratio stable. A ratio that rises for a while is not by itself unsustainable, so "true" is overstated.
21. intergenerational-equity-fiscal-policy.md, Core Idea: treats "present value of spending matched by taxes" (solvency) as the definition of intergenerational equity. These are different concepts. The Explainer also says persistent deficits satisfy the budget constraint "only if some future generation faces a dramatic tax increase". That does not hold when r < g.
22. intergenerational-equity-fiscal-policy.md, Question 2 stem: "a deficit-financed tax cut is equivalent in welfare terms to an immediate tax cut of the same present value" is garbled, since both are tax cuts today. It probably means equivalent to tax-financed (no change) policy.
23. dsge-models.md, Core Idea: calls DSGE models "the state-of-the-art tool for macroeconomic forecasting". Their forecasting record relative to statistical models is mixed; consider softening.
