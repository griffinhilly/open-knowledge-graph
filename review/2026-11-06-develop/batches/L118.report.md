## Flags
- ya-multiple-perspective-narration.md, Q4 explanation: "in 'It Ends with Us' or similar dual-perspective narratives". Colleen Hoover's *It Ends with Us* is adult fiction, not YA. It is narrated mainly by one character (Lily, with her journal letters), so it is a poor example of dual-perspective YA narration.
- ya-lgbtq-identity-representation.md, Explainer: "Research consistently shows that representation in literature supports psychological wellbeing of LGBTQ+ young readers". Q3 explanation says the same: "Research and reader testimony both confirm". Both overstate the evidence and need a hedge.
- ya-psychological-maturation-development.md, Explainer: "Central to psychological maturation is development of ... 'theory of mind'". Basic theory of mind develops in early childhood (around ages 4-5 on false-belief tasks), so calling it central to adolescent maturation is misleading. More advanced perspective-taking does keep developing in adolescence. Also a typo in the Q4 explanation: "synonymry".
- young-adult-literature-genre-emergence.md, Explainer: "*The Outsiders*, written when Hinton was seventeen". Hinton is usually reported as starting the book at about 15 and finishing it at 16, and it was published in 1967 when she was 18. The age needs checking.
- young-adult-literature-genre-emergence.md, Explainer: "Before this emergence, the literary landscape was divided into children's literature and adult literature". This is in tension with the file's own Q3 answer ("Literature for adolescents existed before"), since teen-targeted novels existed in the 1940s-50s. Core Idea/Explainer: "the dominant category for readers aged 12-18" is unsourced and vague.
- ya-realism-contemporary-social-problems.md, Q2 (true-false): the claim that realist fiction depicting systemic racism "is promoting activism" is keyed false with a flat answer. Whether such fiction advocates is a contested critical question, and the file's own tags include "activism". Consider rewording.
