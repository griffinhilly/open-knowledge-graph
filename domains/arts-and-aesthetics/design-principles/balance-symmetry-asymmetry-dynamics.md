# Balance, Symmetry, and Asymmetry

## Core Idea
Balance is the distribution of visual weight in a composition. Symmetrical balance (mirroring across an axis) creates formality and stability. Asymmetrical balance (unequal but felt to be balanced) creates energy and interest. Radial balance organizes elements around a central point. All three types can feel balanced, but they communicate different messages and emotions.

## How It's Best Learned
Photograph or sketch compositions that use each type of balance (symmetrical, asymmetrical, radial). Notice how each type feels and what mood it conveys. Recompose an asymmetrical layout as symmetrical and vice versa to see how the message changes.

## Common Misconceptions
- Symmetrical designs are boring; symmetry can be powerful and commanding when used intentionally.
- Asymmetrical balance requires equal elements on opposite sides; balance is about felt equilibrium, not mathematical equality.
- Radial balance is only for logos; it applies to any composition organized around a center point.

## Explainer

Think of balance as the visual equivalent of physical weight on a seesaw. If you place a heavy object on one side, the seesaw tips — and a composition works the same way. Large, dark, or visually complex elements carry more **visual weight** than small, light, or simple ones, and a composition feels balanced when these weights are distributed so that no single area overwhelms the rest. The three types of balance — symmetrical, asymmetrical, and radial — are not ranked from simple to complex; they are different tools that communicate different things.

**Symmetrical balance** mirrors elements across a central axis, either vertically, horizontally, or both. Think of a formal wedding invitation, a government building's facade, or the human face. Symmetry communicates stability, authority, formality, and order. It is the default language of institutions — courthouses, corporate logos, ceremonial documents — precisely because it feels resolved and permanent. But symmetry can also feel static or rigid if used without variation. Most "symmetrical" designs introduce subtle asymmetries (a slightly off-center title, a decorative element that breaks the mirror) to keep the eye engaged while preserving the overall sense of order.

**Asymmetrical balance** is more challenging to achieve because it requires the designer to intuit rather than calculate equilibrium. A large, muted photograph on the left side of a layout might be balanced by a small block of bold text on the right — they are not equal in size, but they feel equal in visual pull. This works because visual weight depends on multiple factors: size, color intensity, contrast with the background, level of detail, and position relative to the center. An element near the edge of a composition exerts more leverage (like sitting at the far end of a seesaw), so a small element placed far from center can balance a larger one closer to center. Asymmetrical compositions feel dynamic, contemporary, and energetic — they suggest movement and tension rather than repose.

**Radial balance** organizes elements around a central point, like spokes on a wheel or petals on a flower. Rose windows in Gothic cathedrals, mandalas, and clock faces all use radial balance. The effect is one of focus and unity — all elements point toward or radiate from the center, drawing the eye inward. Radial balance can feel meditative, ceremonial, or hypnotic depending on context. In practical design, radial arrangements appear in dashboards, circular navigation menus, and infographics organized around a central concept.

The key insight is that balance is not about mathematical equality — it is about **felt equilibrium**. A viewer does not measure pixel counts; they sense whether a composition feels settled or tipping. Developing this sense requires practice: take any layout, cover one half, and notice whether the remaining half feels like it could stand alone or whether it pulls toward the missing side. Recompose the same content using each type of balance and observe how the emotional register shifts — from formal to dynamic to focused. This exercise builds the intuition that makes balance a controllable design variable rather than an accident.
