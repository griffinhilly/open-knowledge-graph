# Fresco Technique and Renaissance Mural Painting

## Core Idea
Fresco is the technique of painting on freshly laid, still-wet plaster (intonaco), allowing pigments to fuse chemically with the wall and creating permanent color. The method requires rapid execution, advance planning through preparatory drawings (sinopia), and mastery of pigments that work only in alkaline conditions.

## How It's Best Learned
Study the frescoes of Giotto, Masaccio, and Michelangelo to see how the technique constrains composition and style. Research the sinopia (underdrawings) revealed when frescoes are detached. Understand the difference between buon fresco (true fresco) and fresco secco (painting on dry plaster), which produce different visual qualities.

## Common Misconceptions
- Fresco is simply painting on walls—the chemical process and working speed create unique aesthetic and practical constraints. - Frescoes are fragile—when properly executed, fresco is one of the most durable painting techniques. - Any wall painting is fresco—fresco secco and other techniques produce different results and longevity.

## Explainer

To understand fresco, you need to think of it not as painting on a wall but as painting *into* a wall. In **buon fresco** (true fresco), the artist applies pigments mixed with water directly onto freshly laid lime plaster called **intonaco**. As the plaster dries, a chemical reaction called carbonation occurs: the lime absorbs carbon dioxide from the air and converts into calcium carbonate, essentially locking the pigment particles into a crystalline mineral surface. The color becomes part of the wall itself, which is why properly executed frescoes survive for centuries while oil paintings on canvas crack and flake.

This chemistry imposes severe constraints that shaped Renaissance artistic practice. The plaster dries in roughly six to eight hours, so the artist can only apply as much intonaco as can be painted in a single working session — a section called a **giornata** (literally "a day's work"). Every brushstroke is essentially permanent; unlike oil painting, you cannot scrape off and repaint. This means the entire composition must be planned in advance. Renaissance artists developed elaborate preparatory systems: full-scale drawings called **cartoons** were transferred to the wall using pouncing (pricking holes along outlines and dusting charcoal through them) or incision. Earlier artists painted preliminary sketches called **sinopia** directly on the rough underlayer of plaster, and when modern conservators detach frescoes for restoration, these sinopia are sometimes revealed — offering a fascinating window into the artist's original intentions.

The technique also limits the painter's palette. Only pigments that remain stable in the alkaline environment of wet lime plaster can be used in buon fresco — earth pigments like ochres, siennas, and umbers work well, but certain blues and greens must be applied **a secco** (on dry plaster with a binding medium) after the fresco has cured. This is why you sometimes see damage patterns where secco passages have flaked away while the surrounding buon fresco remains intact. Michelangelo's Sistine Chapel ceiling, for instance, was executed almost entirely in buon fresco, which accounts for the remarkable preservation of its colors after five centuries.

Understanding fresco helps explain why Renaissance mural painting looks the way it does. The need to work quickly encouraged bold, confident brushwork rather than meticulous blending. The giornata system meant that compositions were divided into logical sections — often a single figure or a head — and the seams between days of work are sometimes visible under raking light. The constraints of the medium did not limit artistic ambition; rather, they demanded extraordinary planning, technical mastery, and decisiveness that became hallmarks of the Renaissance workshop tradition.
