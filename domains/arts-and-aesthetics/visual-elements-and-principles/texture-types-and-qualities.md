# Texture: Types and Qualities

## Core Idea
Texture refers to the visual or tactile quality of a surface—the way it looks and feels. Textures can be actual (physically felt) or implied (visually suggested through marks or patterns). Textures range from smooth and refined to rough and organic, from regular geometric patterns to irregular, spontaneous variations. Texture adds visual richness, suggests materiality and age, creates visual interest through variety, and can contrast with smoother areas to create emphasis.

## Explainer

Among the seven visual elements you have already surveyed, texture is the one most directly connected to our sense of touch. Even when we are only looking at a surface — a photograph of bark, a painting of velvet, a digital rendering of stone — our brain activates tactile associations. We "feel" the roughness or smoothness through our eyes alone. This crossover between seeing and feeling makes texture an unusually powerful tool for creating visceral, immediate responses in visual work.

The first distinction to grasp is between **actual texture** and **implied texture**. Actual texture is physically present: the raised brushstrokes in an impasto oil painting, the bumpy surface of handmade paper, the ridges in a letterpress print. You can close your eyes and feel it. Implied texture exists only visually — an artist uses marks, patterns, and value variation to suggest a surface quality that is not physically there. A pencil drawing of a woolen sweater has the same smooth graphite surface everywhere, but clusters of short, curving lines imply the softness and bulk of knitted fabric. Most visual work relies primarily on implied texture, and mastering it means learning which mark patterns trigger which tactile associations in a viewer's mind.

Textures can be further classified by their character. **Regular textures** repeat a consistent pattern — think of brickwork, woven fabric, or a tiled floor. They feel ordered, manufactured, and controlled. **Irregular textures** are unpredictable — cracked earth, lichen on stone, the grain of split wood. They feel natural, organic, and often more visually interesting because the eye cannot predict the next variation. Most compelling compositions mix both: the regular texture of a building contrasted against the irregular texture of surrounding foliage, or the smooth skin of a figure set against a roughly textured background.

Texture's most important compositional role is creating **contrast and emphasis**. A heavily textured area next to a smooth area draws the eye to the boundary between them. This is why portrait painters often render faces with smooth, careful blending but leave backgrounds loose and textured — the contrast focuses attention on the face. Conversely, a uniformly textured surface can create a sense of all-over pattern that de-emphasizes any single area. Learning to control texture — where to add it, where to withhold it, and how much variation to introduce — gives you a tool for directing attention and enriching the visual experience of any composition, whether realistic or abstract.
