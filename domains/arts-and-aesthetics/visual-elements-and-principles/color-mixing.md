# Advanced Color Mixing

## Core Idea
Color mixing is the process of combining pigments (or light) to produce new colors. Subtractive mixing — used with paints and inks — means pigments absorb certain wavelengths of light, and mixing them produces darker results (mixing all colors approaches black). Additive mixing — used with light — means colors add brightness, and mixing all primaries produces white. Understanding which model applies to your medium prevents confusion when mixing behaves unexpectedly.

## How It's Best Learned
Work with a limited palette (e.g., just red, yellow, blue, black, and white) to explore how many colors can be derived through mixing. Keep a mixing journal documenting proportions.

## Common Misconceptions
- More pigment always makes a color more intense — false; excessive mixing muddies colors.
- Digital color mixing behaves like light (additive), not paint (subtractive), which surprises students switching between media.

## Explainer

When you learned the color wheel, you saw how colors relate to each other — primaries, secondaries, and complementaries. Color mixing is where that knowledge becomes practical: it explains what actually happens when you combine pigments or lights, and why the results sometimes surprise you.

The key concept is that there are two entirely different mixing systems depending on your medium. **Subtractive mixing** applies to physical pigments — paints, inks, dyes. The word "subtractive" refers to what the pigments do to light: each pigment absorbs (subtracts) certain wavelengths and reflects others. When you mix two pigments, you are combining their absorption ranges, so more wavelengths get absorbed and fewer are reflected. The result is always darker than either starting color. Taken to the extreme, mixing all pigment primaries together absorbs nearly all light and produces a dark, muddy near-black.

**Additive mixing** applies to light itself — screens, stage lighting, projectors. Here, combining light sources adds wavelengths together. Red light plus green light makes yellow; add blue and you approach white. This is why your phone screen can display any color using only tiny red, green, and blue pixels. The result moves toward brightness, not darkness. These two systems use different sets of primaries: red, yellow, blue for subtractive (traditional painting); red, green, blue for additive (light and screens).

Understanding which system you're working in prevents a lot of confusion. A common trap for painters moving to digital is expecting digital colors to blend like paint — but a screen is an additive system, even if the software simulates subtractive blending. Conversely, mixing complementary pigments (opposite sides of the color wheel) tends to neutralize and darken the mixture because their combined absorptions cover a wide range of wavelengths — a useful technique for mixing grays and darks without using black.

In practice, painters work with subtractive mixing daily. The most important takeaways: limit the number of pigments in any single mix (two or three at most), understand that adding more pigment darkens and neutralizes rather than intensifying, and pay attention to the specific pigments in your paints — not all reds, yellows, or blues behave identically because each reflects a particular range of wavelengths.
