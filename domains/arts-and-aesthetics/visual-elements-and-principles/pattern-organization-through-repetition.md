# Pattern: Organization Through Repetition

## Core Idea
Pattern is the principle of repeating visual elements to create order and visual interest. Patterns can be regular (predictable and orderly) or irregular (varied with surprise). Patterns organize space, unify designs, and can communicate information efficiently. Understanding pattern is foundational to textile, graphic, and interior design.

## Explainer

From your study of visual fundamentals and shape, you know that individual elements — a circle, a triangle, a brushstroke — carry meaning on their own. **Pattern** is what happens when you repeat those elements systematically. The moment a shape appears a second time in a predictable relationship to the first, the viewer's brain stops seeing isolated objects and starts seeing a system. This shift from "things" to "structure" is the essence of pattern: repetition transforms individual elements into organized wholes.

The simplest patterns are **regular patterns**, where an element repeats at equal intervals in a predictable arrangement. Think of a checkerboard, a brick wall, or the tiles on a bathroom floor. Regular patterns communicate order, stability, and intentionality. Your eye can predict what comes next, and that predictability creates a sense of calm and control. The repeating unit in a pattern is called the **motif** — the smallest complete element that, when repeated, generates the entire pattern. A single brick is the motif; the wall is the pattern. Understanding the motif lets you design patterns efficiently: get the unit right, and the repetition does the rest.

**Irregular patterns** introduce variation into the repetition. The motif might change slightly each time it appears — varying in size, color, spacing, or orientation. A field of wildflowers has a pattern (flowers repeat across the landscape) but it is irregular (no two flowers are identical, and their spacing varies). Irregular patterns feel more natural and organic than regular ones because they echo how repetition works in the real world, where perfect uniformity is rare. The tension between predictability and surprise is what makes irregular patterns visually engaging — the viewer recognizes the system but stays interested because each repetition offers something slightly new.

Pattern serves several practical functions in design. It **unifies** a composition by creating visual consistency — when the same element appears throughout, the parts feel like they belong together. It **fills space** efficiently, turning empty areas into active surfaces without requiring unique content in every spot. And it **communicates rhythm** — just as musical rhythm emerges from repeated beats, visual rhythm emerges from repeated elements. Breaking a pattern is one of the most powerful ways to create emphasis: if you establish a regular grid of blue squares and then place one red square among them, the break in the pattern instantly draws the eye. Pattern and pattern-breaking work together as complementary tools for organizing attention.
