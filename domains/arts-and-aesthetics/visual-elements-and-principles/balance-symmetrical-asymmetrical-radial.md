# Balance: Symmetrical, Asymmetrical, and Radial

## Core Idea
Visual balance refers to the distribution of visual weight in a composition—the sense that elements are evenly distributed rather than top-heavy, lopsided, or chaotic. Symmetrical balance (identical elements mirrored across a centerline) creates stability, formality, and predictability. Asymmetrical balance (different but equally weighted elements) creates more dynamic, interesting, and complex compositions. Radial balance (elements radiating from a center point) creates movement, focus, and natural organization.

## Explainer

From your study of the seven design principles, you know that balance is one of the fundamental forces governing how a composition feels. Now it is time to understand the three distinct strategies for achieving it. Think of a composition as a visual seesaw: every element has **visual weight** — determined by its size, color intensity, value contrast, detail complexity, and position — and the composition feels balanced when those weights are distributed so the whole image does not seem to tip in one direction.

**Symmetrical balance** is the most straightforward approach. Mirror identical or near-identical elements across a vertical, horizontal, or diagonal axis, and the composition automatically feels stable and ordered. Think of a classical building facade, a butterfly's wings, or a formal wedding invitation. The effect is calm, dignified, and authoritative. The trade-off is that perfect symmetry can feel static or predictable — the viewer's eye has no reason to explore because each side delivers the same information. Symmetrical balance is powerful when you want formality, gravity, or timelessness, but it requires deliberate effort to keep it from becoming monotonous.

**Asymmetrical balance** achieves equilibrium through contrast rather than mirroring. A large, light-colored shape on one side might be balanced by a small, dark, highly detailed element on the other — like a heavy adult and a light child on a seesaw, where the child sits farther from the fulcrum. This is where visual weight becomes nuanced: a bright red dot can balance a large gray rectangle because color intensity carries disproportionate weight. Asymmetrical compositions feel more dynamic, energetic, and engaging because the viewer's eye must work to discover how the balance is achieved. Most contemporary design and editorial layouts rely on asymmetrical balance for this reason.

**Radial balance** arranges elements around a central point, like spokes on a wheel or petals on a flower. The center becomes the natural focal point, and the radiating elements create a sense of movement and energy flowing outward or inward. You see radial balance in rose windows, mandalas, clock faces, and ripples in water. It combines the orderliness of symmetry with a rotational dynamism that keeps the eye circling. In practice, many compositions blend these three types — a largely asymmetrical layout might use a radially balanced element as its focal point, or a symmetrical composition might introduce slight asymmetries to add life. The key is recognizing which type of balance you are using and whether it serves the emotional intention of the piece.
