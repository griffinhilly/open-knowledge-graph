# Line: Weight, Direction, and Continuity

## Core Idea
Lines have expressive properties independent of their path: weight (thickness), direction (horizontal, vertical, diagonal), and continuity (unbroken, dotted, dashed, or implied). These properties communicate emotion and guide viewer attention; a thin diagonal line feels different from a thick horizontal line, and an implied line can be as powerful as a drawn one.

## How It's Best Learned
Practice drawing the same line with different weights, directions, and continuities to directly experience how each property changes the feeling and visual impact. Notice how weight can suggest material, direction can suggest movement, and broken lines can suggest distance or discontinuity.

## Common Misconceptions
All lines in a drawing should have consistent weight. A broken or implied line is less important than a continuous one. Only fully drawn lines count as lines.

## Explainer

From your observation of lines in nature and art, you know that lines exist everywhere — in the edges of objects, the cracks in pavement, the horizon where sky meets land. Now the question becomes: what makes one line feel different from another, even when they follow the same path? The answer lies in three expressive properties — **weight**, **direction**, and **continuity** — each of which changes how a line communicates and how the viewer responds to it.

**Line weight** is thickness. A heavy, bold line feels confident, solid, and close — it demands attention. A thin, delicate line feels tentative, distant, or refined. Artists use weight variation within a single drawing to create depth and emphasis: thicker lines for foreground objects and contour edges that face the viewer, thinner lines for background details and edges that turn away. Think of a comic book inker who uses thick outlines on the hero's silhouette but thin, light lines for facial details — the weight difference separates what is structurally important from what is secondary. Varying weight within a single continuous stroke (thick-to-thin or thin-to-thick) creates a sense of pressure, speed, and life that uniform weight cannot.

**Direction** determines a line's emotional character. Horizontal lines suggest rest, calm, and landscape — the horizon is the most fundamental horizontal line in human experience. Vertical lines suggest strength, growth, and alertness — trees, buildings, standing figures. Diagonal lines introduce tension, movement, and energy because they feel unstable, as though caught mid-fall or mid-ascent. **Continuity** refers to whether the line is unbroken, dashed, dotted, or merely implied — suggested by the alignment of other elements without being physically drawn. An implied line is remarkably powerful: three dots arranged diagonally will make your eye trace a line between them automatically, completing the path that the artist only hinted at. Broken lines can suggest distance, uncertainty, or permeability, while solid continuous lines feel definitive and assertive. Together, these three properties give you precise control over how a line feels, what it communicates, and where it sends the viewer's eye.
