# Balance in Composition

## Core Idea
Balance is the principle of distributing visual weight throughout a composition so that no single area feels too heavy or too light. Symmetrical balance mirrors elements across an axis, creating formal, stable, often static compositions. Asymmetrical balance achieves equilibrium through contrast — a small, bright object can counterbalance a large, dull one. Radial balance arranges elements around a central point, like a wheel or mandala. Every compositional choice — size, value, color, texture — contributes to visual weight.

## How It's Best Learned
Cut shapes from colored paper and physically arrange them on a background, shifting position until the arrangement feels stable. Notice how a small dark shape can balance a large light shape.

## Common Misconceptions
- Balance always means symmetry — asymmetrical balance is equally (and often more) visually sophisticated.
- Visual weight is determined only by size — color, value, texture, and isolation all contribute to how 'heavy' a shape feels.

## Explainer

When you studied positive and negative space, you learned that what is absent in a composition is as meaningful as what is present. Balance builds directly on that idea: it is about how the visual weights of all elements — positive forms and the negative spaces between them — are distributed across the composition so that the whole feels stable and intentional.

The most familiar type of balance is symmetrical: mirror both sides across a central axis, and the result feels formal, stable, and authoritative. Think of a classical building's facade, a religious altarpiece, or a corporate logo. Symmetry communicates permanence and order. Its limitation is that it can feel static or obvious — there is little tension for the eye to resolve, and the composition's outcome is predictable once you have seen one half.

Asymmetrical balance is more demanding to achieve but far more common in dynamic design and contemporary art. The challenge is that visual weight depends on multiple properties simultaneously — not just size. A small, dark shape can balance a large, light one. A textured area can balance a smooth one. An isolated element commands more weight than a similar element surrounded by others. A warm-colored object feels heavier than a cool one of the same size. Placing a bright red dot far from the center of a composition can balance a large field of neutral gray on the other side — not despite being small, but because of the intense contrast it creates.

Radial balance is less common but immediately recognizable: elements radiate outward from a center point, as in a mandala, a rose window, or a sunflower. It creates a different kind of stability than bilateral symmetry — one that feels expansive and cyclical rather than static.

The practical skill here is developing sensitivity to visual weight so you can make and evaluate compositional choices deliberately. When a composition feels "off," the problem is almost always imbalance — something is pulling too hard in one area without a counterweight. The diagnostic question is: what does this element weigh, and what is counterbalancing it? Adjusting value, size, color temperature, texture, or placement can all shift the balance without moving the main subject.
