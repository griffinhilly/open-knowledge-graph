# Drawing Tools, Papers, and Painting Substrates

## Core Idea
Different drawing and painting materials behave uniquely and demand specific techniques. Pencils, charcoal, and pastels have different hardness grades and mark-making properties; papers vary in weight, texture, and sizing; and painting substrates (canvas, paper, panel) affect paint handling and longevity. Understanding material properties—tooth, absorbency, permanence—allows you to match tool to intention and troubleshoot when results don't match expectations.

## How It's Best Learned
Handle a variety of pencil grades, charcoal types, and paper textures. Make sample marks on different substrates and observe differences in opacity, blending, and permanence.

## Common Misconceptions
- All white paper is the same; acid-free and sizing matter for longevity.
- Expensive materials guarantee better work; material knowledge beats price.
- Canvas is the only 'serious' surface; paper and panel are equally valid.

## Explainer

From your prerequisite work on drawing materials, you understand the general categories — graphite, charcoal, ink, pastel, and so on — and their basic properties. This topic goes deeper into the interaction between tool and surface, because the surface you draw or paint on is not a passive recipient. It is an active partner that shapes how every medium behaves.

**Paper** is defined by three key properties. **Weight** (measured in pounds or grams per square meter) determines thickness and durability — lightweight sketch paper (50–60 lb) is fine for pencil studies but buckles under wet media, while heavyweight paper (140 lb+) can handle watercolor washes without warping. **Tooth** describes the surface texture: rough or "cold-pressed" paper has pronounced peaks and valleys that grip charcoal and pastel, producing rich, textured marks; smooth or "hot-pressed" paper allows fine pen lines and detailed graphite work but lets charcoal slide off. **Sizing** — a gelatin or starch coating applied during manufacture — controls absorbency. Heavily sized paper resists water penetration, letting watercolor sit on the surface in bright pools; unsized paper absorbs like a sponge, producing soft, diffused edges.

**Painting substrates** follow a parallel logic. **Canvas** — linen or cotton stretched over a frame and primed with gesso — has a woven tooth that grips paint and allows textured brushwork. Linen is finer-grained and more durable; cotton is affordable and perfectly adequate for most work. **Wood panels** (typically hardboard or birch plywood, primed with gesso) offer a rigid, smooth surface ideal for detailed work and thin paint applications. **Paper** for painting (watercolor paper or mixed-media paper) is lightweight and portable but less archival for oil paint unless properly prepared.

The practical skill is **matching material to intention**. If you want soft, blended graphite shading, you need paper with enough tooth to hold multiple layers but not so much texture that it dominates the mark. If you want crisp ink lines, you need smooth, well-sized paper that prevents bleeding. If you want impasto oil painting with visible brushstrokes, you need canvas or panel with enough tooth and rigidity to support thick paint. When a drawing or painting is not working, the problem is often not technique but a mismatch between medium and surface — charcoal on glossy paper, watercolor on lightweight stock, oil paint on unprimed canvas. Learning to diagnose these mismatches is as important as learning to use the tools themselves.
