# Foreshortening

## Core Idea
Foreshortening is the apparent compression of a form that is oriented toward or away from the viewer, caused by the same perspective principles that make railroad tracks seem to converge. A forearm pointing directly at the viewer appears very short — perhaps a third of its actual length — even though the circles of the knuckles remain full size. Drawing foreshortened forms requires abandoning knowledge of 'how long an arm is' and drawing only what is seen: overlapping shapes, compressed lengths, and the ellipses of rounded cross-sections. It is one of the most counterintuitive challenges in observational drawing.

## How It's Best Learned
Practice by drawing your own arm extended toward you in a mirror. Compare the apparent length to the width. Then work with posed figures on Quickposes filtered to foreshortening views. Use negative space around the foreshortened limb to check accuracy.

## Common Misconceptions
- Students consistently draw foreshortened limbs too long because they 'know' how long a limb is. Trust what the eye sees, not what the brain expects.
- Overlapping forms are the most powerful cue for foreshortening — emphasize them rather than trying to indicate depth through shading alone.

## Explainer

You already understand how one-point and two-point perspective make parallel lines converge toward vanishing points, creating the illusion of depth on a flat surface. Foreshortening applies the same principle to **organic, rounded forms** — arms, legs, torsos, cylinders, fingers pointing at you. When a form extends directly toward or away from the viewer, perspective compresses its apparent length while its width remains relatively unchanged. The result looks dramatically different from what you "know" the form looks like, which is why foreshortening is one of the hardest drawing skills to develop.

Consider a simple cylinder — like a paper towel roll — lying flat on a table. Viewed from the side, it looks like a rectangle. Now tilt one end toward you. The rectangle appears to shorten. Tilt it further until it points straight at you, and you see mostly a circle with almost no visible length. The cylinder has not changed, but your viewing angle has compressed its longest dimension. This is foreshortening in its simplest form. Now replace the cylinder with a human arm: the shoulder is a sphere, the upper arm a tapered cylinder, the elbow a hinge, the forearm another cylinder, the hand a complex block. When that arm points at you, each segment compresses, and the key visual information shifts from **length** to **overlap** — the hand overlaps the forearm, the forearm overlaps the upper arm, the upper arm overlaps the shoulder.

**Overlap** is the single most important tool for drawing foreshortened forms convincingly. When one form passes in front of another, the viewer's brain instantly reads depth. The contour line where the nearer form crosses in front of the farther one — called a **cross-contour** or **overlap line** — does more to communicate foreshortening than any amount of shading. This is why the Common Misconceptions section warns against relying on shading alone. To practice, think of each body segment as a simple geometric solid and focus on where each solid passes in front of the next.

The psychological challenge is trusting observation over knowledge. Your brain insists that an arm is much longer than it is wide, and it will fight you when you try to draw an arm that appears almost as wide as it is long. The antidote is **measuring** — use your pencil held at arm's length to compare the apparent width to the apparent length of the foreshortened form. You will often find the ratio is close to 1:1, which feels wrong but draws correctly. Checking proportions through measurement, using negative space around the form as a cross-reference, and consciously emphasizing overlaps will gradually override the brain's distortion and make foreshortened forms feel natural to draw.
