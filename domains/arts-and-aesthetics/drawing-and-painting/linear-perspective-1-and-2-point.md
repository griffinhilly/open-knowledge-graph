# Linear Perspective: One-Point and Two-Point Systems

## Core Idea
Linear perspective uses horizon lines and vanishing points to create the illusion of recession into space. One-point perspective (one vanishing point) shows frontal views and interior corridors; two-point perspective (two vanishing points) shows corners and oblique views of objects and buildings. Mastering these systems allows convincing depiction of interior and exterior spaces.

## Explainer

You already understand one-point and two-point perspective individually. This topic is about understanding them as two cases of a single unified system, knowing when to choose one over the other, and combining them fluently in compositions that contain multiple objects at different orientations.

The underlying principle is the same in both systems: **parallel lines in the real world appear to converge toward a shared vanishing point on the horizon line**. The horizon line represents your eye level — raise your eyes and it rises; lower them and it drops. In **one-point perspective**, you're looking straight at a surface (the front face of a building, the back wall of a hallway), so one set of receding lines converges to a single vanishing point directly ahead. The horizontal and vertical edges of the facing surface remain truly horizontal and vertical on the page. This setup works naturally for interiors, tunnels, roads, and any scene where you're facing something head-on.

In **two-point perspective**, you're looking at the corner of an object, so no face is parallel to your picture plane. Both sets of horizontal edges now recede — one set converging to a vanishing point on the left, the other to a vanishing point on the right. Only the vertical edges remain truly vertical on the page. This is the more common real-world viewing situation: you rarely see buildings perfectly face-on. Two-point perspective gives objects a more dynamic, three-dimensional presence because both sides visibly recede.

The practical decision between the two systems depends on your compositional intent. One-point perspective creates a strong sense of depth pulling the viewer inward — it's dramatic for hallways, roads, and architectural interiors where you want a focal point at the center of recession. Two-point perspective distributes visual interest more broadly and feels more natural for exterior scenes, street corners, and arrangements of multiple objects. Critically, a single scene can use both: a building seen at an angle uses two-point perspective, but a window on that building, if viewed straight-on, shows its interior in one-point. The horizon line unifies everything — all vanishing points in a scene sit on the same horizon because all objects share the same viewer eye level. Getting the horizon line right and placing vanishing points far enough apart (cramped vanishing points cause exaggerated, fish-eye distortion) are the two most important practical skills in making perspective drawings look convincing rather than warped.
