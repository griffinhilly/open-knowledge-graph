# Stippling and Pointillism

## Core Idea
Stippling is a technique that builds tone and form using individual dots or small marks rather than lines. The density and spacing of dots create perceived value, allowing artists to render detailed forms with patience and precision. It requires understanding how the eye optically blends separated marks.

## How It's Best Learned
Begin with light subjects to avoid hours of repetitive work. Use a consistent dot size initially, then experiment with varying mark size to create depth.

## Common Misconceptions
Thinking all dots must be identical in size and spacing. Larger or varied dots often create more interesting and realistic effects.

## Explainer

You already know from your work with value and tone that the illusion of three-dimensional form on a flat surface depends on controlling the transition from light to dark. In most drawing techniques — pencil shading, hatching, blending — you achieve this by varying the pressure or direction of continuous strokes. **Stippling** takes a radically different approach: it builds every tonal value entirely from individual dots. No lines, no strokes, just points of pigment arranged at varying densities across the surface.

The principle is straightforward. Where you want a dark area, you place dots close together so less white paper shows through. Where you want a light area, you space dots far apart. Your eye does the rest — from a normal viewing distance, the brain averages the dark dots and the white gaps between them into a smooth perceived tone, much the way a newspaper photograph resolves into a continuous image despite being printed as tiny halftone dots. This is called **optical mixing**, and it is the same phenomenon that underlies Neo-Impressionist painting, where Georges Seurat and Paul Signac built entire canvases from small points of pure color — the technique known as **Pointillism**.

In practice, stippling rewards patience and consistency. Start by establishing your lightest lights — these areas will have the fewest, most widely spaced dots. Gradually increase density as you move into the shadow areas, thinking of it like slowly turning up a dimmer switch. The key variable is **dot density** (how many dots per unit area), but you can also vary **dot size** to extend your tonal range: smaller dots for delicate highlights, larger dots for deep shadows. Resist the temptation to cluster dots in neat rows or grids — random-looking placement produces smoother, more natural gradations, while visible patterns create distracting moiré effects.

One of stippling's great strengths is the luminous quality it produces. Because the white paper is never fully covered, even the darkest stippled passages retain a subtle inner glow that solid shading cannot match. This makes it especially effective for rendering subjects with translucent or textured surfaces — skin, stone, fabric, foliage. The technique also forces you to slow down and observe value relationships more carefully than faster methods, which is why it remains a valuable training exercise even if you never use it as your primary technique.
