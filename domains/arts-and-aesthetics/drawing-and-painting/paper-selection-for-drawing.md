# Paper Selection for Drawing

## Core Idea
Paper varies in weight, texture, and composition, each affecting how drawing media behaves. Heavyweight papers (140 lb+) resist tearing and buckling; rough surfaces grip graphite, while smooth surfaces allow fine detail. Acid-free papers ensure longevity. Selecting appropriate paper for the medium—smooth for pen, textured for charcoal—enhances the final work and prevents frustration.

## How It's Best Learned
Purchase sample packs of drawing papers and test them with different media: pencil, charcoal, ink, and markers.

## Common Misconceptions
Believing any paper will work for any medium. Charcoal on smooth paper slides; pen on rough paper splays. Match the paper tooth to the medium.

## Explainer

Paper is not just something you draw on — it is a material with its own properties that actively shape your marks. Choosing the right paper for your medium is one of the simplest ways to improve your drawings immediately, and choosing the wrong paper is one of the most common sources of frustration for beginners who blame their technique when the real problem is the surface.

The most important property to understand is **tooth** — the texture of the paper surface. Run your finger across a sheet of watercolor paper and you feel pronounced bumps and valleys; that is heavy tooth. Run your finger across a sheet of Bristol smooth and it feels almost like glass; that is minimal tooth. Tooth matters because drawing media need something to grip. **Charcoal** and **soft pastel** are powdery and rely on tooth to hold their particles — on smooth paper, they slide around and refuse to build up dark values. **Graphite pencils** work on moderate tooth but produce their finest detail on smoother surfaces where the point can maintain consistent contact. **Ink pens** need smooth, well-sized paper; on rough paper, the nib catches on fibers and the line splays or bleeds.

**Weight** is the second key property, measured in pounds (lb) or grams per square meter (gsm). Lightweight sketch paper (50–60 lb) is fine for dry media like pencil but buckles and warps under wet media like ink washes or watercolor. Heavyweight paper (90 lb+ for drawing, 140 lb+ for watercolor) resists warping and can handle erasure and reworking without tearing. If you plan to layer, blend, and erase — as graphite techniques require — you need paper sturdy enough to withstand repeated pressure without pilling or developing shiny spots.

**Composition** determines longevity. **Acid-free** paper resists yellowing and brittleness over time; paper made from wood pulp without acid-free treatment will brown and deteriorate within years. For finished work you want to preserve, acid-free is non-negotiable. For daily practice and quick studies, less expensive paper is perfectly fine — the goal is volume of practice, not archival permanence. A practical approach is to keep two types on hand: inexpensive sketch paper for warmups and exploration, and higher-quality, acid-free paper for pieces you intend to finish and keep.
