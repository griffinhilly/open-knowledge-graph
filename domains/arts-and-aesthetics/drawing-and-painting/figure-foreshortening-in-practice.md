# Figure Foreshortening in Practice

## Core Idea
Body parts pointing toward or away from the viewer appear compressed and shortened. Understanding foreshortening allows drawing convincing figures from extreme angles. Perspective principles apply directly to the human form: receding parts shrink; overlapping clarifies depth.

## How It's Best Learned
Draw a model with limbs extending directly toward you — an arm reaching forward, a leg pointing at your eye level. Rely on what you see rather than what you know about the limb's actual proportions. Use overlapping contours and comparative measurement to override your brain's tendency to lengthen foreshortened forms.

## Common Misconceptions
- Students draw what they know (a full-length arm) instead of what they see (a compressed oval). Trusting observation over knowledge is the central challenge.
- Foreshortened forms are not simply smaller versions of the same shape; they change in proportion, with nearer parts appearing much larger than farther parts.

## Explainer

You already understand foreshortening as a perspective concept — objects receding in space appear compressed along the axis pointing toward the viewer. You also know the anatomical landmarks of the human body. **Figure foreshortening** is where those two bodies of knowledge collide, and it is one of the most challenging problems in figure drawing because it forces you to override your strongest instinct: drawing what you know instead of what you see.

Imagine a person lying on the ground with their feet pointing directly at you. You know their legs are roughly half their total height. But from this angle, the feet appear enormous — almost as wide as the torso — and the legs compress to a fraction of their true length. The thighs might occupy only a few inches of vertical space on your paper, while the feet loom large in the foreground. Your brain screams that this is wrong, that legs are long and feet are small. The entire challenge of figure foreshortening is learning to trust your eyes over that internal model. Every time you elongate a foreshortened limb to make it "look right," you flatten the drawing and destroy the illusion of depth.

The most powerful tool for drawing foreshortened figures is **overlap**. When one form passes in front of another — a knee overlapping a thigh, a shoulder overlapping a ribcage — it creates an unambiguous depth signal. Think of the body as a series of stacking cylinders: when a cylinder points toward you, its near end (a large ellipse) overlaps the form behind it, and each successive segment appears smaller. Draw the near form first and let the far forms tuck behind it. This overlap sequence is more important than getting proportions exactly right — even a rough drawing with clear overlaps reads as three-dimensional, while a careful drawing without overlaps reads as flat.

**Comparative measurement** is your practical method for getting the proportions right. Instead of relying on your memorized proportions (the arm is three heads long), hold your pencil at arm's length and measure what you actually see: the foreshortened forearm might appear to be the same width as the hand. Note these observed ratios and transfer them directly to your drawing. The anatomical landmarks you have studied — the elbow, the wrist, the knee — serve as checkpoints to confirm that your foreshortened proportions make structural sense, even when they look radically different from a standing figure. With practice, you will develop the ability to see foreshortened forms as compressed volumes rather than distorted flat shapes, and your figures will gain a convincing sense of depth and physical presence.
