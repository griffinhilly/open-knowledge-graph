# Counting to Ten

## Core Idea
Children extend their counting sequence to include 6, 7, 8, 9, 10. Understanding the full basic sequence to ten is essential for early math operations.

## How It's Best Learned
Count fingers (two hands). Use a ten-frame with counters. Count familiar objects like steps, buttons on a coat, or beads on a string.

## Common Misconceptions
Rushing and skipping numbers. Treating teen numbers (11-20) the same as single digits. Losing count mid-sequence.

## Explainer

You already know the counting sequence up to five: one, two, three, four, five. Now we extend that sequence all the way to ten by adding six, seven, eight, nine, ten. The numbers don't change their meaning — each one still means "one more than the one before." Counting to ten just means knowing six more words in the right order after five.

Your two hands make ten perfect: five fingers on one hand, five on the other. When you've counted all five on your left hand and all five on your right, you've counted ten things. You can use your fingers to keep track of any group of objects up to ten. Count one finger for each object you see, and the last finger you raised tells you how many there are.

A **ten-frame** is a helpful tool that looks like two rows of five boxes. To show the number seven, you fill seven boxes — a full row of five, plus two in the second row. This helps you see seven as "five and two more," eight as "five and three more," and so on. Seeing numbers as parts of ten builds a mental picture that will help you with addition and subtraction later.

When you count a group of things, always say the numbers in order, point to one object for each number you say, and stop when you've pointed to every object. The last number you say is how many objects there are. This is called counting — matching each number word to exactly one thing.
