# Writing Numerals 1–20

## Core Idea
Writing numerals 11–20 requires children to form two-digit numbers with correct digit order. This reinforces that the left digit represents tens and the right digit represents ones, even if place value is not yet formally taught. It also practices the fine motor skills needed for all future arithmetic.

## How It's Best Learned
Practice writing numbers in sequence on number lines. Pair with ten frames so children see the quantity alongside the numeral.

## Common Misconceptions
- Writing digits in wrong order (e.g., 41 for 14).
- Adding extra strokes or starting from the wrong point on the numeral.

## Explainer

You can already write the numerals 1 through 10 and recognize the symbols for 11 through 20. Now you're combining both skills: writing those two-digit symbols yourself. Each numeral from 11 to 20 is made of two digits written side by side, and the order matters — 14 is different from 41, even though both use the same two digits. Getting the order right every time is the central challenge of this topic.

The most important habit to build is: write the **left digit first**, then the **right digit**. All the teen numbers (11–19) start with the digit 1 on the left. That left 1 means "one group of ten," though you don't need to think about it in those terms yet. What matters now is forming the motor habit — write 1, then write the ones digit beside it. For 13, you write "1" then "3." For 17, you write "1" then "7." The digits themselves are ones you already know how to form from writing 1–10. The new skill is combining two familiar digits into one two-digit numeral, keeping them close together and in the right sequence.

A helpful pairing is writing numerals alongside **ten frames** — grids with two rows of five boxes. For 13, you see one completely filled frame (ten dots) and three extra dots. The numeral 13 and the picture belong together: the 1 represents the full frame and the 3 represents the extras. Seeing the quantity while writing the symbol strengthens the connection between the numeral as a written shape and the amount it stands for. This connection matters because numerals are not just art — they are symbols that carry meaning, and that meaning is the quantity.

Accurate numeral formation now pays off in every arithmetic task that follows. When you write an addition problem, a measurement, or a number line, each symbol must be legible and correctly formed. A sloppy 1 that looks like a 7, or digits written in reversed order, leads to wrong calculations later. Writing slowly and carefully — using dotted guides or a number line to check — is worth the effort. These symbols are the vocabulary of all future mathematics, and getting them right from the start is the foundation everything else builds on.
