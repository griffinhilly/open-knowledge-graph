# Fact Family Relationships

## Core Idea
A fact family consists of four related equations using the same three numbers. For example, with numbers 3, 5, and 8: 3 + 5 = 8, 5 + 3 = 8, 8 - 3 = 5, and 8 - 5 = 3. Seeing these relationships helps students understand the inverse relationship between addition and subtraction.

## How It's Best Learned
Organize equations visually using triangles or diagrams that show the three numbers and their relationships. Create fact families for numbers students already know well.

## Common Misconceptions
- Seeing each equation as isolated rather than related.
- Not understanding why all four facts are considered part of one family.

## Explainer

You already know that addition and subtraction are opposite operations — addition puts groups together, and subtraction takes part of a group away. A fact family is what you get when you take three numbers that work together and write down all four equations you can make with them. Understanding why all four belong together is the key insight.

Let's use the numbers 3, 5, and 8. If you have 3 apples and someone gives you 5 more, you end up with 8 apples: **3 + 5 = 8**. If you start with the 5 apples first and get 3 more, you still end up with 8: **5 + 3 = 8**. Now flip it: if you have 8 apples and give away 5, you have 3 left: **8 − 5 = 3**. And if you give away 3, you have 5 left: **8 − 3 = 5**. Four equations, same three numbers — that's the family. The two addition facts and two subtraction facts all belong together because they are describing the same relationship between 3, 5, and 8.

A helpful way to see this is to think of a triangle with one number on top and two numbers on the bottom. Put 8 at the top, and 3 and 5 on the bottom. The top number is always the biggest — it is the whole group. The two bottom numbers are the parts. When you add the parts, you get the whole. When you subtract one part from the whole, you get the other part. The triangle shows you all four facts at once, because the relationship between the three numbers works in all four directions.

Why does this matter? Because knowing one fact for free gives you the others. If you know 6 + 7 = 13, you automatically know 7 + 6 = 13, 13 − 7 = 6, and 13 − 6 = 7. You don't have to memorize four separate facts — you just have to understand the family they belong to. This is one of the most powerful shortcuts in all of early math: three numbers, one relationship, four facts.
