# Fact Family Relationships

## Core Idea
A fact family consists of four related equations using the same three numbers. For example, with numbers 3, 5, and 8: 3 + 5 = 8, 5 + 3 = 8, 8 - 3 = 5, and 8 - 5 = 3. Seeing these relationships helps students understand the inverse relationship between addition and subtraction.

## How It's Best Learned
Organize equations visually using triangles or diagrams that show the three numbers and their relationships. Create fact families for numbers students already know well.

## Common Misconceptions
- Seeing each equation as isolated rather than related.
- Not understanding why all four facts are considered part of one family.

  type: multiple-choice
  options:
    - "Because addition and subtraction always produce numbers in the same family"
    - "Because 9, 4, and 13 have a part-whole relationship that works in all four directions"
    - "Because 13 is always connected to 9 in the times tables"
    - "Because you can always add 3 to find the next fact"
  answer: 1
  explanation: "The three numbers 9, 4, and 13 form one relationship: 9 and 4 are parts, 13 is the whole. That single relationship can be expressed four ways: 9+4=13, 4+9=13, 13-9=4, and 13-4=9. This is the essence of a fact family — one relationship, four facts. The student doesn't need to memorize each separately; understanding the part-whole structure gives them all four."

- question: "In a fact family, the largest number is always the result of adding the other two numbers together."
  type: true-false
  answer: true
  explanation: "The largest number in any fact family is the whole — the sum of the two parts. For example, in the family 3, 5, 8: 3+5=8 and 5+3=8. When you subtract, you always subtract a smaller number (a part) from the largest number (the whole). This is why the top number on the fact-family triangle is always the whole — it is always the largest of the three."

- question: "Every fact family has exactly four different equations."
  type: true-false
  answer: false
  explanation: "Most fact families have four equations, but when the two parts are equal (like 5, 5, and 10), the two addition facts are identical (5+5=10 and 5+5=10) and the two subtraction facts are identical (10-5=5 and 10-5=5). This gives only two unique equations, not four. When the whole is formed by doubling one part, the family collapses to fewer distinct facts."

- question: "Explain why knowing one fact in a fact family automatically gives you the other three, using the numbers 7, 6, and 13 as an example."
  type: short-answer
  answer: "Because all four facts describe the same part-whole relationship: 7 and 6 are parts, 13 is the whole. Knowing 7+6=13 tells you the parts and the whole, so you can immediately write 6+7=13 (swap the parts), 13-7=6 (remove one part from the whole to get the other), and 13-6=7 (same logic). The relationship is the same in all four directions."
  explanation: "This question targets the core insight: a fact family isn't four separate facts to memorize — it's one relationship written four ways. A student who truly understands this can reconstruct any missing fact from any known fact in the family, rather than treating each equation as an isolated piece of information."

## Explainer

You already know that addition and subtraction are opposite operations — addition puts groups together, and subtraction takes part of a group away. A fact family is what you get when you take three numbers that work together and write down all four equations you can make with them. Understanding why all four belong together is the key insight.

Let's use the numbers 3, 5, and 8. If you have 3 apples and someone gives you 5 more, you end up with 8 apples: **3 + 5 = 8**. If you start with the 5 apples first and get 3 more, you still end up with 8: **5 + 3 = 8**. Now flip it: if you have 8 apples and give away 5, you have 3 left: **8 − 5 = 3**. And if you give away 3, you have 5 left: **8 − 3 = 5**. Four equations, same three numbers — that's the family. The two addition facts and two subtraction facts all belong together because they are describing the same relationship between 3, 5, and 8.

A helpful way to see this is to think of a triangle with one number on top and two numbers on the bottom. Put 8 at the top, and 3 and 5 on the bottom. The top number is always the biggest — it is the whole group. The two bottom numbers are the parts. When you add the parts, you get the whole. When you subtract one part from the whole, you get the other part. The triangle shows you all four facts at once, because the relationship between the three numbers works in all four directions.

Why does this matter? Because knowing one fact for free gives you the others. If you know 6 + 7 = 13, you automatically know 7 + 6 = 13, 13 − 7 = 6, and 13 − 6 = 7. You don't have to memorize four separate facts — you just have to understand the family they belong to. This is one of the most powerful shortcuts in all of early math: three numbers, one relationship, four facts.
