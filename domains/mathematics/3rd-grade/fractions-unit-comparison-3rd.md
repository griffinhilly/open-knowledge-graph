# Comparing Unit Fractions

## Core Idea
Unit fractions have a numerator of 1. As the denominator increases, the unit fraction gets smaller (1/2 > 1/3 > 1/4). Comparing requires understanding that more equal parts means each part is smaller.

## How It's Best Learned
Use pie charts or area models (folded paper, drawings) to show unit fractions visually. Compare side-by-side. Arrange unit fractions on a number line and notice the pattern.

## Common Misconceptions
- Thinking 1/4 is larger than 1/3 because 4 is larger than 3.
- Confusing the denominator with the size of the part (higher denominator = bigger piece).

## Explainer

Building on your earlier work with unit fractions like 1/2, 1/3, and 1/4, you can now compare them with confidence. The central principle is the same one you encountered before: in a unit fraction, the **denominator tells you how many equal parts the whole is divided into**. Since you always get exactly 1 part, more divisions means a smaller piece. The fraction 1/3 gives you one of three equal parts; 1/4 gives you one of four equal parts — and one of four is smaller than one of three.

The most reliable way to build this intuition is with **area models**: draw the same-sized rectangle, divide one into 3 equal columns and another into 4 equal columns, and shade one column in each. The shaded region in the thirds rectangle is visibly bigger. A number line works too — place 1/2, 1/3, and 1/4 between 0 and 1, and you'll see them crowd closer and closer to 0 as denominators grow.

A useful comparison strategy: since both fractions have a numerator of 1, you only need to compare denominators — the fraction with the **smaller denominator is the larger fraction**. This shortcut only works for unit fractions (numerator = 1). For example, 1/5 vs. 1/9: 5 < 9, so 1/5 > 1/9. No drawing needed once the logic is internalized.

This comparison principle is the foundation for all fraction comparison that comes next. When you eventually compare fractions like 3/4 vs. 2/3, you'll use strategies (common denominators, benchmarks) that build directly on understanding how denominator size controls part size. Getting comfortable with the inverse relationship now — "bigger bottom number, smaller fraction" — prevents the persistent misconception that trips up students for years.
