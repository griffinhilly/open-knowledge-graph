# Mental Math Strategies for Subtraction

## Core Idea
Subtraction strategies include: counting back (13 - 2: count 12, 11), counting up (13 - 5: start at 5, count 6, 7, 8, ..., 13), and using related addition facts (13 - 5: think 5 + 8 = 13, so 13 - 5 = 8). These strategies reveal the relationship between addition and subtraction.

## How It's Best Learned
Use a number line to model each strategy. Show how 'counting back' works for subtracting small amounts and 'counting up' works when the difference is small. Connect to addition facts.

## Common Misconceptions
- Counting backward without careful tracking.
- Not recognizing that addition and subtraction are related.
- Using inefficient strategies (like counting back by ones repeatedly).

## Explainer

You already know how to add and subtract tens mentally — 70 − 30 = 40 without hesitation. You also know that subtraction within 100 is possible, though it can take effort. Now you are learning to make that process faster and more reliable by choosing the right strategy for each problem, rather than using the same approach every time.

The first strategy is **counting back**. It works best when you are subtracting a small number. For 13 − 3, count back three steps: 12, 11, 10. Done. But counting back gets clumsy for larger subtractions. If you tried 13 − 9 by counting back nine steps — 12, 11, 10, 9, 8, 7, 6, 5, 4 — you would need to track nine steps carefully, with plenty of room for error.

That's when **counting up** is smarter. Instead of going backward from 13, start at 9 and count forward until you reach 13: 10, 11, 12, 13 — only four steps. The gap between 9 and 13 is 4, so 13 − 9 = 4. This is exactly the strategy cashiers use when making change: rather than computing 20 − 13, they count from 13 up to 20. Counting up is especially efficient when the two numbers are close together.

The most powerful strategy of all is **think addition**. You already know that 5 + 8 = 13 from your addition facts. So 13 − 5 is simply asking "5 + what = 13?" and the answer appears immediately — 8 — with no counting required at all. This works because addition and subtraction are mirror images of each other: every addition fact has a subtraction fact hiding inside it. Skilled mathematicians rarely think about subtraction directly; they reframe it as addition whenever they can, because addition facts are usually more automatic.
