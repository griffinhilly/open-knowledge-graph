# Introduction to Fractions

## Core Idea
A fraction represents a part of a whole that has been divided into equal parts. In the fraction 3/4, the denominator (4) tells how many equal parts the whole is divided into, and the numerator (3) tells how many of those parts we have. Fractions also represent division (3/4 = 3 divided by 4) and points on a number line. Understanding that fractions are numbers -- not just "parts of pizzas" -- is a critical conceptual shift. Students must grasp that the parts must be equal in size, that the whole matters (1/2 of a small pizza is not the same amount as 1/2 of a large pizza), and that fractions can be greater than 1.

## How It's Best Learned
Begin with physical partitioning: folding paper strips, sharing food equally, shading regions. Use multiple models (area models, set models, number lines) so students do not over-rely on one representation. Emphasize equal partitioning -- show non-examples where parts are unequal. Introduce fraction notation only after the concept of equal sharing is solid.

## Common Misconceptions
- Thinking the denominator counts shaded parts and the numerator counts unshaded parts (or vice versa).
- Not requiring equal-sized parts (accepting a shape split into unequal pieces as showing "thirds").
- Believing fractions are always less than 1.
- Treating the numerator and denominator as two separate whole numbers rather than a single quantity.
