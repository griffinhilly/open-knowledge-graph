# Histograms and Frequency Distributions

## Core Idea
A frequency distribution organizes quantitative data into class intervals (bins) and records how many observations fall in each. A histogram is the corresponding bar chart where bar heights represent frequencies (or relative frequencies) and bars share edges — reflecting the continuous nature of the data. The shape of a histogram reveals key distribution features: symmetry, skewness, modality, and the presence of outliers.

## How It's Best Learned
Have students build histograms from raw data by hand, choosing bin widths and justifying their choices. Then use software (Desmos, GeoGebra, or Excel) to explore how bin width changes the apparent shape. Introduce vocabulary: left-skewed, right-skewed, unimodal, bimodal.

## Common Misconceptions
- Confusing histograms with bar charts: histograms have no gaps (continuous data); bar charts have gaps (categorical data).
- Thinking skewness direction is where the hump is — it is where the tail points.
- Ignoring the effect of bin width on distribution shape.
