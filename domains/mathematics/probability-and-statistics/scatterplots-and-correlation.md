# Scatterplots and Correlation

## Core Idea
A scatterplot displays pairs of quantitative measurements (x, y) as points on a coordinate plane, revealing the relationship between two variables. An association can be described by its direction (positive or negative), form (linear or curved), and strength (strong, moderate, or weak). Correlation describes linear association — a strong linear trend means points cluster near a line — but correlation does not imply causation.

## How It's Best Learned
Use real datasets with genuine interest (height vs. shoe size, study hours vs. GPA). Have students describe associations before any formula is introduced. Explicitly show examples of strong association with zero correlation (curved patterns) and spurious correlations to build intuition about the causation distinction.

## Common Misconceptions
- Correlation implies causation: a classic and persistent error.
- Confusing an outlier in a scatterplot (an influential point) with one in a single distribution.
- Assuming 'no linear correlation' means 'no relationship' — the relationship could be nonlinear.
