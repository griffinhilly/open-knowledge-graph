# Coulometric Titration

## Core Idea
Coulometric titration generates a titrant electrochemically in situ rather than dispensing it from a buret, using Faraday's law (Q = nFN) to calculate the exact amount of reagent produced from the measured charge. The technique requires 100% current efficiency — every electron must produce the intended reagent and nothing else. A constant current is applied, and the endpoint is detected by a conventional indicator (potentiometric, amperometric, or colorimetric). Because the "titrant" is produced from electrons (whose flow is measured with extreme precision), coulometric titration is an absolute method requiring no standardized solutions. The Karl Fischer titration for water determination is the most commercially important example, where iodine is electrogenerated to react with water stoichiometrically.

## How It's Best Learned
Perform a coulometric titration of an acid with electrogenerated OH⁻ (from water reduction at a platinum cathode), monitoring pH to detect the endpoint. Calculate the moles of analyte from Q = It and Faraday's law, and compare with the known amount to verify 100% current efficiency and understand why side reactions must be absent.

## Common Misconceptions
- Coulometric titration does not require a standard solution — this is its chief advantage over volumetric titration, because the 'titrant' amount is determined purely from electrical measurements and Faraday's constant.
- The requirement for 100% current efficiency is not merely aspirational; if side reactions consume even a few percent of the current, the calculated analyte amount will be systematically too high, and special cell designs and electrolyte compositions are chosen to ensure efficiency.
