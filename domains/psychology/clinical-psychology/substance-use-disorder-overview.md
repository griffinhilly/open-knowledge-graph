# Substance Use Disorders: Overview and Classification

## Core Idea
Substance Use Disorders involve a maladaptive pattern of substance use leading to clinically significant impairment, characterized by tolerance, withdrawal, loss of control, and continued use despite negative consequences. SUDs vary by substance class (alcohol, opioids, stimulants, hallucinogens, cannabis) and severity. The biopsychosocial model integrates genetic vulnerability, learned associations with drug cues, and environmental contingencies.

## Explainer

You know from psychopharmacology basics that psychoactive drugs work by mimicking, blocking, or modulating neurotransmitter systems, and from the dopamine system that dopamine is central to reward and motivation. Substance Use Disorders can be understood as what happens when drugs repeatedly hijack these systems: the brain adapts to the drug's presence in ways that make normal functioning impossible without it, while simultaneously degrading the motivational circuitry that governs voluntary decision-making.

The two pharmacological hallmarks of physical dependence are **tolerance** and **withdrawal**. Tolerance is the progressive reduction in drug effect with repeated use — it takes more substance to achieve the same effect. The cellular mechanism varies by drug class but generally involves receptor downregulation, reduced receptor sensitivity, or compensatory changes in downstream signaling. **Withdrawal** is the mirror image: when the drug is removed, the compensatory changes produce symptoms opposite to the drug's effects. Opioids, for example, produce analgesia and euphoria acutely; opioid withdrawal produces pain hypersensitivity and profound dysphoria. Alcohol produces CNS depression; alcohol withdrawal produces CNS hyperexcitability — which can be life-threatening (seizures, delirium tremens). Understanding withdrawal helps explain why people continue use despite wanting to stop: withdrawal is powerfully aversive, and using again immediately terminates it (negative reinforcement).

But tolerance and withdrawal are only part of the story. The DSM-5 conceptualization of SUDs is broader, organized around eleven criteria across four domains: impaired control (using more than intended, failed attempts to cut down, excessive time spent obtaining/using/recovering, craving), social impairment (failure to meet obligations, interpersonal problems, giving up activities), risky use (use in hazardous situations, continued use despite physical/psychological harm), and pharmacological criteria (tolerance, withdrawal). Severity is determined by how many criteria are met: 2–3 is mild, 4–5 is moderate, 6+ is severe. This dimensional approach replaced the older distinction between "abuse" and "dependence" because the evidence supported a spectrum model.

The **dopamine prediction error** mechanism illuminates why SUDs are so persistent. Drugs of abuse — whether opioids, stimulants, alcohol, or nicotine — all cause supraphysiological dopamine release in the nucleus accumbens, far beyond what any natural reward produces. The dopamine system encodes the difference between expected and received reward; drugs overwhelm this system and are encoded as extraordinarily valuable. Over time, the system remodels: natural rewards produce less dopamine response (they feel flat), while drug cues — the environment, people, and objects associated with using — trigger strong dopamine anticipation and craving even before any drug is consumed. This is why relapse is triggered most powerfully by cues, not by the drug itself, and why treatment must address the **cue-conditioned learning** that underlies craving alongside the pharmacological dependence.
