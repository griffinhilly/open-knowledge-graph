# Substance Use Disorders: Overview and Neurobiology

## Core Idea
Substance Use Disorders involve problematic use patterns leading to clinical impairment and compulsive use despite consequences. Modern understanding emphasizes dopamine dysregulation, prefrontal cortex dysfunction, and the transition from recreational to compulsive use.

## Explainer

From your study of the DSM-5 framework, you know that diagnostic criteria are organized around clusters of symptoms with defined severity thresholds. From your prerequisite on the dopamine reward system, you know that dopamine signals expected and unexpected rewards, reinforcing behaviors that generated them. **Substance Use Disorder (SUD)** is best understood as what happens when a substance directly hijacks this reward architecture — bypassing the natural contingencies that normally calibrate the system.

Most addictive substances either release dopamine directly into the **nucleus accumbens** (stimulants), or disinhibit dopamine neurons by blocking inhibitory interneurons (alcohol, opioids, cannabis). The dopamine surge produced by drugs is typically larger, faster, and more reliable than anything naturally rewarding behaviors produce. This floods the reward system with an unconditioned signal, and the brain responds by scaling back — reducing receptor density, decreasing dopamine synthesis — in what is called **neuroadaptation** or tolerance. Now the baseline reward signal for everyday experiences (food, social connection, accomplishment) is blunted, and the substance becomes necessary just to feel normal. This is the neurobiological substrate of **withdrawal** and the powerful pull to use.

The DSM-5 organizes SUD criteria into four domains: **impaired control** (using more than intended, failed quit attempts, craving), **social impairment** (role failure, interpersonal conflict due to use, giving up activities), **risky use** (use in hazardous situations, continued use despite knowing consequences), and **pharmacological tolerance and withdrawal**. Two or more criteria across these domains in twelve months constitutes a disorder; severity (mild/moderate/severe) is graded by count. The criteria apply across substance classes — alcohol, opioids, stimulants, cannabis, sedatives — with class-specific variations in the withdrawal profiles.

The critical theoretical distinction is between **impulsive use** (early-stage: driven by reward-seeking) and **compulsive use** (later-stage: driven by relief from withdrawal and habit). Early in addiction, the orbitofrontal cortex and ventral striatum dominate — the drug is genuinely rewarding. As use escalates, the dorsal striatum takes over, encoding drug-seeking as a habit independent of pleasure, and the prefrontal cortex loses regulatory capacity. This shift explains why severe SUD persists even when the person reports getting no enjoyment from use. Treatment approaches must target both stages: early motivational work addresses impulsive reward-seeking; later maintenance work targets the compulsive habit circuits and rebuilds prefrontal regulatory capacity through structure, behavioral activation, and in some cases pharmacotherapy.
