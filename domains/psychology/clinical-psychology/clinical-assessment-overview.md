# Clinical Assessment and Diagnosis

## Core Idea
Clinical assessment integrates information from interviews, tests, observations, and collateral sources to understand a client's presenting problems, history, and functioning. Reliable assessment requires understanding measurement principles and clinician bias; assessment guides diagnosis but is not diagnosis itself. Validity depends on instrument selection, interpretation accuracy, and integration across methods.

## Explainer

Clinical assessment is the systematic process of gathering information about a client in order to understand who they are, what problems they are experiencing, and how those problems developed. It is the investigative phase of clinical work — preceding treatment planning and informing diagnosis. Unlike an informal conversation, formal assessment uses structured methods designed to produce information that is reliable (consistent across raters or occasions) and valid (actually measuring what it claims to measure).

The core methods of clinical assessment form a complementary set. Clinical interviews — structured, semi-structured, or unstructured — allow the clinician to hear the client's own account, observe affect and interpersonal style, and explore history in flexible depth. Standardized tests and questionnaires (intelligence tests, personality inventories, symptom measures) provide quantitative data referenced against normative samples. Behavioral observations capture how the client actually behaves in a setting, rather than how they report behaving. Collateral information from family members, medical records, or prior treatment providers adds context and external perspective. No single method is sufficient; each captures different things and each has characteristic blind spots.

A central concept is the distinction between assessment and diagnosis. Assessment generates findings — a pattern of scores, observations, and history. Diagnosis is a clinical judgment that names and categorizes the client's condition using a system like the DSM-5. That judgment requires more than reading numbers off a test: it requires integrating imperfect evidence, ruling out alternative explanations (including medical causes), accounting for cultural context, and applying diagnostic criteria that were themselves developed with their own limitations. Good assessment informs that judgment; it does not replace it.

Reliability and validity matter throughout. A measure with poor reliability introduces noise into every conclusion drawn from it. An instrument validated only on one population may be misleading when used with another. Clinician bias — the tendency to seek evidence that confirms an initial impression — is as much a threat to valid assessment as a poorly designed instrument. Best practice requires selecting instruments appropriate to the referral question, interpreting within norms, cross-checking findings across methods, and remaining open to revising hypotheses when evidence conflicts.

Assessment is not a neutral or purely technical exercise. It occurs in relationship — how a client experiences the assessment process affects their performance — and it carries real consequences for how they are understood and treated. Competent clinical assessment requires both psychometric literacy (understanding what tests measure and how accurately) and clinical wisdom (knowing how to weigh, integrate, and communicate findings responsibly).
