# Post-Traumatic Stress Disorder (PTSD)

## Core Idea
PTSD develops after exposure to a traumatic event and involves re-experiencing (flashbacks, nightmares), avoidance of reminders, negative mood/cognition changes, and hyperarousal. The amygdala-mediated fear response becomes conditioned to trauma cues, while the hippocampus and prefrontal cortex fail to properly contextualize and extinction-learn the memory. Complex trauma from repeated interpersonal violence presents with additional features affecting identity and relationships.

## Explainer

From your study of fear conditioning circuits, you know that the amygdala encodes threat associations with exceptional speed and durability. A stimulus that reliably predicts danger — a sound, a smell, a physical sensation — gets wired to the fear response through a process that is both fast and resistant to simple forgetting. This is adaptive in most contexts: remembering what nearly killed you is survival-relevant. PTSD represents a case where this fundamentally healthy system gets stuck. The trauma memory does not integrate into ordinary autobiographical memory; instead, it remains hyperaccessible, emotionally charged, and insufficiently contextualized as past rather than present.

The four symptom clusters of PTSD reflect distinct underlying mechanisms. **Re-experiencing symptoms** — intrusive memories, flashbacks, trauma-related nightmares — reflect the amygdala's continued treatment of trauma cues as ongoing threats. A flashback is not merely a vivid memory; neuroimaging suggests it recruits brain regions associated with actual sensory experience rather than just recollection, which is why survivors describe them as "reliving" rather than "remembering." **Avoidance** is the behavioral response to this: if trauma reminders trigger intense distress, the natural adaptation is to avoid those reminders — places, people, thoughts, conversations. This avoidance is self-maintaining: it prevents the exposure that would be needed for the fear memory to extinguish. **Negative cognitions and mood** reflect changes in beliefs about self, world, and others that often generalize beyond the trauma itself ("I am permanently damaged," "nowhere is safe"). **Hyperarousal** — sleep disturbance, irritability, exaggerated startle, hypervigilance — reflects the autonomic nervous system remaining in a chronic threat-detection state.

The neurobiology integrates what you know about limbic circuitry. The hippocampus normally provides temporal and contextual tags to memories: this happened there, then, under those conditions. When hippocampal function is compromised — as it appears to be in PTSD, possibly due to glucocorticoid-mediated damage from chronic stress — trauma memories lose their contextual grounding. The prefrontal cortex, which normally inhibits amygdala reactivity through top-down control, also shows reduced regulatory capacity in PTSD. The result is an amygdala that keeps firing, a hippocampus that cannot say "this is past," and a prefrontal cortex that cannot say "stand down." Evidence-based treatments for PTSD — Prolonged Exposure (PE) and Cognitive Processing Therapy (CPT) — work precisely by restoring these regulatory processes: PE creates new extinction learning through repeated non-reinforced exposure to trauma cues; CPT targets the distorted cognitions that block natural recovery.

**Complex PTSD** arises from repeated, prolonged interpersonal trauma, especially in childhood or captivity, where escape was impossible. In addition to the four standard clusters, complex presentations involve disturbances in self-organization: persistent shame and a sense of being permanently different from others, difficulty regulating affect, disrupted patterns of relating to others, and a fragmented sense of identity. These features reflect not just a conditioned fear memory but deeper disruptions in the developmental scaffolding of self and attachment. This distinction has treatment implications: approaches that work well for single-incident PTSD may need significant modification for complex presentations, which often require stabilization and relational safety before trauma-focused work can proceed effectively.
