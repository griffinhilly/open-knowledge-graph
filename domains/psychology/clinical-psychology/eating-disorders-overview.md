# Eating Disorders: Overview and Classification

## Core Idea
Eating Disorders (Anorexia Nervosa, Bulimia Nervosa, Binge-Eating Disorder) involve maladaptive eating behaviors and related cognitions (body image disturbance, overvaluation of weight/shape control). They have the highest mortality rate of any psychiatric disorder and involve significant medical complications. Etiology includes genetic predisposition, temperamental factors (perfectionism, neuroticism), and sociocultural pressures around thinness and body ideals.

## Explainer

What distinguishes an eating disorder from a diet or even an unhealthy relationship with food is the presence of clinically significant **body image disturbance** — a persistent, ego-syntonic distortion in how one perceives, evaluates, and experiences one's own body, combined with an overvaluation of weight and shape as the primary measure of self-worth. From your clinical assessment background, you know that diagnosis requires both behavioral criteria and associated distress or impairment. In eating disorders, the cognitive disturbance often precedes and outlasts the behavioral symptoms, which is why they are more accurately understood as disorders of thinking and self-concept expressed through eating behavior.

The three major categories carve different profiles from this shared cognitive core. **Anorexia Nervosa (AN)** is defined by caloric restriction producing significantly low body weight, intense fear of weight gain, and disturbed body perception (feeling fat despite emaciation). There are two subtypes: the restricting type (pure caloric restriction) and the binge-purge type (restriction punctuated by binge-purge episodes). **Bulimia Nervosa (BN)** involves recurrent binge episodes — eating objectively large amounts in a discrete time period with loss of control — followed by compensatory behaviors (purging, fasting, excessive exercise). Unlike AN, individuals with BN are typically at normal weight, making the disorder less visible. **Binge-Eating Disorder (BED)**, the most prevalent of the three, involves recurrent binge episodes without regular compensatory behavior, leading to distress and frequently to obesity, but it is distinct from obesity itself because the defining feature is the episodic loss of control, not weight.

These disorders carry the highest mortality rate of any psychiatric diagnosis. In AN, death results from both the direct effects of malnutrition (cardiac arrhythmias from electrolyte imbalance, bone density loss, multi-organ failure) and suicide. In BN, repeated purging causes hypokalemia (low potassium), esophageal damage, and dental erosion. Understanding the medical complications matters clinically because physical stabilization often precedes psychological treatment — a severely malnourished patient cannot engage in meaningful cognitive work, and refeeding protocols require medical supervision. This places eating disorder treatment in a medical-psychological interface that most other psychiatric conditions do not require.

Etiology is best understood as **diathesis-stress**: a vulnerability-activation model where genetic predisposition interacts with environmental triggers. The heritability of AN and BN is substantial (50–80%), and twin studies implicate genes affecting serotonin regulation, anxiety sensitivity, and reward processing. Temperamentally, high perfectionism and neuroticism are robust risk factors — these traits make individuals especially sensitive to perceived failure and self-critical when falling short of ideals. Sociocultural pressure toward thinness provides the environmental scaffold: it directs perfectionism toward weight and body control as a domain of achievement. This is why eating disorders cluster in populations exposed to weight-focused cultural messages (athletes, dancers, fashion-industry workers) and show cross-cultural variation linked to Westernized media exposure. No single factor is sufficient; etiology requires the convergence of vulnerability, temperament, and cultural context.
