# Health Disparities, Equity Frameworks, and Social Determinants

## Core Idea
Health disparities arise from structural inequities in income, education, housing, environmental quality, and healthcare access. These social determinants shape disease burden and outcomes more powerfully than individual behaviors. Public health frameworks distinguish between equity (fair distribution of health) and equality (equal treatment), and emphasize upstream structural changes rather than downstream individual interventions.

## How It's Best Learned
Compare health outcomes (mortality, morbidity) across racial/ethnic groups, socioeconomic strata, and geographic areas, then trace disparities back to underlying structural factors. Examine how policies (housing, transportation, economic) shape health outcomes.

## Common Misconceptions
- Disparities reflect individual lifestyle differences; they reflect historical and ongoing structural discrimination embedded in institutions.
- Addressing disparities requires only culturally tailored programs; without structural change, individual programs have limited impact.

## Explainer

From your study of social determinants of health, you have a map of the forces that shape health outside clinical settings: the neighborhood you live in, the school you attended, your income and job security, your exposure to environmental hazards, and whether you have reliable housing. Health disparities are the observable downstream consequence of these forces being distributed unequally across social groups defined by race, ethnicity, income, geography, gender, or disability status. A **health disparity** is a measurable difference in health outcomes between groups that is systematic, avoidable, and unjust—not merely a biological variation. The distinction matters because it implies a remedy: if a disparity is systematic and avoidable, it can in principle be addressed through policy and structural change.

The conceptual distinction between **equality** and **equity** is foundational to public health frameworks. Equality means giving everyone the same thing. Equity means giving people what they need to reach the same outcome—which may require giving more to those who start from a more disadvantaged position. A widely used analogy: three people of different heights trying to watch a game over a fence. Giving all three the same-height box (equality) still leaves the shortest person unable to see. Giving each person a box sized to their need (equity) allows all three to see the game. In health terms, providing identical clinical services to populations with vastly different exposures, resources, and stressors may produce equal inputs but inequitable outcomes. Equity frameworks explicitly ask: who is being left behind, and what structural supports would close that gap?

Public health frameworks organize the causes of disparities into **upstream** (fundamental) and **downstream** (proximate) determinants. Downstream interventions target behaviors or clinical risk factors—smoking cessation programs, diabetes management clinics, cancer screening campaigns. These are important but limited: they address the person after the harm has largely been set in motion. Upstream interventions target the structural conditions that make certain behaviors more likely and certain exposures unavoidable—zoning laws that determine where polluting industries are sited, school funding formulas linked to local property tax bases, minimum wage policies, criminal justice policies that remove breadwinners from households. The **life-course perspective** extends this upstream thinking: health disparities compound over time. Poor nutrition in utero affects fetal development; early childhood adversity alters the hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenal axis and immune function; educational inequity limits adult economic opportunity; cumulative disadvantage produces dramatically different health trajectories by midlife. A Black American's higher rate of cardiovascular mortality at age 55 cannot be separated from decades of differential exposure to stress, environmental toxins, food insecurity, and barriers to preventive care.

Measuring disparities requires choosing the reference group and the metric carefully. Absolute disparities (e.g., 200 additional deaths per 100,000) convey scale; relative disparities (e.g., 2.5× the mortality rate) convey the magnitude of unfairness. Rates that converge in absolute terms may still show persistent relative differences, or vice versa. **Intersectionality**—the framework recognizing that race, gender, class, sexuality, and disability interact to produce compounded disadvantage not reducible to any single axis—is increasingly incorporated into disparity analysis, because a low-income Black woman faces a different risk profile than the combination of race, gender, and income risks modeled separately would predict. Equity-focused public health practice therefore requires disaggregating data by multiple social dimensions simultaneously, rather than examining race or income in isolation.
