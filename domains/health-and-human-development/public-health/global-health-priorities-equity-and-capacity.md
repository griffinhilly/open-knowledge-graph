# Global Health Priorities, Equity, and Health System Capacity

## Core Idea
Global health priorities reflect disease burden, but also equity (focusing on health of most disadvantaged), capacity (health system infrastructure varies 100-fold across countries), and sustainability (dependence on external funding limits long-term progress). Effective global health strategies build health system capacity, strengthen local research and leadership, and address underlying social determinants.

## How It's Best Learned
Compare disease burden, health system capacity, and existing health programs across contrasting countries (e.g., Sub-Saharan Africa vs. Southeast Asia), identifying how burden and capacity shape priority-setting.

## Common Misconceptions
- Disease burden alone determines priorities; political will, existing capacity, and funding mechanisms are equally important.
- Vertical disease-specific programs are most efficient; they often bypass fragile health systems and fail to build capacity for future health challenges.

## Explainer

From your study of the global burden of disease, you have a quantitative map of what is killing and disabling people worldwide: the shift from communicable diseases toward non-communicable diseases in middle-income countries, the persistent burden of diarrheal disease and pneumonia in low-income settings, the enormous toll of maternal and neonatal mortality in Sub-Saharan Africa and South Asia. That burden analysis answers the question "where is the suffering?" but it does not directly answer "where should we direct resources?" — because burden, equity, capacity, and sustainability pull in different directions, and global health priority-setting requires weighing all four.

**Equity** introduces a normative dimension that raw DALYs cannot capture. A disease causing 10,000 deaths per year among the poorest quintile of a country may warrant more attention than one causing 50,000 deaths in the wealthiest quintile, if the former is entirely preventable with cheap interventions while the latter is already receiving substantial investment. Health equity frameworks distinguish **horizontal equity** (equal treatment for equal need) from **vertical equity** (greater effort for greater need) and argue that global health resources should weight disproportionate disadvantage — addressing conditions that cluster among those with the least access to care and the least political voice. Without an equity lens, burden-based allocation risks concentrating resources where the infrastructure already exists to use them effectively, deepening rather than closing global health gaps.

**Health system capacity** is perhaps the most underappreciated constraint. A country may have a disease burden that justifies a particular intervention, but if it lacks the trained health workers, cold chains, diagnostic laboratories, drug supply chains, and functioning referral systems to deliver that intervention, the burden analysis tells us little about what is actually achievable. Capacity varies roughly 100-fold across countries — in physician density, hospital beds per capita, and public health laboratory infrastructure. This variation explains why a treatment that dramatically reduces mortality in a well-resourced clinical trial may have limited real-world impact in low-income settings where it cannot reach patients reliably. Effective global health strategies must therefore invest in health system strengthening — training local health workers, building supply chains, supporting data systems — not just in developing better treatments.

The tension between **vertical** and **horizontal** programs crystallizes these trade-offs. **Vertical programs** target a single disease: PEPFAR for HIV, the Global Polio Eradication Initiative, mass drug administration for malaria. They can be very effective at reducing burden for that specific disease, and they can achieve measurable, fundable outcomes. But they often compete with general health systems for skilled personnel, creating "brain drains" within countries as nurses and lab technicians are hired away from primary care by better-paid disease-specific programs. They may also distort national health priorities toward the diseases that attract donor funding rather than toward what most burdens the local population. **Horizontal approaches** — investing in primary health care, training community health workers, strengthening district hospitals — build general capacity that benefits multiple diseases simultaneously, but they produce diffuse outcomes that are harder to attribute, measure, and sell to donors. The most resilient global health systems typically combine both: using vertical programs as platforms that also build horizontal capacity, while insisting that external funding transitions toward sustainable local financing over time.
