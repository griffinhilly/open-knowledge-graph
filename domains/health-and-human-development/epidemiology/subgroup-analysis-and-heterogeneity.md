# Subgroup Analysis and Treatment Heterogeneity

## Core Idea
Subgroup analysis investigates whether treatment effects vary across population subsets (age, sex, disease severity, genetic markers). Genuine treatment heterogeneity reflects differential causal effects; apparent heterogeneity may result from chance, multiple comparisons, or bias. Meta-regression and individual patient data (IPD) meta-analysis test heterogeneity more rigorously than separate subgroup analyses. Prespecification prevents false discoveries; testing too many subgroups inflates Type I error even with Bonferroni adjustment.

## How It's Best Learned
Perform IPD meta-analysis testing treatment × subgroup interactions using appropriate statistical tests; assess statistical heterogeneity with I².

## Common Misconceptions
Lack of statistical significance means homogeneity of effects (insufficient power). Stratified subgroup analyses are valid without prespecification.

- question: "A clinical trial reports that the drug is effective in men (p = 0.02) but not in women (p = 0.18). The authors conclude there is evidence of sex-based treatment heterogeneity. What is the critical flaw in this reasoning?"
  type: multiple-choice
  options:
    - "The analysis should have used Bonferroni correction to adjust for two comparisons"
    - "Differing significance levels within subgroups are not evidence of heterogeneity — only a formal treatment × subgroup interaction test is valid evidence of differential effects"
    - "The result in women should be considered significant at a relaxed threshold given the smaller sample size"
    - "The conclusion is valid because a significant result in men and a non-significant result in women demonstrates a clear difference"
  answer: 1
  explanation: "This is the most common error in subgroup reporting. A non-significant result in women may simply reflect lower statistical power, not a genuinely smaller effect. The effect sizes could be nearly identical. The correct approach is to fit a model with a treatment × sex interaction term and test whether that interaction term is statistically significant. Comparing p-values across separate subgroup analyses — 'significant here, not there' — is not a valid test of heterogeneity."

- question: "Researchers conduct a meta-analysis and obtain individual patient data (IPD) from 15 trials to test whether age modifies the treatment effect. Why is this preferable to comparing the subgroup results across published trial reports?"
  type: multiple-choice
  options:
    - "IPD allows researchers to change the primary outcome to one where heterogeneity is more likely"
    - "IPD enables a patient-level interaction test, avoiding ecological bias and providing the statistical power that study-level comparisons of averages cannot"
    - "IPD meta-analysis is required by regulatory agencies for subgroup analysis"
    - "Published trial reports suppress subgroup findings, so IPD is the only way to access them"
  answer: 1
  explanation: "Comparing subgroup findings across trials confuses patient-level variation with study-level variation (ecological bias). A study with more elderly patients might show a lower average effect, but that doesn't mean elderly patients in every study respond differently. IPD analysis fits a single model at the patient level, allowing a properly powered test of whether age modifies treatment effect within each study and pooled across all studies. The unit of analysis is the patient, not the trial."

- question: "Prespecifying subgroup hypotheses before data analysis is essential not merely because it limits the number of tests, but because post-hoc subgroup findings are generated by a process that violates the assumptions underlying p-values."
  type: true-false
  answer: true
  explanation: "P-values assume you fixed your hypothesis before seeing the data. When you look at data, notice a pattern, and then test it, the p-value is no longer calibrated — the probability of a false positive is far higher than the nominal alpha level suggests. Bonferroni can partially correct for testing many prespecified hypotheses, but it cannot rescue a p-value derived from data-snooping, because the number of potential patterns examined before testing is unknown and uncorrected."

- question: "If a drug shows statistical significance in elderly patients (p = 0.03) but not in younger patients (p = 0.12), this constitutes valid evidence that the drug works better in elderly patients."
  type: true-false
  answer: false
  explanation: "These p-values do not constitute valid evidence of differential effects. The elderly subgroup may have been larger or have had a higher event rate, giving it more power, while the younger subgroup was simply underpowered. The effect sizes might be nearly identical. Valid evidence requires a significant treatment × age interaction term. The p-value comparison across subgroups is the classic error in subgroup analysis and a frequent source of misleading clinical guidance."

- question: "Why is the I² statistic from a standard meta-analysis insufficient for identifying the source of treatment heterogeneity, and what additional analysis is needed?"
  type: short-answer
  answer: "I² quantifies how much of the variance in treatment effects across studies exceeds what chance alone would predict, but it does not explain where that heterogeneity comes from. A high I² tells you effects differ across studies but not whether the cause is differences in patient age, disease severity, dose, or something else. Meta-regression adds study-level covariates to model potential sources, but the sample size is the number of studies (often small), so power is limited. Individual patient data (IPD) meta-analysis is needed to test whether specific patient-level characteristics — rather than study-level averages — are genuine effect modifiers."
  explanation: "I² is a diagnostic, not an explanation. It is the starting point ('effects vary across studies') not the answer ('here is why'). The distinction matters because clinical decisions about precision medicine require knowing which patient characteristics predict differential response, which requires patient-level data and properly powered interaction tests, not summary statistics from published reports."

## Explainer

From your study of effect modification and interaction, you know that an average treatment effect can conceal systematic variation across groups — the drug that works well on average might be useless in elderly patients and highly effective in younger ones. Subgroup analysis is the attempt to find these pockets of differential treatment response. From your study of meta-analysis, you know how to pool estimates across studies. Subgroup analysis and treatment heterogeneity sit at the intersection of these two ideas: investigating whether a treatment's effect varies across patient characteristics, either within a single study or by pooling individual-level data across many studies.

The statistical starting point is the **interaction test** — not separate analyses within each subgroup, but a formal test of whether the treatment effect estimate differs significantly across subgroups. This is a critical distinction. Separate analyses within each subgroup (e.g., "significant in men, not significant in women") are routinely misinterpreted as evidence of heterogeneity when they are not: a nonsignificant result in women may simply reflect lower statistical power, not a genuinely smaller effect. The correct approach is to test the **treatment × subgroup interaction term** in a regression model including the subgroup variable, the treatment variable, and their product. A significant interaction term is the only valid evidence of differential treatment effects across groups.

The **multiple comparisons problem** is severe in subgroup analysis, and it is the reason prespecification is not optional. If you test 10 subgroups post-hoc, you expect roughly one spurious significant finding even if no true heterogeneity exists. **Bonferroni correction** and similar adjustments can be applied, but they are insufficient when the number of potential subgroups is large and correlated. The deeper problem is that post-hoc subgroup findings are often generated by looking at the data, noticing an interesting pattern, and then testing it — which violates the assumptions that p-values rely on. Prespecified hypotheses, registered before data analysis, are the only reliable protection against false discoveries. When you see a paper reporting many subgroup analyses, the key question is: which were prespecified and which were data-driven?

**Individual patient data (IPD) meta-analysis** is the gold standard for studying treatment heterogeneity across studies. Instead of combining summary statistics from each trial, IPD meta-analysis obtains the raw data from each study and fits a single model including patient-level characteristics, allowing properly powered tests of interaction. This avoids **ecological bias** — the error of drawing patient-level conclusions from study-level correlations (a study with more elderly patients might have lower average treatment effect, but that doesn't mean elderly patients within each study respond differently). The **I² statistic** you've encountered in meta-analysis quantifies heterogeneity in treatment effects across studies, but it doesn't explain where heterogeneity comes from. **Meta-regression** adds study-level covariates (mean age, disease severity) to model potential sources, though the sample size is the number of studies, not patients, which sharply limits power. IPD meta-analysis provides the patient-level resolution needed to test whether specific patient characteristics — rather than study-level averages — are true effect modifiers.
