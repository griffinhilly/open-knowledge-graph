# Subgroup Analysis and Treatment Heterogeneity

## Core Idea
Subgroup analysis investigates whether treatment effects vary across population subsets (age, sex, disease severity, genetic markers). Genuine treatment heterogeneity reflects differential causal effects; apparent heterogeneity may result from chance, multiple comparisons, or bias. Meta-regression and individual patient data (IPD) meta-analysis test heterogeneity more rigorously than separate subgroup analyses. Prespecification prevents false discoveries; testing too many subgroups inflates Type I error even with Bonferroni adjustment.

## How It's Best Learned
Perform IPD meta-analysis testing treatment × subgroup interactions using appropriate statistical tests; assess statistical heterogeneity with I².

## Common Misconceptions
Lack of statistical significance means homogeneity of effects (insufficient power). Stratified subgroup analyses are valid without prespecification.

## Explainer

From your study of effect modification and interaction, you know that an average treatment effect can conceal systematic variation across groups — the drug that works well on average might be useless in elderly patients and highly effective in younger ones. Subgroup analysis is the attempt to find these pockets of differential treatment response. From your study of meta-analysis, you know how to pool estimates across studies. Subgroup analysis and treatment heterogeneity sit at the intersection of these two ideas: investigating whether a treatment's effect varies across patient characteristics, either within a single study or by pooling individual-level data across many studies.

The statistical starting point is the **interaction test** — not separate analyses within each subgroup, but a formal test of whether the treatment effect estimate differs significantly across subgroups. This is a critical distinction. Separate analyses within each subgroup (e.g., "significant in men, not significant in women") are routinely misinterpreted as evidence of heterogeneity when they are not: a nonsignificant result in women may simply reflect lower statistical power, not a genuinely smaller effect. The correct approach is to test the **treatment × subgroup interaction term** in a regression model including the subgroup variable, the treatment variable, and their product. A significant interaction term is the only valid evidence of differential treatment effects across groups.

The **multiple comparisons problem** is severe in subgroup analysis, and it is the reason prespecification is not optional. If you test 10 subgroups post-hoc, you expect roughly one spurious significant finding even if no true heterogeneity exists. **Bonferroni correction** and similar adjustments can be applied, but they are insufficient when the number of potential subgroups is large and correlated. The deeper problem is that post-hoc subgroup findings are often generated by looking at the data, noticing an interesting pattern, and then testing it — which violates the assumptions that p-values rely on. Prespecified hypotheses, registered before data analysis, are the only reliable protection against false discoveries. When you see a paper reporting many subgroup analyses, the key question is: which were prespecified and which were data-driven?

**Individual patient data (IPD) meta-analysis** is the gold standard for studying treatment heterogeneity across studies. Instead of combining summary statistics from each trial, IPD meta-analysis obtains the raw data from each study and fits a single model including patient-level characteristics, allowing properly powered tests of interaction. This avoids **ecological bias** — the error of drawing patient-level conclusions from study-level correlations (a study with more elderly patients might have lower average treatment effect, but that doesn't mean elderly patients within each study respond differently). The **I² statistic** you've encountered in meta-analysis quantifies heterogeneity in treatment effects across studies, but it doesn't explain where heterogeneity comes from. **Meta-regression** adds study-level covariates (mean age, disease severity) to model potential sources, though the sample size is the number of studies, not patients, which sharply limits power. IPD meta-analysis provides the patient-level resolution needed to test whether specific patient characteristics — rather than study-level averages — are true effect modifiers.
