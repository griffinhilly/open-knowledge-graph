# Speech Acts and the Nature of Communication

## Core Idea
Communication through language fundamentally involves performing acts—making requests, sharing information, expressing emotions, committing to positions. Understanding communication requires analyzing the acts speakers perform through utterances, not just the propositions they express. This act-based framework unifies diverse phenomena: irony, politeness, indirect requests, and presupposition all become intelligible when viewed through the lens of what a speaker is doing with words.

## Questions

```yaml
- question: "A guest at a dinner party looks pointedly at an open window and says, 'It's quite chilly in here.' The host closes the window. What does speech act theory reveal about this exchange that a purely propositional analysis would miss?"
  type: multiple-choice
  options:
    - "The statement was false, so the communication technically failed"
    - "The utterance performed an indirect request — its illocutionary force was to get the window closed, even though its literal content stated a temperature fact"
    - "The host misunderstood the guest, since the utterance only expressed a proposition about room temperature"
    - "The guest's act was impolite because it did not directly state what was wanted"
  answer: 1
  explanation: "Speech act theory distinguishes what is literally said (the locutionary act) from what the speaker is doing with the utterance (the illocutionary act). The guest stated a fact, but the illocutionary force was a request to close the window. A purely propositional analysis — focused only on whether 'it is cold' is true — would miss the communicative purpose entirely. The host understood not just the proposition but the act being performed. This is exactly why the act-based framework is necessary: many communicative purposes cannot be read off from propositional content alone."

- question: "Why does speech act theory claim that understanding communication requires analyzing what speakers do with words, not just what propositions they express?"
  type: multiple-choice
  options:
    - "Because propositions are always ambiguous and cannot reliably convey meaning"
    - "Because many communicative acts — promises, requests, warnings, apologies — perform actions in the world rather than merely describing states of affairs"
    - "Because grammar rules determine utterance meaning more precisely than propositional content"
    - "Because speakers always use words to do exactly what they literally say"
  answer: 1
  explanation: "When someone says 'I promise to return your book,' the utterance is not just describing a state of affairs — it is creating a commitment. Asking whether the sentence is true or false misses the point. Promises, warnings, apologies, and requests do things in the world rather than picture it. Speech act theory shows that propositional content is one layer of meaning; the illocutionary force (what act is being performed) is often what actually matters. The same proposition can serve completely different communicative purposes depending on context."

- question: "According to speech act theory, the full meaning of an utterance is captured by determining whether the proposition it expresses is true or false."
  type: true-false
  answer: false
  explanation: "Propositional truth is only one dimension of utterance meaning. Speech act theory identifies at least two additional dimensions: the illocutionary act (what the speaker is doing — requesting, promising, warning, asserting) and the perlocutionary effect (what the speaker intends to bring about in the listener). The same true proposition can perform completely different illocutionary acts: 'The door is open' can be a report, an indirect request to close it, a warning, or a hint — and knowing its truth value tells you nothing about which act was performed."

- question: "A promise, a warning, and a sincere apology are all examples of illocutionary acts — things done through words rather than merely described by them."
  type: true-false
  answer: true
  explanation: "Illocutionary acts are actions performed in the very act of speaking: promising, warning, apologizing, asserting, questioning, commanding. These are not descriptions of actions taken elsewhere — the words themselves constitute the action. When someone says 'I apologize,' they are not reporting that an apology exists somewhere; they are performing it. Austin's key insight was that many utterances are not truth-apt descriptions at all but performatives that change the social world when uttered in the right context."

- question: "Why does understanding communication require analyzing what a speaker is doing with words, rather than only what proposition they are expressing?"
  type: short-answer
  answer: "Because the same words can perform entirely different communicative acts depending on context, and because many acts — promising, requesting, warning, apologizing — cannot be captured by propositional truth conditions. Language is not just a system for encoding facts; it is a tool for doing things: creating commitments, regulating behavior, expressing attitudes, and coordinating action. Without analyzing the act being performed (the illocutionary force), phenomena like irony, indirect requests, and polite refusals become unintelligible."
  explanation: "Consider: 'Can you pass the salt?' is grammatically a question about ability, but communicatively a request. Analyzing only its propositional content (does the person have the physical ability to pass salt?) would make the exchange bizarre. Listeners routinely interpret utterances at the level of illocutionary force, not just propositional content. This is also why context is so central to meaning — the same sentence performs different acts in different situations, and a theory of communication must explain how speakers and listeners coordinate on the intended act."
```
