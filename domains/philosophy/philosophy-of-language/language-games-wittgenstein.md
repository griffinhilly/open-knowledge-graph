# Wittgenstein's Language Games

## Core Idea
Wittgenstein's later notion of language-games emphasizes that meaning emerges from use within rule-governed social practices. Different contexts ("buying," "praying," "playing") involve different language-games with different meanings for the same words. This challenges the idea that meaning is fixed or decontextualized, and that a single definition captures a word's meaning.

## Explainer

Wittgenstein's later philosophy represents a sharp turn away from his own earlier work and from most of the philosophical tradition. Where his early *Tractatus* held that language pictures facts and words stand for objects, the later *Philosophical Investigations* begins with a scene of builders calling out words for stones—"slab," "block," "pillar"—and argues that even this simple language is already a practice, embedded in a form of life, with its own internal rules. This primitive example is a **language-game**, and Wittgenstein's point is that all language use is like this: it is always part of a practice, always rule-governed, always dependent on shared human activities and reactions. The word "game" itself illustrates the insight—try to find a single definition that covers board games, card games, ball games, the Olympic games, and ring-around-the-rosy. You will find not a shared essence but **family resemblance**: overlapping and crisscrossing similarities without a single defining thread.

From your study of the basics of language-games, you know the core slogan: meaning is use. This does not mean words can mean anything or that meaning is arbitrary. The rules of a language-game determine what counts as a correct or incorrect use of a term within it. But crucially, the rules are not fixed by a mental act of defining—they emerge from and are maintained by participation in the practice. To understand a word is to be able to deploy it correctly in the relevant contexts, to respond to it appropriately, to recognize when it is being used correctly or incorrectly. Understanding is knowing how to go on, not grasping an inner mental object.

The diversity of language-games explains why philosophical confusion so often arises. We take a word that works well in one language-game—"know," "mean," "understand," "exist"—and ask a philosophical question about it as if it had a single fixed meaning applicable to all contexts. Wittgenstein diagnoses philosophical problems as typically arising from **language going on holiday**: we take words from their natural homes, strip away the context that gives them their function, and then are bewildered by the puzzles that result. The solution is not a theory but **perspicuous representation**—a clear view of how the language actually functions in its varied uses. From your work on the private language argument, you can see this applied: the temptation to think of sensation-words as naming private inner objects trades on a misleading language-game where we model sensation-language on the language of ordinary public objects.

The philosophical range of language-games is enormous. Promising, praying, commanding, reporting, thanking, joking, arithmetic, storytelling—each involves distinct kinds of moves, distinct criteria for correctness, distinct relationships between speakers. Wittgenstein is not saying these are all the same or that anything goes within them; each game has its own internal standards. He is saying that philosophical problems about truth, meaning, reference, and knowledge cannot be solved by a single theory imposed from outside: they require a patient investigation of how the relevant words actually function in the particular practices where they live. The goal is **therapeutic**: to dissolve the confusion, not to construct a competing metaphysical system.
