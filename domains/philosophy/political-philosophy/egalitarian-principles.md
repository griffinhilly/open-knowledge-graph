# Egalitarian Principles in Politics

## Core Idea
Egalitarian political theory holds that fundamental equality matters morally and should shape institutions. Different versions prioritize different forms of equality: equal opportunity, equal access to resources, equal political power, or equality of welfare. Egalitarians debate whether inequality is justified only if it benefits the least advantaged, whether merit-based inequalities are fair, and whether equality requires dismantling group-based hierarchies.

## How It's Best Learned
Map different egalitarian theories (luck egalitarianism, prioritarianism, democratic equality, relational equality) and compare their policy implications.

## Common Misconceptions
Egalitarianism does not require absolute equality—most egalitarians accept some inequalities if they benefit everyone or the worst-off. Also, equality can conflict with liberty or efficiency, creating practical trade-offs.

## Explainer

You already know from distributive justice that a central question in political philosophy is how a society should allocate benefits and burdens — income, wealth, opportunity, power. Egalitarianism enters that debate with a specific commitment: equality is not just instrumentally valuable (useful for producing other goods) but has *intrinsic* moral weight. But what kind of equality, and equality of what? This is where egalitarian theory fractures into a family of distinct views with different implications.

The most influential contemporary egalitarianism flows from John Rawls, whom you encountered in distributive justice. Rawls's **difference principle** permits inequalities only if they benefit the least-advantaged members of society. This is not a demand for equal outcomes; it is a demand that inequalities be *justified* — that they must make the worst-off group better off than they would be under strict equality. Think of a surgeon who earns more than a janitor: that inequality may be acceptable if the higher pay is needed to incentivize people to acquire scarce medical skills, and the resulting better health care improves outcomes for everyone including the poorest. But an inequality that merely benefits the already-wealthy, with no trickle-down to the least advantaged, fails the difference principle. The key move is that equality is the *default*, and departures from it require a specific justification.

**Luck egalitarianism** (associated with G.A. Cohen, Eric Rakowski) refines this by distinguishing between inequalities that arise from *luck* and those that arise from *choice*. If your poverty results from bad brute luck — being born into a poor family, having a disability, facing discrimination — egalitarianism demands redistribution. But if it results from a *free and informed choice* — choosing leisure over work, taking a risky gamble that paid off poorly — the inequality may be permissible. The intuition is that people should bear the consequences of their own choices but not of factors entirely outside their control. The difficulty is that "luck" and "choice" are hard to separate: your choices are shaped by your character, which was partly formed by circumstances you didn't choose.

**Relational egalitarianism** (Elizabeth Anderson, Samuel Scheffler) challenges both Rawlsian and luck egalitarian approaches by shifting focus from distribution to *social relations*. On this view, equality is fundamentally about how people stand with respect to each other — whether society is organized as a community of equals or is stratified by hierarchies of status, power, and social esteem. The problem with poverty is not primarily that some people have less stuff but that social inequality creates relations of domination, humiliation, and dependence. Anderson's critique of luck egalitarianism is sharp: in practice, identifying the "deserving poor" (those whose poverty stems from luck) requires intrusive inquiries into people's choices and character, which is itself degrading. A society of equals would not put people through such scrutiny to access basic provisions. These different egalitarian strands agree that equality matters but disagree profoundly about *what* equality requires and *why* — making egalitarian theory one of the richest debates in contemporary political philosophy.
