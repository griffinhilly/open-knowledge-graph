# Political Authority and Sources of Legitimacy

## Core Idea
Political authority is the right to command and be obeyed; legitimacy is what confers that right. Theories differ on sources: consent, tradition, democratic procedure, divine right, expertise, or justice of outcomes. A government might be effective without being legitimate, or vice versa. Understanding legitimacy separates tyranny from rightful rule and grounds obligations to obey.

- question: "A stable autocratic government has ruled for generations, consistently protecting citizens' rights and delivering just outcomes. However, citizens never consented to its authority. According to consent theory, is this government politically legitimate?"
  type: multiple-choice
  options:
    - "Yes — a government that actually protects rights fulfills the purpose of political authority, which is what matters"
    - "No — consent theory holds that authority is only legitimate if the governed have agreed to it; just outcomes cannot substitute for consent"
    - "Partially — the government has de facto legitimacy through effective rule but lacks de jure legitimacy"
    - "Yes — generations of continued residence implies tacit consent, even without explicit agreement"
  answer: 1
  explanation: "On consent theory (Locke's version), political authority is legitimate only when the governed have agreed to it. A just autocracy — however well-governed — lacks this foundation. Consent theory connects authority directly to individual autonomy: rules you haven't agreed to violate your freedom regardless of their content. Note that option A describes a 'justice of outcomes' criterion, which is a different theory that would indeed legitimate the autocracy. The theories give genuinely different answers because they ground legitimacy in different things."

- question: "A democratic majority consistently votes to remove legal protections from a religious minority, stripping them of civil rights while following all proper democratic procedures. Which theory of legitimacy is most challenged by this scenario?"
  type: multiple-choice
  options:
    - "Tradition-based legitimacy, because democracy is a modern institution without the historical durability that tradition requires"
    - "Democratic procedural legitimacy, because the scenario shows that formally correct procedures can produce unjust outcomes affecting minorities"
    - "Consent theory, because the minority never consented to being subject to majority rule"
    - "Justice-of-outcomes theory, which would straightforwardly condemn this as illegitimate"
  answer: 1
  explanation: "Democratic proceduralism grounds legitimacy in fair procedures that give all citizens equal standing — but this scenario follows the procedures while systematically harming a minority. This is the characteristic failure of democratic proceduralism: a procedurally correct democracy that consistently oppresses minorities may score poorly on justice-of-outcomes criteria while being formally 'legitimate' on procedural grounds. The scenario doesn't particularly challenge consent theory (the minority may have consented to majority rule in general) or tradition (the question is about outcomes, not historical continuity). It most sharply tests whether procedure alone is sufficient for legitimacy."

- question: "A government can be effective — fully capable of enforcing its commands and maintaining order — without being politically legitimate."
  type: true-false
  answer: true
  explanation: "True. This distinction — between effectiveness and legitimacy — is foundational to political philosophy. A government's power (its ability to coerce) and its right to command (its legitimacy) are logically independent. A dictator who maintains order through fear is effective but may lack any of the properties that various theories require for legitimacy: consent of the governed, just procedures, just outcomes, or traditional authority. Conversely, a legitimate government that loses control of its territory retains legitimacy even as its effectiveness wanes. Tyranny is characterized precisely by effectiveness without legitimacy."

- question: "According to social contract theorists like Locke, an individual who remains in a country and benefits from its institutions has consented to that government's authority in the same meaningful way as explicit contractual consent."
  type: true-false
  answer: false
  explanation: "False — and this is a major objection to tacit consent theories. The claim that 'remaining counts as consenting' is problematic because true consent requires a genuine ability to choose otherwise. When leaving a country is costly (financially, culturally, linguistically), staying cannot be freely distinguished from resignation or lack of alternatives. Hume made this objection classically: telling a poor man he has consented by staying is like telling someone they consented to a sea voyage by not jumping overboard. Tacit consent is therefore a much weaker form of authorization than explicit agreement, and whether it grounds the same obligations is contested."

- question: "Why does the source of political legitimacy matter for questions about civil disobedience and the duty to obey unjust laws?"
  type: short-answer
  answer: "The source of legitimacy determines the conditions under which it can be withdrawn or defied. On consent theory, authority is legitimate only while it protects natural rights; a government that violates those rights forfeits its claim to obedience, justifying resistance. On democratic proceduralism, a law passed through fair procedures may be unjust yet still obligatory — but systematic procedural unfairness could dissolve that obligation. On justice-of-outcomes theory, unjust laws lack legitimacy by definition, and disobedience is potentially always justified. Different legitimacy theories draw the line between legitimate authority and tyranny in different places, which directly determines when civil disobedience, resistance, or revolution is justified."
  explanation: "This is the practical payoff of legitimacy theory: it is not merely academic. The source you identify determines your conclusion about edge cases — minority oppression by democratic majorities, unjust but procedurally correct laws, effective but non-consensual governments. Each theory draws the obligatory/non-obligatory line differently. Understanding this is prerequisite to evaluating arguments for civil disobedience (MLK's Letter from Birmingham Jail), revolution (Locke on resistance to tyranny), and international political authority (who has the right to intervene in another state's affairs)."

## Explainer

From your prerequisite on **political authority and legitimacy**, you know the basic conceptual distinction: authority is the *right* to command and be obeyed, and legitimacy is what makes that right genuine rather than merely asserted. Now the question becomes: where does legitimacy actually *come from*? Different theories give radically different answers, and each has distinctive strengths and characteristic difficulties.

The most influential modern theory is **consent**. On this view, government authority is legitimate only if the governed have agreed to it — either through an explicit social contract or through tacit consent expressed by continued residence in a political community. Locke's version is canonical: people leave the state of nature by consenting to political authority in exchange for protection of their natural rights. The appeal of consent is that it connects authority directly to individual autonomy: your freedom is not violated by rules you agreed to. The problem is empirical — most citizens have never explicitly consented to anything, and the claim that "staying in the country counts as tacit consent" is difficult to distinguish from mere coercion when leaving is costly.

**Democratic procedure** offers a different source: authority is legitimate when it results from fair democratic processes that give all citizens equal standing to participate, regardless of whether each individual consented. **Tradition** grounds legitimacy in historical continuity — institutions that have governed stably over generations earn a kind of authority through demonstrated durability and shared expectations. **Justice of outcomes** reverses the procedural emphasis entirely: what makes authority legitimate is whether it actually produces just results, regardless of how decisions were reached. A benevolent autocracy might outscore a corrupt democracy on this criterion, while a procedurally correct democracy that systematically oppresses minorities might score poorly.

Each source generates characteristic tensions. Consent theories must explain authority over those who have explicitly withheld consent. Democratic theories must explain minorities who consistently lose votes. Tradition-based theories must explain how unjust but stable systems — like apartheid South Africa — could be legitimate. The practical stakes are high: the source of legitimacy determines when **civil disobedience**, **resistance**, and **revolution** are justified — topics you will encounter in the topics ahead. A government can be *effective* — capable of enforcing its commands — without being *legitimate*. And a government can be legitimate in procedure while producing unjust outcomes. Holding these distinctions clearly is the conceptual foundation for all of political philosophy's most urgent questions about when and why citizens must obey.
