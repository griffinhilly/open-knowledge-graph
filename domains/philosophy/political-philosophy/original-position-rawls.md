# The Original Position and Veil of Ignorance

## Core Idea
John Rawls's 'original position' is a hypothetical decision procedure: rational agents choose principles of justice from behind a 'veil of ignorance' that strips them of knowledge of their own place in society — class, wealth, natural talents, conception of the good. Because no one knows their position, no one can bias principles in their favor; the resulting principles reflect pure procedural fairness. Rawls argues that from behind the veil, rational agents would choose (1) equal basic liberties and (2) fair equality of opportunity plus the difference principle, because they would use maximin reasoning — protecting against the worst outcome. The original position is not a historical event but a model of what fairness requires.

## How It's Best Learned
Read A Theory of Justice §3–4, 24–26. Work through the maximin argument carefully: why would risk-neutral agents not choose utilitarianism? Engage the objections — Harsanyi argues expected utility maximization is more rational; Sen argues the veil distorts by stripping out morally relevant information.

## Common Misconceptions
- The veil of ignorance strips particular facts, not all knowledge — parties know general facts about psychology, economics, and human society.
- The original position is not an actual contract — it is a device for representing the conditions of fairness, not an account of how societies actually form.
- Rawls's maximin reasoning is not irrational risk-aversion per se; he argues the stakes (basic rights and life prospects) are too high for gambling.

## Explainer

From your study of distributive justice, you know the central question: how should the benefits and burdens of social cooperation be fairly allocated? Rawls's innovation is to answer this through a thought experiment that models the very *idea* of fairness as a choice procedure. The **original position** is not a claim about what people historically agreed to — it is a device for representing the conditions under which any principle of justice would be *unbiased*.

The **veil of ignorance** is the key mechanism. Imagine you are about to choose the basic structure of society — its institutions, its principles of distribution, its basic liberties — but you do not know who you will be in that society. You do not know your race, sex, class, natural talents, or even your conception of what makes life good. You know general truths about human psychology, economics, and political theory, but no particular facts that would allow you to bias the principles in your favor. In this condition of radical impartiality, Rawls argues, you would choose principles that protect *everyone* — because you could be anyone.

The **maximin** reasoning follows from this setup. Behind the veil, you face a choice under uncertainty about your future position. Standard decision theory might say: choose to maximize expected utility, weighting outcomes by their probability. But Rawls argues that when the stakes involve your basic rights and life prospects — the things that make a decent life possible at all — gambling is not rational. The worst possible outcome (being born into permanent disadvantage, denied basic liberties, trapped at the bottom) is too bad to risk for a chance at the top. The rational strategy is therefore **maximin**: choose to maximize the position of the least advantaged. This generates Rawls's two principles: (1) equal basic liberties for all, with priority over economic considerations; (2) social and economic inequalities are permissible only if they benefit the least well-off members of society (the **difference principle**).

The genius and the controversy of the original position lie in the same feature: it converts contested substantive questions about justice into a seemingly procedural question about what rational agents would choose under fair conditions. Critics like Robert Nozick argue that the veil strips out morally relevant features of persons (their actual histories, entitlements, talents). Harsanyi argues that expected-utility maximization is actually more rational than maximin, and that behind the veil rational agents would choose utilitarianism. Rawls's response to Harsanyi is that maximin is justified not by risk-aversion per se but by the special nature of the goods at stake — basic rights and the social bases of self-respect — which cannot be compensated for by gains elsewhere.
