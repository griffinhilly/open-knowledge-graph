# Metaphysical Structure and Architectural Form

## Core Idea
Metaphysical structure refers to the way entities, properties, and relations are organized in reality—what counts as fundamental versus derivative, how entities relate to their parts and properties, and what principles govern possible worlds. Formal metaphysics treats this structure as systematically analyzable, mapping the conceptual landscape that grounds all other facts.

## Questions

```yaml
- question: "A physicalist claims that mental facts are 'real but derivative.' What does this mean within the framework of metaphysical structure?"
  type: multiple-choice
  options:
    - "Mental facts are less important than physical facts for practical purposes"
    - "Mental facts are caused by physical processes in the brain, but exist independently at a different level"
    - "Mental facts exist but are grounded in physical facts — they have a metaphysical explanation in terms of something more basic, so no separate ontological category is needed"
    - "Mental facts do not really exist; the physicalist is simply denying consciousness"
  answer: 2
  explanation: "In the grounding framework, 'derivative' means grounded in something more basic — not non-existent. The physicalist strategy is precisely to accept mental facts as real while holding they are fully grounded in physical facts, avoiding the need for a separate ontological category for mind. This is importantly different from causation (option B): grounding is a non-causal, atemporal relation of metaphysical determination, not a physical process that unfolds over time. Option D describes eliminativism, a distinct and more radical view."

- question: "Which example best illustrates the shift from asking 'does X exist?' to asking 'what grounds X?' in formal metaphysics?"
  type: multiple-choice
  options:
    - "Asking whether consciousness exists versus asking whether neuroscience has fully explained it"
    - "Asking whether shadows exist versus asking whether shadows are fundamental entities or exist in virtue of light and objects blocking it"
    - "Asking whether God exists versus asking whether religious experience is reliable evidence"
    - "Asking whether numbers exist versus asking whether all conjectures about them have been proved"
  answer: 1
  explanation: "The shadow case cleanly illustrates the distinction. 'Does a shadow exist?' is almost trivially yes — shadows are real. The more refined question is: 'At what level of the metaphysical hierarchy does a shadow sit, and what grounds it?' The answer is that shadows are derivative: they exist in virtue of the light source and the occluding object. This is not a causal claim (the object doesn't cause the shadow in the grounding sense) but a metaphysical one. The other options conflate existence questions with epistemic or empirical questions."

- question: "Grounding is a causal relation: a shadow is grounded in the light and object that cause it."
  type: true-false
  answer: false
  explanation: "Grounding is explicitly a *non-causal* relation of metaphysical determination. Causation is a temporal process — the object blocks the light at a time and the shadow results. Grounding is atemporal constitution: the shadow's existence is metaphysically determined by the light and object *at the same moment*, not through a process. The distinction matters because grounding is irreflexive, asymmetric, and transitive like causation, but it operates at the level of what things *are*, not what brings them *about*."

- question: "In formal metaphysics, something can be real without being fundamental — it genuinely exists but exists in virtue of something more basic."
  type: true-false
  answer: true
  explanation: "This is the central claim of the fundamental/derivative distinction. Ontological parsimony does not require eliminating derivative entities — it requires not multiplying *fundamental* entities beyond necessity. Shadows, mental facts, composite objects, and social institutions may all be real while being grounded in more basic facts. The question 'does X exist?' and the question 'is X fundamental?' have different answers, and conflating them leads to false dilemmas (either X is real-and-fundamental or X doesn't exist at all)."

- question: "Why does the concept of 'grounding' require that something can be both real and derivative? What problem would arise if we treated 'derivative' as equivalent to 'doesn't exist'?"
  type: short-answer
  answer: "If 'derivative' meant 'doesn't exist,' then physicalism would be committed to eliminating mental facts, biological facts, chemical facts, and every other level of description above fundamental physics — a radical and implausible conclusion. The grounding framework allows us to say that mental facts are real (they enter into explanations, ground further facts, are objects of genuine knowledge) while also being metaphysically explained by physical facts. Without this distinction, every non-fundamental entity would have to be denied existence, leaving only the fundamental level as real."
  explanation: "This connects to the broader methodological point: metaphysical structure lets you ask 'how does X relate to the rest of reality?' rather than the blunter 'does X exist?' The latter tends to produce false dichotomies (substance dualism vs. eliminativism about mind). The former allows a richer picture: a hierarchy where things at different levels are all real but stand in grounding relations to each other. The question of what is fundamental remains open and contested; the existence of derivative entities at higher levels is not threatened by that debate."
```

## Explainer

In your prerequisite work on **what metaphysics is** and **ontological categories**, you learned that metaphysics asks foundational questions about what exists and how it is categorized. Metaphysical structure takes the next step: given that things exist and fall into categories, how are those categories and things *organized*? Reality isn't a flat list of equally basic items — there appears to be hierarchy, dependency, and architecture. Some things exist because of other things; some properties are built out of simpler properties; some facts obtain because of more fundamental facts. Mapping this architecture is what formal metaphysics does.

The key organizing concept is the distinction between **fundamental** and **derivative** entities. Something is fundamental if it doesn't exist in virtue of something else — it has no metaphysical explanation in terms of more basic things. Something is derivative if it does exist in virtue of something more basic. A shadow is derivative: it exists because of the light and the object blocking it. The particles making up that object might be fundamental, or they might themselves be derivative with respect to quantum fields. Metaphysical structure is the question: how deep does this hierarchy go, and what does it look like?

The formal relation that articulates this hierarchy is **grounding**: a relation of non-causal metaphysical determination. The fact that a ball is red and round *grounds* the fact that there is something colored. The physical facts about a brain *ground* the mental facts about its subject (if physicalism is true). Grounding is asymmetric, irreflexive, and transitive — it's a strict partial order on facts or entities. The structural question is whether grounding bottoms out in some set of fully ungrounded fundamentalia, or whether it could be infinitely descending.

This architectural picture has direct implications for **ontological parsimony** — the methodological preference for not multiplying entities beyond necessity. If mental facts are fully grounded in physical facts, we don't need an additional ontological category for minds: they're real but derivative. This is the strategy that physicalists use. Metaphysical structure lets you make these commitments precise: rather than asking "does X exist?" the more refined question is "at what level of the metaphysical hierarchy does X sit, and what grounds it?" Understanding this architecture is prerequisite to the more specific debates in grounding, fundamentality, and the theory of properties that this course builds toward.
