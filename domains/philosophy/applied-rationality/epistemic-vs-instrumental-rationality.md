## Core Idea

Epistemic rationality is the art of forming accurate beliefs — building a mental map that corresponds to the territory of reality. Instrumental rationality is the art of achieving your goals — choosing actions that produce desired outcomes given your beliefs. These two types of rationality are complementary: you cannot reliably achieve your goals with false beliefs, and the motivation to form true beliefs often comes from wanting to act effectively. The Rationalist tradition treats epistemic rationality as foundational, arguing that systematic improvement in belief-formation is both possible and trainable.

## How It's Best Learned

Start by examining cases where the two types of rationality come apart. A comforting false belief might feel instrumentally useful ("I'll definitely get the job") but harms long-term planning. Conversely, someone might hold accurate beliefs but fail to act on them. Work through examples from your own life where accurate beliefs would have changed your actions, and where motivated reasoning led you to conclusions that felt good but turned out wrong.

## Common Misconceptions

- Rationality is not the same as being emotionless or coldly calculating — emotions often carry real information and motivate useful action.
- Epistemic and instrumental rationality do not always conflict — in most real-world situations, having accurate beliefs directly serves your goals.
- Rationality is not about being "right" in arguments — it is about systematically improving the accuracy of your beliefs and the effectiveness of your actions.
