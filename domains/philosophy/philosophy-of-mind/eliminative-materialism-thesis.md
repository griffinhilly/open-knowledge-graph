# Eliminative Materialism

## Core Idea
Eliminative materialism claims that our everyday psychological concepts (beliefs, desires, consciousness) are fundamentally mistaken and will be replaced by neuroscientific descriptions. Mental states as conceived by folk psychology may not actually exist, much as phlogiston was eliminated from chemistry.

## Explainer

From physicalism-reduction-commitment, you know the core thesis: mental states are nothing over and above physical (neural) states. But *how* physical are they? Most physicalists keep folk-psychological categories — belief, desire, pain — and argue these reduce to or are realized by physical ones. Eliminative materialism stakes out a far more radical position: those categories may not survive contact with mature neuroscience at all.

The historical analogy is instructive. "Phlogiston" was the 18th-century theory of combustion — a substance supposedly released during burning. The phlogiston framework was not refined by chemistry; it was eliminated. Oxygen-based combustion theory did not reduce phlogiston to something else — it showed phlogiston was a theoretical posit in a false framework. Paul and Patricia Churchland argue that **folk psychology** — our everyday framework of beliefs, desires, intentions, and reasoning — is similarly a theory, one we have held implicitly for millennia. And like any theory, it could be false.

The key claim is that folk-psychological categories may be radically mismatched with the actual structure of cognition. Neuroscience describes neural firing patterns, connectionist networks, and distributed representations — nothing that obviously maps onto "having a belief that it will rain" as a discrete, sentence-like state. If the map never matches the territory, the rational response is not to find a neural reduction of beliefs but to abandon the belief-desire framework altogether, just as we abandoned caloric fluid.

Eliminativism's critics raise two powerful objections. First, the **self-refutation worry**: to claim that there are no beliefs seems to *use* the concept of belief (you believe there are no beliefs). Eliminativists respond that this assumes folk psychology is necessary for self-description — but a mature neuroscience might provide entirely new self-descriptive vocabulary. Second, there is a phenomenological objection: whatever neuroscience says, there *seem to be* beliefs and desires. Eliminativists reply that seemings are themselves part of the theory — we should not let folk-psychological intuitions serve as the standard by which we judge folk psychology.

Understanding eliminativism clarifies the full landscape of physicalism. It marks the outer edge: physicalism ranges from non-reductive views (mental properties supervene on physical ones but are irreducible) through reductive views (mental states reduce to brain states) all the way to elimination (mental states as typically conceived do not exist). The eliminativist wager is that neuroscience is, or will become, mature enough to make folk psychology as obsolete as astrology.
