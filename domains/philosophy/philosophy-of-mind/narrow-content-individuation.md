# Narrow Content and Mental Individuation

## Core Idea
Narrow content theory proposes that what a mental state represents depends only on the individual's intrinsic, internal physical properties—not on the external environment. On this view, your thoughts about water would be identical to your thoughts about 'twater' if your intrinsic brain states were identical, even though water and twater are different.

## How It's Best Learned
Examine Twin Earth scenarios carefully. Consider what role the body, brain, and environment play in determining thought content.

## Common Misconceptions
- Assuming narrow content is obviously true.
- Thinking narrow content requires representationalism.
- Confusing narrow content with introspective content.

## Explainer

You already know that mental states are **intentional** — they are about things in the world. When you think about water, your thought has water as its content. But here is a pressing question: what *makes* a mental state be about one thing rather than another? Is it determined entirely by what is going on inside your head, or does the external environment play an essential role? Narrow content theory takes the first side of this debate.

The clearest way to understand narrow content is through Hilary Putnam's famous **Twin Earth** thought experiment. Imagine a planet, Twin Earth, that is molecule-for-molecule identical to Earth in every way except one: the clear, drinkable liquid that Twin Earthlings call "water" is not H₂O but a different compound, XYZ, which behaves exactly like water in every everyday respect. Now consider two people: you on Earth, and your perfect physical duplicate on Twin Earth. You both have exactly the same brain states, the same behavioral dispositions, the same intrinsic physical properties. Yet when you think "water," your thought is about H₂O; when your twin thinks "water," their thought is about XYZ. Putnam's conclusion: "meanings just ain't in the head." The content of mental states is partly determined by factors *outside* the individual — by what you are actually causally connected to in your environment.

**Narrow content** is the response: perhaps there is still a sense in which you and your twin share a mental content. Narrow content is whatever is determined solely by the thinker's intrinsic physical states — the stuff that is the same for you and your twin regardless of environmental differences. It can be thought of as a **function from contexts to truth-conditions**: your narrow thought about water is a mental state that, placed in an Earth context, picks out H₂O and, placed in a Twin Earth context, picks out XYZ. The "narrow content" is the function itself; the "wide content" is the output of the function given your actual environmental context. You encountered representationalism as background — on narrow content views, the representational properties of a mental state that matter for its computational and inferential role are captured by the narrow content; the causal-historical connections to the world that fix the wide content are external add-ons.

Why does this matter? Think about how you came to understand spectrum inversion — the possibility that your experience of red could be internally identical to mine while referring to different things in the world. Narrow content generalizes that intuition to *all* intentional content, not just phenomenal qualities. It also has deep implications for cognitive science: if mental computations are sensitive to narrow content rather than wide content (since the brain only "sees" its own internal states), then a science of cognition might be a science of narrow states, with wide content added later when we situate the individual in their environment. The debate between narrow and wide content theories is really a debate about what mental states *are* — whether they are fully constituted by what is inside the skin or whether they are essentially world-involving.
