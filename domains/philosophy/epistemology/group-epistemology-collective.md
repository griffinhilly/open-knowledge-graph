# Group Knowledge and Collective Epistemology

## Core Idea
Group epistemology extends knowledge analysis beyond individuals to social units and collectives. A group can be said to collectively know something when appropriate information is distributed among members and suitable procedures exist for aggregating that information into group-level beliefs and actions. This extension reveals challenges in applying individual-level knowledge conditions to emergent group-level phenomena.

## How It's Best Learned
Consider knowledge possessed by organizations: does a corporation 'know' facts distributed across its departments? How do communication breakdowns and organizational structure affect group knowledge? These questions reveal whether knowledge conditions apply at multiple levels.

## Common Misconceptions
- Group knowledge isn't just that all individual members know something individually. - Collective knowledge can exist even when no single individual possesses it. - Analyzing group knowledge requires attention to information pooling and decision procedures.

## Explainer

Your study of **justified true belief (JTB)** gave you the classical framework for individual knowledge: a belief is knowledge if it is true, believed, and held for good reasons. That framework assumes an individual knower — someone who holds a mental state, has access to evidence, and can form inferences. **Group epistemology** asks: can these conditions be meaningfully extended to groups? Does a corporation "know" the contents of its files? Does a scientific community "know" the facts it has collectively established? These questions force us to think carefully about what each knowledge condition requires and whether it can be instantiated at a level above the individual.

The first challenge is the **belief condition**. Groups do not have unified mental states — they have procedures, commitments, records, and members. One approach is **summativist**: group belief just is some function of individual beliefs (e.g., majority or unanimous belief). Another is **non-summativist**: group belief is constituted by collective acceptance through appropriate procedures, regardless of what individuals privately believe. A legal body, for example, "accepts" findings through its procedural outputs — its rulings are group-level epistemic acts even when individual members dissent. The non-summativist view captures something important: groups act, commit, and are held responsible as units, not merely as collections of individuals.

The second challenge is the **justification condition**. For an individual, justification depends on their access to evidence and the quality of their inference. For a group, justification depends on the quality of its **information-pooling and deliberation procedures**. A group is epistemically justified when it has methods that reliably aggregate evidence from members, correct for individual biases, and track the truth. This is why **epistemic proceduralism** matters: not every way of reaching group consensus is equally good. Voting may be less truth-tracking than deliberation; hierarchical authority may suppress accurate information that flows only upward through gatekeepers. Group justification is a property of the group's processes, not just its conclusions.

The most interesting puzzles arise at the intersection of the truth condition and distributed information: a group can collectively "know" something in the distributed sense — the information exists among the members — while no individual knows it and no group-level process has yet extracted it. This points toward an important practical insight: **the epistemic value of a group depends on its ability to pool distributed information effectively**. Groups that fail to aggregate what their members collectively know — because of hierarchical filters, communication barriers, or incentives that suppress sharing — will behave epistemically worse than their pooled information would permit. Good group epistemics is therefore as much an organizational and institutional problem as a philosophical one.
