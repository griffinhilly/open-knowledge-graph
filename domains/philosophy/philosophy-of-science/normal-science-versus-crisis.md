# Normal Science and Crisis

## Core Idea
Kuhn distinguished normal science—routine puzzle-solving within an established framework—from crisis science, where accumulated anomalies undermine confidence in fundamental assumptions. Most scientific work is normal science; crises are rare but transformative moments when the field must reorient itself entirely.

## Explainer

From your study of the **demarcation problem**, you know that philosophers have struggled to define what separates science from non-science. Popper's answer was falsifiability: a good scientific theory makes bold predictions that could in principle be refuted. Kuhn's account of normal science complicates this picture in a historically grounded way. Real scientists, Kuhn observed, do not treat their core theories as ever ready to be falsified. They treat them as more or less definitely correct and spend their careers working out the details. This is not a failure of rationality — it is what makes cumulative scientific progress possible.

Kuhn introduces the concept of a **paradigm**: an exemplary achievement that defines a field's standards, methods, and accepted questions. The paradigm is not just a theory; it is a complete practice that includes instruments, techniques, and assumptions about what constitutes a satisfactory answer. Newtonian mechanics was a paradigm: not just F=ma, but a whole toolkit for setting up problems, computing solutions, and judging when a result is right. A new student entering a field is socialized into its paradigm through textbooks and training — they learn not just facts but how to think like a physicist, biologist, or chemist.

**Normal science** is paradigm-governed puzzle-solving. The paradigm tells you which problems are worth tackling, what constraints an acceptable solution must satisfy, and which anomalies are merely technical difficulties to be resolved later versus genuine threats to the framework. The key point is that anomalies — observations that don't fit the current framework — are never taken automatically as refutations. Instead, they become puzzles: something about the setup must be wrong, the instruments need refinement, the calculations need revision. Scientists are expected to solve puzzles within the paradigm, not to overthrow it. This is why falsification, as a description of normal science, misses the mark: a single anomalous result rarely leads rational scientists to abandon a productive paradigm.

**Crisis** occurs when anomalies resist all attempts at puzzle-solving and accumulate to a degree that unsettles the community's confidence. Crucially, scientists do not simply continue as before; they begin to entertain multiple competing alternatives simultaneously, loosen their methodological norms, and openly debate foundations — activities that normal science suppresses. The crisis is not just empirical failure; it is a loss of consensus about what the field is even doing. Eventually, one framework emerges as a compelling alternative, and a **paradigm shift** occurs. The old framework is not simply refuted; it is replaced. This is the topic you will pursue next. The importance of the normal/crisis distinction is that it explains why science is both conservative (resistant to premature revision) and revolutionary (capable of radical reorientation) — two traits that seem in tension but are both essential to how science actually works.
