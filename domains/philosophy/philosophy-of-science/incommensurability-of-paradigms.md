# Incommensurability of Paradigms

## Core Idea
Kuhn controversially claimed successive paradigms are incommensurable: they employ different concepts, standards, and observational frameworks, making neutral comparison impossible. This raises the question of how rational progress occurs across paradigm shifts, or whether the appearance of progress is merely a retrospective narrative.

## Common Misconceptions
Thinking incommensurability means paradigms cannot be compared at all (Kuhn allows some comparison). Assuming incommensurability entails relativism about rationality. Misreading Kuhn as claiming science is irrational.

## Explainer

You've studied how **paradigm shifts** work: science doesn't progress through steady accumulation but through revolutionary breaks where one paradigm is replaced by another. Incommensurability is Kuhn's sharpest and most controversial claim about what happens at those breaks. It says the two paradigms don't just disagree — they can't be directly compared using a single neutral measuring stick.

**Incommensurability** literally means "no common measure" — the term comes from the geometric discovery that the diagonal and side of a square have no common unit. Kuhn argues that pre- and post-revolutionary paradigms are incommensurable in (at least) two ways. First, **semantic incommensurability**: key terms change meaning across the transition. "Mass" in Newtonian mechanics is an intrinsic property of matter; in Einsteinian mechanics, mass is relative to reference frame. These aren't the same concept with updated content — the entire conceptual role of the term has shifted. If "mass" means something different in each theory, direct contradiction is impossible: they're not even making competing claims about the same thing.

Second, **methodological incommensurability**: the standards by which a good theory is evaluated also shift. Newtonian mechanics prized deterministic trajectories; quantum mechanics made irreducible probabilism a virtue rather than a defect. A Newtonian judging quantum mechanics by Newtonian standards would find it deficient — but the quantum physicist isn't playing by those rules. This is why Kuhn says scientific revolutions involve something like a **Gestalt switch**: you don't just accept new conclusions, you learn to see the phenomena through an entirely new set of conceptual lenses.

The stakes for scientific rationality are high. If paradigms can't be evaluated by neutral criteria, how do scientists ever rationally choose between them? Kuhn's answer was never that paradigm choice is irrational — he insisted scientists have good reasons, including problem-solving success, predictive accuracy, and fruitfulness. But those reasons don't add up to a decisive *proof* that one paradigm is correct by criteria both sides accept. Ultimately, the transition happens when a critical mass of practitioners finds the new paradigm more promising. Critics like Lakatos and Putnam charged that this makes science sound more like sociology than logic. Kuhn responded late in his career by restricting incommensurability to *local* term changes rather than wholesale world-view incompatibility — but the original provocative claim remains the one that shaped the subsequent debate over scientific rationality and realism.
