# The Problem of Demarcation

## Core Idea
The demarcation problem asks: by what criterion can we distinguish scientific claims from non-scientific ones? This question is crucial for deciding what deserves inclusion in science curricula, research funding, and educational standards, yet no universally accepted answer exists despite centuries of effort.

## Explainer

Your introduction to philosophy of science established that science is a distinctive form of inquiry — but *how* distinctive, and where does it end? The demarcation problem is the attempt to answer that question precisely. It matters not just philosophically but practically: courts adjudicate whether creationism is science, regulatory agencies evaluate whether homeopathy deserves the same evidentiary standard as pharmacology, and funding bodies decide which research programs to support. Each of these decisions implicitly applies a demarcation criterion.

The **logical positivists** of the Vienna Circle proposed **verificationism**: a statement is meaningful only if it is either analytically true (true by definition) or empirically verifiable in principle. Metaphysical claims — "the Absolute is beyond all determination," "the soul persists after death" — came out as neither true nor false but literally meaningless. Science was the domain of verifiable propositions. The criterion was radical but faced a fatal objection: universal scientific laws like "all copper conducts electricity" cannot be conclusively verified, because verification would require testing every piece of copper that exists or ever will exist. The verification principle excluded much of science itself.

Popper's **falsificationism** reversed the logic. Instead of asking what evidence would confirm a claim, ask what evidence would *refute* it. A claim is scientific if it sticks its neck out — if it rules out some possible observations. "Tomorrow it will rain or it won't" is unfalsifiable and scientifically empty. "The orbit of Mercury will precess by exactly 43 arc-seconds per century due to spacetime curvature" is a bold prediction that general relativity makes and could in principle be wrong. The power of this criterion is that it explains not just what science is but what good science looks like: genuine risk-taking, not prediction after the fact.

Falsificationism's weaknesses led to more nuanced proposals. Lakatos developed the concept of a **scientific research programme**, with a hard core of central commitments surrounded by a protective belt of auxiliary hypotheses. A programme is **progressive** when it generates novel successful predictions, **degenerative** when it merely accommodates anomalies with ad hoc adjustments. Feyerabend went further, arguing that no methodological rule — including falsifiability — has been followed consistently by the greatest scientific advances; his slogan "anything goes" challenges the very project of universal demarcation. Kuhn's paradigm account offers yet another angle: what counts as science is partly defined by the practice of a scientific community, not an abstract criterion.

The upshot is that demarcation is **gradient, not binary**. Disciplines do not simply fall into "science" or "not science" — they exhibit more or less of the markers associated with successful scientific inquiry: bold testable predictions, falsifiability of core claims, progressive development, intersubjective reproducibility, and mechanism-based explanation. Pseudosciences like astrology or homeopathy typically fail several of these markers simultaneously, which is why they are recognizable as pseudoscience even without a single knock-down criterion.
