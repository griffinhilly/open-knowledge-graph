# Capital Flows and Balance of Payments

## Core Idea
The balance of payments records a country's international transactions: the current account (trade in goods and services plus income flows) and the capital account (flows of foreign investment). Countries with current account deficits attract foreign capital inflows, financing either higher domestic investment or consumption. Long-term sustainability requires that persistent current account deficits eventually reverse or that foreign debt levels remain manageable relative to income and collateral.

## Explainer

From the Mundell-Fleming framework, you already know that in an open economy, monetary and fiscal policy operate through both domestic channels and international capital flows, with the exchange rate regime determining which channel dominates. The balance of payments extends this analysis by tracking every transaction between a country and the rest of the world, revealing the aggregate pattern of borrowing and lending that accompanies trade.

The **current account** captures the flow of goods, services, and income. When a country imports more than it exports, it runs a current account deficit—it is consuming more than it produces. But this deficit is not free; it must be financed. The accounting identity of the balance of payments guarantees that a current account deficit is matched dollar-for-dollar by a **capital account surplus**—net inflows of foreign investment. This is not a theory but a bookkeeping fact: if Americans buy $500 billion more in foreign goods than they sell, foreigners must be acquiring $500 billion in American assets (Treasury bonds, equities, real estate, or direct investment). The current account deficit and capital account surplus are two sides of the same coin.

The critical question is whether persistent deficits are **sustainable**. A country borrowing from abroad to finance productive investment—building factories, infrastructure, or technology—can generate returns that service the accumulated foreign debt. This is the benign interpretation: capital flows from rich, capital-abundant countries to poorer, capital-scarce countries where returns are higher, improving global efficiency. But a country borrowing to finance consumption or unproductive investment accumulates debt without building the capacity to repay it. The **intertemporal budget constraint** requires that the present value of future trade surpluses eventually equals the accumulated foreign debt. A country that persistently violates this constraint faces a **sudden stop**—an abrupt reversal of capital inflows when foreign lenders lose confidence, forcing a sharp and painful adjustment in domestic spending.

Historical experience illustrates both paths. East Asian economies in the 1990s attracted massive capital inflows that partly financed productive investment but also fueled asset bubbles and crony lending. When confidence reversed in 1997, capital fled, currencies collapsed, and the region experienced severe recessions. By contrast, the United States has run current account deficits almost continuously since the 1980s, financed by the dollar's reserve currency status and deep, liquid capital markets that make U.S. assets attractive to foreign savers. Whether this represents sustainable borrowing or a slow-building vulnerability remains one of the central debates in international macroeconomics. The balance of payments framework gives you the accounting structure to track these flows; the economic challenge is assessing when the accumulated position crosses from manageable to dangerous.
