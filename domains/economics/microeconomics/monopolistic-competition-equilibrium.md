# Monopolistic Competition and Long-Run Equilibrium

## Core Idea
Monopolistic competitors have downward-sloping demand (product differentiation) but face entry in long run. Short-run profit triggers entry, shifting each firm's demand leftward until zero economic profit where P = AC and MR = MC. Long-run equilibrium involves excess capacity (firms produce left of minimum AC) and zero profit, unlike perfect competition. Welfare loss is from underutilized capacity and monopoly power, but product variety may offset part of this inefficiency.

This means each firm is producing less than the output that would minimize its average cost. In perfect competition, the flat demand curve forces tangency exactly at minimum AC (efficient scale). The excess capacity result is a direct consequence of downward-sloping demand combined with zero-profit tangency."

- question: "In the long-run equilibrium of a monopolistically competitive firm, economic profit is zero, meaning the firm charges a price equal to its average cost."
  type: true-false
  answer: true
  explanation: "Zero economic profit (the condition for long-run equilibrium once entry has driven out excess returns) requires P = AC. This is because economic profit per unit is (P - AC), so zero profit means price exactly covers average cost. The firm is not earning a normal accounting return on top of nothing — 'zero economic profit' includes a normal return to capital. The demand curve is tangent to the AC curve at the profit-maximizing output, geometrically confirming P = AC at that quantity."

- question: "In the long-run equilibrium of a monopolistically competitive industry, each firm produces where price equals marginal cost, just as in perfect competition."
  type: true-false
  answer: false
  explanation: "This is a critical distinction. In monopolistic competition, each firm has a downward-sloping demand curve and sets MR = MC for profit maximization. Because MR < P for a downward-sloping demand curve, profit-maximizing output (where MR = MC) always yields P > MC. In perfect competition, MR = P (the firm is a price-taker with a flat demand), so MR = MC implies P = MC. Monopolistic competition achieves P = AC (zero profit) but not P = MC — the firm retains a degree of market power even in long-run equilibrium."

- question: "Why does monopolistic competition lead to 'excess capacity' in long-run equilibrium, and is this necessarily a sign of inefficiency?"
  type: short-answer

However, whether this is 'inefficient' depends on whether you count product variety as a benefit. The extra firms and the underutilization of scale is the cost of variety; consumers may value having many differentiated options more than they would value cheaper homogeneous products. The welfare tradeoff cannot be resolved by the diagram alone."
  explanation: "The excess capacity result follows directly from the geometry: a downward-sloping demand curve can only be tangent to a U-shaped AC curve on the falling portion, never at the minimum. This is an inescapable consequence of product differentiation. The deeper question — is this waste worth the variety? — is one of industrial organization's central open questions. Perfect competition is statically efficient (P = MC, production at minimum AC) but delivers no variety. The efficiency-variety tradeoff is why monopolistic competition occupies a distinct theoretical niche between perfect competition and monopoly."

## Explainer

From your knowledge of monopolistic competition, you know that product differentiation gives each firm a downward-sloping demand curve — customers have some brand loyalty, so the firm is not a pure price-taker. In the short run, this market power can generate economic profit. But monopolistic competition differs from pure monopoly in one critical way: **entry is free**. This single feature transforms the long-run outcome entirely.

When firms in the industry are earning positive economic profit, new entrants arrive offering their own differentiated products. Each new entrant steals some customers from existing firms, shifting each incumbent's demand curve leftward (and typically making it more elastic, as more substitutes become available). Firms respond by cutting prices or increasing marketing, but profits erode. Entry continues until every firm earns exactly zero economic profit — at which point there is no further incentive to enter. This self-correcting dynamic is the same force that drives perfect competition to zero profit, but it operates through product variety rather than price competition alone.

The **long-run equilibrium geometry** is distinctive. Zero profit means the demand curve is tangent to the average cost curve: price exactly covers average cost, leaving no surplus. At the same point, MR = MC (profit maximization). The tangency occurs on the downward-sloping portion of the AC curve — to the *left* of minimum average cost. This is the **excess capacity** result: each firm produces less than the output that would minimize average cost. There are "too many" firms, each running below efficient scale, in contrast to perfect competition where long-run equilibrium drives every firm to minimum AC.

Whether this outcome is inefficient depends on what you count as the benefit. Technically, excess capacity is wasteful — the same total output could be produced by fewer, larger firms at lower average cost. But monopolistic competition also delivers **product variety**, which consumers value. Diners have many restaurant choices; readers have many book options. The welfare comparison requires weighing the cost of excess capacity against the consumer value of variety, which cannot be resolved by the supply-demand diagram alone. This tradeoff — efficiency versus variety — is one of the central unresolved debates in industrial organization economics.
