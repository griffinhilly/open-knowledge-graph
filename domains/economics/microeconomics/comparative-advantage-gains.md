# Comparative Advantage and Gains from Trade

## Core Idea
Comparative advantage occurs when a producer can make a good at a lower opportunity cost than another. Even if one producer is better at everything (absolute advantage), both parties benefit from specializing in what they have comparative advantage in and trading. This explains why specialization and exchange occur.

## Explainer

The key to comparative advantage is understanding that the word "advantage" is about **opportunity cost**, not raw productivity. Suppose you've already mastered the concept of opportunity cost: every time you produce something, you give up something else. Comparative advantage asks: who gives up *less* of the other thing? That is the producer with the comparative advantage in the first good, and they should specialize in it.

A concrete example makes this click. Imagine a lawyer and a paralegal. The lawyer types faster and drafts legal briefs better — she has **absolute advantage** in both tasks. But drafting briefs is far more valuable per hour than typing. Every hour the lawyer spends typing is an hour not drafting — a high opportunity cost. The paralegal's opportunity cost of typing is low (he doesn't give up much by not drafting). So the paralegal should type and the lawyer should draft, even though the lawyer is better at both. The legal team produces more output together than if each tried to do everything themselves.

This is the core of **gains from trade**: when each party specializes in their comparative advantage and trades for the rest, total production expands. Neither party needs absolute advantage — even a less productive country or person benefits from trade, because the gains come from the difference in opportunity costs, not the absolute level of productivity. A country that is worse at producing everything can still export the goods where it is *relatively less bad*, because its trading partner has a higher opportunity cost producing those goods.

Why does this work mathematically? The joint production frontier expands when parties specialize and trade. When each producer fully concentrates on the good they make at lower opportunity cost, the pair moves beyond what either could achieve in autarky (producing everything alone). The consumption possibilities after trade exceed the production possibilities without trade — both parties can consume more of both goods than they could if isolated. This expansion of the feasible set is what "gains from trade" refers to. Comparative advantage is not merely a curiosity; it is the fundamental economic argument for why markets and specialization improve outcomes even without any technological change.
