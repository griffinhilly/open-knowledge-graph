# The Lewis Model: Dual Economies and Unlimited Labor Supply

## Core Idea
W. Arthur Lewis proposed that developing economies have a traditional agricultural sector with surplus labor earning subsistence wages and a modern industrial sector. As capital accumulates in industry, workers migrate from agriculture at constant wages, enabling industrial capitalists to retain profits for reinvestment and accelerating growth until the agricultural surplus is exhausted.

## How It's Best Learned
Map rural-to-urban labor flows and wage trends in case studies like China or Vietnam. Compare the model's predictions (constant wages during industrial expansion) with actual wage data.

## Explainer

From your study of structural transformation, you know that economic development involves a shift from agriculture to industry and services — a process every wealthy country has undergone. The Lewis model provides a specific mechanism for how this transformation begins and sustains itself, built on a single striking assumption: in the early stages of development, the agricultural sector has so many workers that some of them contribute virtually nothing to output. This **surplus labor** — workers whose marginal product is at or near zero — is the engine that drives the model.

Imagine a rural village where 10 people farm land that really only needs 6 workers to cultivate fully. The other 4 contribute little or nothing to total farm output — if they left, the harvest would barely change. These workers earn a **subsistence wage** determined by tradition, family sharing norms, or the average (not marginal) product of the farm. Now suppose a factory opens in a nearby city. It needs workers, and it only has to offer slightly more than the subsistence wage to attract them. The surplus laborers migrate, and crucially, agricultural output does not fall because their marginal product was near zero. The factory gets cheap labor, the workers get higher income, and the countryside loses nothing.

This creates a powerful growth dynamic. Because industrial wages stay roughly constant (the factory can always recruit more surplus labor from the countryside at the same low wage), **industrial profits are high**. The Lewis model assumes capitalists reinvest these profits — building more factories, buying more equipment — which creates demand for still more workers. As long as agricultural surplus labor exists, this cycle continues: investment → more industrial jobs → more migration → constant wages → high profits → more investment. Growth feeds on itself without wages rising, because the labor supply to industry is essentially **perfectly elastic** at the subsistence wage.

The model predicts a critical turning point: the **Lewis turning point**, when surplus labor is finally exhausted. Once the agricultural sector has shed all its excess workers, further industrial expansion requires pulling workers whose departure actually reduces farm output. Now agriculture must compete for labor, rural wages begin to rise, and industrial wages must rise with them. Profits get squeezed, the pace of capital accumulation slows, and the economy transitions to a neoclassical growth pattern where wages track productivity. China's experience illustrates this beautifully — decades of rapid industrial growth with relatively flat factory wages gave way, around 2010, to rising wages as the rural surplus diminished. This is exactly what Lewis predicted.

The model has important limitations. It assumes capitalists reinvest profits rather than consuming them or sending capital abroad — an assumption that fails in many developing countries where elites engage in capital flight. It treats the agricultural sector as stagnant, ignoring the possibility that agricultural productivity improvements could also drive development. And it says little about what happens when the modern sector is not manufacturing but services, or when urbanization occurs without industrialization (as in much of contemporary Africa). Despite these limitations, the Lewis model remains the foundational framework for thinking about labor-surplus economies and the mechanics of early-stage structural transformation.
