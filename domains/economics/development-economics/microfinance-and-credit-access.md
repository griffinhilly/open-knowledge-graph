# Microfinance and Financial Inclusion

## Core Idea
Microfinance—small loans to poor individuals without collateral—was celebrated as a poverty cure, but RCT evidence shows modest, mixed impacts. It enables self-employment for those inclined toward it, but does not create jobs or raise average incomes substantially. Most successful programs combine credit with business training, savings products, and insurance.

## How It's Best Learned
Study the Grameen Bank model and its global expansion. Review RCTs from Mexico, India, and Africa comparing treated and control villages. Analyze the microfinance crisis in India.

## Explainer

From your study of poverty traps, you know that poor households often cannot make productive investments because they lack access to credit — the classic threshold problem where a small amount of capital could launch a business, but banks will not lend to people without collateral. **Microfinance** emerged as an elegant proposed solution: give very small loans to very poor people, use social pressure instead of collateral to ensure repayment, and let entrepreneurship lift borrowers out of poverty. The idea won Muhammad Yunus the Nobel Peace Prize and spawned thousands of institutions serving hundreds of millions of borrowers worldwide.

The original **Grameen Bank model**, developed in Bangladesh in the 1970s, had several key innovations. Loans went to groups of five borrowers who were jointly liable — if one defaulted, the others had to cover the debt. This **group lending** mechanism substituted social monitoring for collateral: borrowers screened each other before forming groups, monitored each other's investments, and pressured each other to repay. The bank also required weekly repayment meetings, starting repayment almost immediately after disbursement, which created discipline and allowed early detection of problems. Interest rates were high by developed-country standards (20–30% annually) but far below the 50–100% charged by informal moneylenders.

The promise was transformative: credit access would unlock entrepreneurial potential, creating businesses, raising incomes, and enabling the poor to save their way out of poverty. But rigorous evidence tells a more nuanced story. A wave of **randomized controlled trials** across six countries (India, Morocco, Mongolia, Mexico, Ethiopia, and Bosnia) found consistent patterns: microfinance modestly increases self-employment and business investment among borrowers who are already entrepreneurially inclined, but it does not raise average household income, consumption, or measures of well-being significantly. Most borrowers use loans for consumption smoothing — managing cash flow gaps — rather than launching new enterprises. The adverse selection problem you studied helps explain this: those most eager to borrow at high interest rates are not always those with the best investment opportunities.

The sobering lesson is that credit access alone does not solve poverty. The most effective programs have evolved beyond pure microcredit to offer **bundled services**: savings accounts (which many poor households value more than loans), microinsurance against health or agricultural shocks, and business training to improve the quality of investments. The "graduation approach" — pioneered by BRAC in Bangladesh — combines a productive asset transfer, consumption support, savings facilitation, skills training, and health services in a coordinated package. RCTs of this bundled approach show substantially larger and more sustained impacts than credit alone, suggesting that the binding constraint for the poorest is not just missing credit but a cluster of simultaneous deprivations that must be addressed together.
