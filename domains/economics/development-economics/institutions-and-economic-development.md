# Institutions as Drivers of Development Divergence

## Core Idea
Cross-country divergence in wealth is largely explained by institutional quality: rule of law, contract enforcement, protection from expropriation, and political stability. Weak institutions undermine investment, induce capital flight, and enable rent-seeking. Institutional reform is slow but is foundational for sustained development.

## Explainer

From your study of property rights and incentives, you know the basic logic: people invest, innovate, and trade when they expect to keep the fruits of their effort. **Institutions** are the rules — formal laws, regulations, and constitutions, as well as informal norms, customs, and enforcement practices — that determine whether this expectation holds. The central claim of institutional economics is that these rules explain more of the variation in prosperity across countries than geography, culture, or resource endowments.

Consider two entrepreneurs, one in a country with strong institutions and one in a country with weak ones. Both have the same idea and the same talent. The first can register a business in days, enforce contracts through independent courts, and trust that the government will not arbitrarily seize her profits. The second faces months of bureaucratic approvals (each requiring bribes), has no reliable way to enforce contracts if a partner cheats, and knows that a change in government could mean expropriation. The first invests and grows; the second either stays small, operates informally, or moves her capital abroad. Multiply this across millions of decisions, and you get the divergence in national wealth that we observe.

The empirical evidence is striking. Acemoglu, Johnson, and Robinson's influential research exploited a natural experiment: European colonial powers established different institutions in different colonies depending on settler mortality. Where settlers could survive (temperate colonies like the US, Australia, New Zealand), they built **inclusive institutions** — property rights, political representation, constraints on executive power. Where settlers died quickly from tropical diseases (much of sub-Saharan Africa, Southeast Asia), they built **extractive institutions** — designed to transfer resources to the colonial power with minimal investment in local governance. These institutional differences, established centuries ago, persist today and predict current income levels far better than geography or climate do.

Why are bad institutions so persistent? Because institutions create winners and losers, and the winners use their power to preserve the rules that benefit them. An elite that profits from corruption, monopoly, or resource extraction will resist reforms that would open the economy to competition. This is **institutional lock-in** — a country can be trapped in a low-growth equilibrium not because no one knows what good institutions look like, but because those in power have no incentive to implement them. Reform, when it happens, typically requires either a shift in the balance of political power (democratization, coalition-building among excluded groups) or external pressure (conditional aid, trade agreements). The lesson is that institutional reform is not a technical problem with a known solution — it is a political problem, and understanding the political economy of who benefits from the status quo is essential to changing it.
