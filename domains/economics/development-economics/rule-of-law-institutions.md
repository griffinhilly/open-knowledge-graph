# Rule of Law and Contract Enforcement

## Core Idea
Rule of law refers to predictable, impartial application of rules and reliable contract enforcement. Reliable courts encourage long-term firm investment; weak judicial systems with corruption restrict firms to informal arrangements with trusted partners, limiting scale and complexity. Strong rule of law environments exhibit higher investment rates and faster structural transformation.

## Explainer

From your prerequisite work on institutions and economic development, you know that institutions shape the rules of the game — the incentives and constraints under which economic actors operate. Rule of law is one of the most critical institutional dimensions: it asks whether those rules are applied predictably and impartially, or whether outcomes depend on who you know or who you bribe. Without predictability, the expected return on any investment becomes uncertain not just due to market conditions, but due to the arbitrariness of the legal environment itself.

The economic mechanism is most visible in contracts. Imagine you are a manufacturer considering whether to invest in specialized machinery for a customer who has agreed to buy your output. If courts reliably enforce that contract, your investment is protected — you can sue for damages if the customer walks away. But if courts are slow, corrupt, or favor politically connected parties, the contract provides no real security. Rational firms respond by avoiding specialization, keeping transactions short-term and reversible, and dealing only with parties they personally trust. This is not inefficiency by mistake; it is a rational response to institutional weakness. The result is firms that stay small, refuse to scale, and avoid complex supply chains — exactly what stunts structural transformation.

The concept of **contract enforcement** generalizes beyond bilateral deals. Property rights are contracts between individuals and the state: the state agrees not to expropriate your assets and to defend your ownership against others. When this commitment is credible, firms invest in durable capital, innovate, and accept thin margins on long-horizon projects. When it is not — when governments unpredictably nationalize industries, invalidate licenses, or selectively enforce laws — the risk-adjusted return on investment collapses. Cross-country data consistently show that property-rights protection is among the strongest correlates of long-run income per capita, stronger even than geographic or natural-resource endowments.

The interaction between rule of law and informal institutions is important for understanding variation within weak-institutional environments. Where formal courts fail, businesses substitute kinship networks, ethnic communities, trade associations, and reputation mechanisms. These informal substitutes function but impose a ceiling: you can only transact with parties whose informal enforcement network overlaps with yours. This is why firms in weak-rule-of-law countries cluster in ethnically homogeneous supply chains and why market integration across regions is often low. The informal networks solve enforcement locally but cannot scale nationally or internationally — a binding constraint on growth. Strengthening formal rule of law expands the radius of trust and the scope of achievable transactions.
