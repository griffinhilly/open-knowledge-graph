# Lead Sheet Notation

## Core Idea
A lead sheet presents the essential information needed to perform a song: the melody in standard notation, chord symbols above the staff indicating the harmonic structure, and often lyrics below the staff. Lead sheets are the primary notation format in jazz and popular music, conveying enough structure to allow coherent performance while leaving interpretive details — voicing, rhythm, arrangement, and improvised content — to the performer. Reading and writing lead sheets requires fluency in both standard notation and the chord symbol system.

## How It's Best Learned
Transcribe a jazz standard from recording to lead sheet, noting decisions about chord symbol choice (Cmaj7 vs. C6) and how to represent rhythmically complex melodic figures. Then compose an original 32-bar AABA song and notate it as a lead sheet.

## Common Misconceptions
- Chord symbols in lead sheets indicate harmonic function, not exact voicing — performers are expected to voice chords idiomatically for their instrument.
- A lead sheet melody should capture the composed melody, not the improvised ornaments that appear in a particular recording.

## Explainer

A lead sheet is the jazz and popular music world's solution to a particular notation problem: how do you write down a song concisely enough for a working musician to read at a glance, while leaving room for the interpretation and improvisation that make each performance alive? The answer is a deliberate division of labor. The **melody** is written out in full — the composed tune belongs on the page. The **harmony** is compressed into chord symbols that any trained musician can translate into appropriate voicings for their instrument. Everything else — arrangement, rhythm, texture, ornamentation — belongs to the performer.

From your work with jazz chord symbols, you already know how to read and write the harmonic shorthand: Cmaj7 means a C major seventh chord, Dm7b5 means D minor with a flat five and a minor seventh, and so on. The skill of lead sheet writing is deciding which chord symbol best represents a moment. Sometimes the answer is unambiguous; other times you must choose between two plausible readings of the same harmony. A pianist voicing Cmaj7 and one voicing C6 will sound similar but not identical — and your choice signals your intent about the character of the moment.

Melodic notation on a lead sheet demands care at two levels. First, you are capturing the **composed melody**, not the particular performance you transcribed from. A Miles Davis recording of a standard contains countless expressive ornaments, rhythmic displacements, and improvisational touches; your lead sheet should give the essential melody that any musician could interpret in their own style. Second, the rhythmic notation must be accurate enough to convey the rhythmic character of the tune — a syncopated jazz melody notated in straight quarter notes loses something essential. This is where fluency in standard notation earns its keep.

The **layout conventions** of lead sheets follow from their performance context. Chord symbols appear above the staff, aligned with the beat where the chord changes. Lyrics, when present, go below the staff, syllable-by-syllable beneath their notes. Repeat signs, D.C. and D.S. markings, and first/second ending brackets communicate the song's form — the AABA, ABAC, or verse-chorus structures you studied in song form. A clean, well-formatted lead sheet is a communication artifact: its job is to transmit the song's essential information to a stranger who will sight-read it in a rehearsal. Clarity and accuracy serve that goal; decoration does not.
