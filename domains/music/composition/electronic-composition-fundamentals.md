# Electronic Composition Basics

## Core Idea
Electronic composition involves creating music using synthesizers, computers, and digital audio tools, unlocking entirely new sonic possibilities unavailable in acoustic composition. Understanding sound synthesis, digital audio processing, and the unique affordances of the electronic medium opens fresh compositional approaches. Electronic composition follows different aesthetic and technical rules than acoustic composition, requiring composers to think about timbre, texture, temporal form, and listener attention in innovative ways.

## Explainer

Everything you know from acoustic composition — melody, rhythm, harmony, form — still applies in electronic music, but the medium fundamentally changes what you start from. In acoustic writing, timbre is a consequence of instrumentation: you pick the instrument, and its sound is mostly fixed. In electronic composition, **timbre is a compositional parameter** from the outset. You design the sound itself — its attack shape, spectral content, decay, and texture — before a single note is placed. This means your sound design decisions and your compositional decisions are inseparable.

The foundation is synthesis: the process of generating sound electronically rather than recording it. From your prerequisite work in sound design, you know that **subtractive synthesis** carves a harmonically rich waveform down to shape by filtering, while **additive synthesis** builds a sound by stacking sine waves, and **FM synthesis** produces complex timbres through frequency modulation. Each synthesis method has characteristic sonic profiles that shape what kinds of textures are natural or unnatural to achieve — a composer working fluently in the medium chooses synthesis approaches that serve the expressive goal rather than fighting the tool's tendencies.

Digital audio processing extends this vocabulary. **Effects chains** (reverb, delay, distortion, granular processing) are not decorations applied after composing — they are compositional materials. A long reverb tail creates implied harmonic continuity; a granular stretcher can turn a single recorded sound into an evolving texture that sustains for minutes. The electronic composer thinks of the **signal chain** — the path a sound takes through synthesis, processing, and mixing — as a compositional instrument in itself. Automating parameters over time (filter cutoff sweeping, delay feedback increasing) creates movement and development in ways that have no acoustic equivalent.

What replaces melodic and harmonic development in some electronic works is **textural and spectral development**: sounds that evolve in density, brightness, spatial positioning, and rhythmic complexity over time. Listener attention in electronic music often tracks these timbral changes rather than following a singable line. This demands a different kind of formal thinking: how do you create tension and release, arrival and departure, when the primary material is not a melody but a texture? Electronic composers typically work with **layers** — independent streams of sound that combine, separate, fade in and out — and manage the energy of a piece through how many layers are active at once, how dense or sparse each is, and how they interact spatially across the stereo or surround field. Bringing these tools together — synthesis, processing, layering, automation — is what electronic composition fundamentals prepare you to do deliberately rather than accidentally.
