# Electroacoustic Composition and Digital Sound Design

## Core Idea
Electroacoustic composition treats digital processes—granulation, convolution, spectral transformation—as compositional tools. Form emerges from sound manipulation rather than traditional harmonic and melodic development. Analyzing electroacoustic works requires understanding both signal processing and aesthetic intention.

## Explainer

In acoustic music, composers work with notes: discrete pitches, durations, and timbres governed by the physics of instruments. You learned how timbre is structured in the frequency domain — a clarinet's distinctive sound arises from the particular balance of its harmonics. Electroacoustic composition takes that frequency-domain understanding and makes it a compositional raw material. Instead of choosing which notes to write, the composer asks: how can I transform the spectral content of a sound, or construct new timbres entirely from digital processes? The studio becomes an instrument, and signal processing operations become compositional gestures.

Three operations form the core toolkit. **Granular synthesis** fragments a sound into tiny grains (typically 10–100 milliseconds), then reconstructs or reorganizes them — stretching time without altering pitch, or scattering sound into a cloud of microsounds. A single held piano note can be granulated into a shimmering texture that bears almost no resemblance to its source. **Convolution** blends the impulse response of one acoustic environment (a cathedral, a drainpipe) with a completely different sound source, wrapping a dry recorded voice in the reverberant bloom of a space it was never recorded in. **Spectral transformation** — including pitch shifting, vocoding, and cross-synthesis — operates directly on frequency-domain representations, morphing one sound's spectrum into another's or allowing the harmonics of a human voice to carry the resonance pattern of a violin. Each operation transforms what you hear at the level of timbre and texture rather than at the level of melody or harmony.

Form in electroacoustic music is generated by the arc of these transformations over time. Where a tonal piece might move through a cycle of fifths or develop a motivic cell, an electroacoustic work might begin with recognizable source material (a field recording, a spoken voice), progressively disintegrate it through granulation, then gradually reconstitute a new spectral identity through resynthesis. The concept of **acousmatic music** — sound without visible source — is central: the listener cannot see who or what is making the sound, which heightens focus on the intrinsic qualities of the sound itself and the transformations it undergoes. Works like Stockhausen's *Gesang der Jünglinge* or Bernard Parmegiani's *De Natura Sonorum* demonstrate how vast formal arcs can be built from purely sonic materials.

Analyzing electroacoustic works requires listening analytically for processes rather than themes. Instead of asking "what key is this in?" or "what is the melodic contour?", you ask: "what is the source material?", "what transformation is being applied?", "how is the texture evolving?", and "what is the relationship between recognizable and unrecognizable sound?" A good score or analysis typically includes a **spectrogram** — a visual representation of frequency content over time — which lets you see structural divisions and transformations that are invisible in standard notation. The aesthetic intention behind each transformation is inseparable from its technical execution: the composer chooses not just what process to apply but at what rate, to what degree, and in service of what expressive goal.
