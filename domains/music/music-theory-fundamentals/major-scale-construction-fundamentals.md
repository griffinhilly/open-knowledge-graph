# Constructing Major Scales

## Core Idea
All major scales follow the same pattern of whole and half steps: whole-whole-half-whole-whole-whole-half (W-W-H-W-W-W-H). This interval pattern, starting from any pitch, produces a major scale. For example, C major is C-D-E-F-G-A-B-C, while G major is G-A-B-C-D-E-F#-G. Understanding this pattern allows you to construct any major scale.

## How It's Best Learned
Build major scales starting from different pitches using a keyboard or staff paper. Write out several scales (C, F, G, D) and compare them. Listen to major scales to internalize the sound.

## Common Misconceptions
Students often think all scales use the same notes (forgetting about sharps and flats). Another common error is misremembering the exact pattern—writing W-H-W-W-W-H-W instead of W-W-H-W-W-W-H.

## Explainer

You already know that a **whole step** is two half steps and a **half step** is the smallest interval on a standard keyboard — the distance from one key to the very next key, with no key in between. The major scale is built by applying a specific sequence of those intervals, starting from any pitch you choose. That sequence is **W–W–H–W–W–W–H**: two whole steps, a half step, three whole steps, and a final half step. What makes this pattern remarkable is that it always produces the same characteristic sound — the bright, stable quality you associate with "do-re-mi" — regardless of where you begin.

Start on C and apply the pattern: C to D (W), D to E (W), E to F (H), F to G (W), G to A (W), A to B (W), B to C (H). You get C–D–E–F–G–A–B–C — the white keys of a piano, the C major scale. No sharps or flats needed because the natural half steps on the piano keyboard (E–F and B–C) happen to fall exactly where the pattern demands them. This is why C major is the "easy" scale: the instrument's layout and the pattern's demands align perfectly.

Now start on G and apply the same pattern: G to A (W), A to B (W), B to C (H) — so far so good — C to D (W), D to E (W), E to F#... wait. The pattern demands a whole step from E, but E to F is only a half step. So we must raise the F to F#, making the interval whole. G major is G–A–B–C–D–E–F#–G. The pattern forces the sharp. This is the essential insight: **sharps and flats in a key signature are not arbitrary** — they are the adjustments required to maintain the W–W–H–W–W–W–H pattern from a given starting pitch. Every scale except C major will need at least one accidental.

The pattern has a deeper logic worth internalizing. The two half steps (positions 3–4 and 7–8) create the scale's emotional character. The half step between scale degrees 7 and 8 gives the **leading tone** — scale degree 7 — its strong pull toward the octave. That one half step is much of what makes major scale-based melody feel goal-directed, wanting to resolve. The other half step, between 3 and 4, stabilizes the middle of the scale around the third and fourth degrees. Together, these two anchored half steps give the major scale its distinctive mix of brightness and resolution. Once you can build the scale from any root, you'll also start to see why keys with more sharps or flats are not "harder" harmonically — they are simply starting the same pattern from a different rung of the chromatic ladder.
