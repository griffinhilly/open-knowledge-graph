# Seventh Chord Construction: Types and Qualities

## Core Idea
Seventh chords extend triads by adding a seventh above the root. The five common seventh chord types are major seventh (major triad + major seventh), dominant seventh (major triad + minor seventh), minor seventh (minor triad + minor seventh), half-diminished (diminished triad + minor seventh), and fully diminished (diminished triad + diminished seventh).

## How It's Best Learned
Build seventh chords on a staff starting from major and minor triads. Play and listen to each type to internalize their distinct qualities and harmonic functions.

## Common Misconceptions
A dominant seventh is not built from the dominant scale—it is a major triad built on scale degree V plus a minor seventh above the root, creating tritone tension.

## Explainer

You already know how to build major, minor, and diminished triads by stacking thirds above a root. A **seventh chord** extends that stack one step further: you add another third above the fifth, producing a four-note chord. That added note is called the seventh because it sits a seventh interval above the root. What varies across the five common seventh chord types is the quality of the underlying triad and the quality of the seventh itself — and those two variables combine to give each type its distinctive sound and function.

The easiest to understand is the **major seventh chord** (Cmaj7, written as CΔ7 in jazz): a major triad with a major seventh stacked on top. In C major, that's C–E–G–B. The major seventh (B) is just a half step below the octave C, which creates a lush, slightly suspended quality — the chord sounds stable but with a gentle shimmer. You hear this color constantly in jazz ballads and bossa nova. Contrast it with the **minor seventh chord** (Cm7): a minor triad with a minor seventh. C–Eb–G–Bb. The minor third gives it darkness; the minor seventh (a whole step below the octave) rounds off the sound without the pulling tension of a leading tone. Minor sevenths are the workhorses of jazz harmony and appear on scale degrees ii, iii, and vi in a major key.

The most functionally important type is the **dominant seventh** (C7): a major triad with a minor seventh. C–E–G–Bb. What makes this chord so powerful is the **tritone** embedded within it: the interval from the major third (E) to the minor seventh (Bb) spans exactly three whole steps, the most dissonant interval in tonal music. That tritone wants urgently to resolve — the E pulls up toward F, the Bb pulls down toward A — which is why dominant sevenths built on scale degree V create such strong pressure to resolve to the tonic. Every authentic cadence (V7–I) exploits this tension.

The two diminished variants sit at the more dissonant end of the spectrum. A **half-diminished chord** (Cø7) combines a diminished triad with a minor seventh: C–Eb–Gb–Bb. The diminished fifth in the triad creates instability, but the minor seventh softens the chord just enough to function elegantly — you'll find half-diminished chords commonly on scale degree ii in minor keys and as passing harmonies in jazz. A **fully diminished chord** (C°7) stacks a diminished triad with a diminished seventh (a minor seventh lowered by a half step, enharmonically the same as a major sixth): C–Eb–Gb–A. Every interval in this chord is a minor third, giving it perfect symmetry and maximum ambiguity — it can resolve to four different keys, making it a favorite for dramatic harmonic pivots. The fully diminished chord is the most unstable of the five types; it demands resolution almost more than it establishes identity.
