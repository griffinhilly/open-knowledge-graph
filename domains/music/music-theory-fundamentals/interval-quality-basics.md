# Interval Quality Basics

## Core Idea
After counting the generic interval name, interval quality describes whether it's perfect, major, minor, augmented, or diminished. Perfect intervals (unison, 4th, 5th, octave) are either perfect or altered. Major intervals (2nd, 3rd, 6th, 7th) are major, minor, or altered. Quality is determined by comparing the interval to the major scale.

## How It's Best Learned
Build intervals from the major scale pattern, identifying each as major or perfect. Practice recognizing intervals by ear. Build intervals on keyboard to understand semitone relationships.

## Common Misconceptions
Thinking all intervals come in major and minor (perfect intervals don't). Confusing harmonic and melodic intervals regarding quality. Miscounting semitones when determining quality.

## Explainer

When you first learn to name intervals, you count the number of letter names spanned — that gives you the generic name: 2nd, 3rd, 4th, 5th, and so on. But two intervals can have the same generic name and sound completely different. A 3rd from C to E and a 3rd from C to Eb are both 3rds, yet one feels bright and open while the other feels darker and more inward. That difference is what **quality** captures.

The first step is learning which generic intervals belong to which quality family. Unisons, 4ths, 5ths, and octaves are **perfect intervals** — they form a separate family from the rest. 2nds, 3rds, 6ths, and 7ths are **major/minor intervals**. This distinction is not arbitrary: perfect intervals correspond to the simplest frequency ratios (2:1 for the octave, 3:2 for the fifth, 4:3 for the fourth) and were considered acoustically "complete" by medieval theorists. The quality vocabulary follows from that historical classification, so it is worth memorizing which group each generic number belongs to before anything else.

To find the quality of any specific interval, use the major scale as your reference. Build a major scale starting on the lower note. Each scale degree creates an interval with the tonic: C to D is a major 2nd, C to E is a major 3rd, C to F is a perfect 4th, C to G is a perfect 5th, and so on. These are your baselines. If the upper note sits one half step below the major-scale version, a major interval becomes **minor** (C to Eb is a minor 3rd). If a perfect interval shrinks by one half step, it becomes **diminished** (C to Gb is a diminished 5th). If any interval grows one half step beyond its baseline, it becomes **augmented** (C to F# is an augmented 4th).

A reliable trap to watch for: students often say "major 5th" or "minor 4th" because major and minor are the more familiar terms. But 5ths and 4ths live in the perfect family — those labels simply do not apply. If you catch yourself writing "minor 4th," it is a signal to check which family the interval belongs to before assigning quality.

Once you combine quality and number — "major 3rd," "perfect 5th," "minor 7th" — you have a precise, unambiguous label for any interval. This system underlies every aspect of tonal music theory that follows: chord construction, scale analysis, and harmonic analysis all build directly on interval quality. Fluency here pays dividends across the entire curriculum.
