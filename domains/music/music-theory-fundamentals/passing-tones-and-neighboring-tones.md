# Passing Tones and Neighbor Tones: Melodic Decoration

## Core Idea
Passing tones and neighbor tones are non-chord tones that decorate a melody between harmonic changes. A passing tone connects two chord tones by step (moving up or down), while a neighbor tone (also called a turning tone) steps away from a chord tone and returns to it. Both types occur on weaker beats and create smooth, conjunct motion.

## How It's Best Learned
Analyze melodies to identify passing and neighbor tones. Create simple melodies using only chord tones, then add decoration with passing and neighbor tones, and compare the resulting smoothness.

## Common Misconceptions
A non-chord tone is not a mistake—it is a compositional tool used intentionally to create melodic interest and smooth voice leading.

## Questions

```yaml
- question: "In a melody over a C major chord (C–E–G), the melody moves E → F → G. What type of non-chord tone is the F, and why?"
  type: multiple-choice
  options:
    - "Neighbor tone — F steps away from E and returns to E"
    - "Passing tone — F moves by step between two chord tones, E and G"
    - "Chord tone — F is in the key and belongs to the harmony"
    - "Suspension — F is held over from the previous chord before resolving"
  answer: 1
  explanation: "A passing tone moves by step between two different chord tones — it 'passes through' on the way from one to the other. Here E (chord tone) → F (non-chord tone) → G (chord tone) is a classic passing tone: F fills in the step between E and G, both of which belong to the C major chord. A neighbor tone, by contrast, steps away and returns to the *same* pitch. The F here is directional — it is going somewhere new — which is the defining feature of a passing tone."

- question: "A composer writes the melody C → B → C over a C major chord, with B on a weak beat. What type of non-chord tone is the B?"
  type: multiple-choice
  options:
    - "Passing tone — it passes between two different chord tones"
    - "Suspension — B is held over and resolves down to the next chord"
    - "Neighbor tone — it steps away from C and returns to the same C"
    - "Escape tone — it moves by step and resolves by leap"
  answer: 2
  explanation: "A neighbor tone steps away from a chord tone and returns to that *same* chord tone. C → B → C shows the melody departing from C by a half step (lower neighbor) and returning to C. No new chord tone is being approached — the function is purely decorative, adding a small inflection to the sustained C. A passing tone would move from one chord tone to a *different* chord tone, with the non-chord tone bridging the gap. The B here is a lower neighbor tone."

- question: "Both passing tones and neighbor tones typically occur on strong beats to give them harmonic emphasis."
  type: true-false
  answer: false
  explanation: "Both types occur on *weak* beats — this is a defining characteristic. Placing a non-chord tone on a strong beat creates a dissonance where the harmony is most exposed, which sounds like a mistake rather than smooth decoration. The weak-beat placement is what makes passing and neighbor tones ornamental: the ear hears them as connecting gestures between the chord tones on strong beats. A non-chord tone on a strong beat that then resolves is a different device (suspension or appoggiatura), not a passing or neighbor tone."

- question: "A non-chord tone in a melody always indicates a harmonization error — the composer should have used a chord that makes the note consonant."
  type: true-false
  answer: false
  explanation: "This is the core misconception the topic addresses: non-chord tones are intentional compositional tools, not errors. Passing tones and neighbor tones are used deliberately to create smooth, stepwise motion between chord tones — they are what makes melodies singable and flowing rather than arpeggiated and angular. Every tonal melody uses non-chord tones as decoration. The skill is using them correctly (on weak beats, moving by step, between chord tones) so they read as smooth decoration rather than unwanted dissonance."

- question: "What is the functional difference between a passing tone and a neighbor tone, and what melodic situation calls for each?"
  type: short-answer
  answer: "A passing tone connects two different chord tones by step — it passes through between them (e.g., E → F → G over C major). Use a passing tone when you want smooth stepwise motion between two chord tones a third apart. A neighbor tone steps away from a chord tone and returns to the same one (e.g., C → B → C). Use a neighbor tone when you want to add melodic decoration to a repeated or sustained pitch without moving to a new harmonic target. Both occur on weak beats, but their melodic destinations differ: passing tones go somewhere new, neighbor tones circle back."
  explanation: "The two devices solve different melodic problems. Passing tones enable smooth motion across a harmonic gap; without them, the melody would have to leap the third directly. Neighbor tones add life to stationary pitches; without them, a repeated note on a long chord would feel static. Together, they give the composer a vocabulary for decorating the harmonic skeleton with smooth, vocally natural motion."
```
