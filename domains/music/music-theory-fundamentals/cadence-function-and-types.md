# Cadences: Function and Types

## Core Idea
Cadences are harmonic progressions that conclude phrases or sections, signaling closure (full cadence) or continuation (half cadence). An authentic cadence (V-I) ends decisively at home; a plagal cadence (IV-I) provides a gentler conclusion; a half cadence (any chord-V) leaves the listener suspended in anticipation. Understanding cadence types allows you to recognize formal structures and the emotional weight of endings.

## Explainer

You already know the primary harmonic functions: tonic (home), dominant (tension pointing home), and subdominant (departing or softening). A **cadence** is simply the moment where those functions arrive at a point of rest or suspension — the harmonic equivalent of a sentence ending with a period, comma, or question mark. Just as punctuation controls the rhythm of prose, cadences control musical phrasing: they tell the listener whether a musical thought is complete, paused, or still open.

The most decisive ending is the **authentic cadence**: V moving to I. The dominant chord contains the leading tone — the seventh scale degree, which sits a half-step below the tonic and creates strong upward pull — along with the fifth-scale-degree bass that moves down a fifth to the tonic. When both of these resolutions happen together, the arrival on I feels conclusive. A **perfect authentic cadence (PAC)** adds two further conditions: both the V and the I chords are in root position (the chord root in the bass), and the top voice ends on the tonic pitch. This is the strongest cadence in tonal music — the full stop. A **imperfect authentic cadence (IAC)** still moves V to I but with a different inversion or melodic ending, creating a softer, less final arrival.

The **half cadence** ends on V rather than resolving to I, leaving the phrase open. Musically, this sounds like a held breath or a question waiting for an answer. It's extremely common at the midpoint of a period — a paired phrase structure where the first phrase ends with a half cadence and the second phrase ends with an authentic cadence, the two together forming a complete musical thought. When you hear a half cadence, you're conditioned to expect that the music will continue and eventually resolve the tension. Composers exploit this expectation constantly: they may delay the authentic cadence, digress harmonically, or make the final arrival feel earned through extended tension.

The **plagal cadence** (IV-I) has a softer, more devotional quality — you know it as the "Amen" cadence at the end of hymns. The subdominant function doesn't have the leading-tone urgency of the dominant, so the arrival on tonic feels peaceful rather than dramatic. Cadences are thus not just theoretical labels — they are expressive tools. Learning to recognize them by ear trains your ear to track harmonic momentum, to feel where music is going and when it has arrived. The next step in your study, phrase structure, depends entirely on being able to hear these endpoints reliably.
