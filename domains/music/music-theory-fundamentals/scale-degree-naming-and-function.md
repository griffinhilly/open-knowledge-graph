# Scale Degree Naming and Function

## Core Idea
Scale degrees are numbered positions within a scale (1–7), each with a functional name: tonic, supertonic, mediant, subdominant, dominant, submediant, and leading tone. These names remain constant across all major keys, helping musicians understand each pitch's role in a tonal context. Understanding scale degrees is essential for analyzing chord progressions and voice leading.

## How It's Best Learned
Memorize the scale degree numbers and names in one key (e.g., C major), then transpose to others. Practice identifying scale degrees by ear and labeling chord roots in progressions using degree names.

## Common Misconceptions
Scale degree number is not the same as the note's position in staff order. Scale degree names don't change between keys—they're relative positions. The leading tone (7) always pulls toward the tonic.

## Explainer

You know what a major scale is: a sequence of seven pitches following the pattern W-W-H-W-W-W-H (whole and half steps), creating a recognizable "do-re-mi" sound. Scale degree naming takes the next step: it gives each position in that sequence a **functional identity** that persists across all keys. Whether you're in C major or F♯ major, the first degree is the **tonic** — the home pitch, the point of rest and arrival. The seventh degree is always the **leading tone** — the pitch that feels compelled to rise by half step to the tonic. These functions don't change with the key; they're properties of position, not of specific pitch frequencies.

The seven degree names encode their musical roles. The **tonic** (1) is home base. The **supertonic** (2) sits just above the tonic and tends toward the dominant. The **mediant** (3) is in the middle — the third of the tonic chord. The **subdominant** (4) sits a fifth below the tonic (*sub* = below, *dominant* = the fifth) and creates tension away from home. The **dominant** (5) is the most harmonically active degree after the tonic, creating the strongest pull back toward rest. The **submediant** (6) is a third below the tonic. The **leading tone** (7), only a half step below the tonic, has the most urgent upward pull of any scale degree — it "leads" into the tonic with almost magnetic urgency, and this urgency is why the major scale uses a raised seventh degree rather than a minor seventh.

These names are the vocabulary of tonal music analysis. When a musician says "the progression goes subdominant to dominant to tonic," she's describing a IV–V–I movement — a pattern that appears in virtually every genre of Western tonal music, from Bach to the Beatles. The same progression transposed to any key sounds functionally identical because the scale degree relationships are preserved. This is the power of understanding function rather than specific notes: it lets you recognize the same musical event across all twelve keys, making transposition, modulation, and harmonic analysis vastly more tractable. Once you internalize these roles, you can hear a chord not just as a collection of pitches but as a statement of harmonic position — home, tension, or somewhere in between.
