# Voice Independence and Counterpoint by Ear

## Core Idea
Counterpoint involves multiple melodic lines sounding simultaneously while maintaining their individual melodic independence. Voice independence means each voice has its own distinctive contour, rhythmic profile, and direction; the harmonic result emerges from the combination of independent lines rather than being predetermined. Hearing counterpoint requires developing figure-ground discrimination and understanding how voice-leading choices create specific harmonic and textural effects.

## How It's Best Learned
Listen to two-part inventions by Bach, focusing on one voice completely, then the other, then both together. Practice identifying oblique, parallel, and contrary motion between voices. Transcribe simplified two-part counterpoint where one voice is already clear.

## Common Misconceptions
Thinking counterpoint is about creating harmony first and fitting voices around it (counterpoint is voices first, harmony emerges). Assuming all simultaneous notes create correct counterpoint—voice independence and efficient voice-leading are prerequisites. Focusing on harmonic result rather than understanding how the independent lines create it.

  answer: 1
  explanation: "This is the central misconception about counterpoint. Contrapuntal thinking is voices-first: each melodic line is composed to have its own compelling melodic identity — shape, rhythm, direction — and the harmonic intervals that result are emergent, not predetermined. Starting from a chord progression and filling in voices produces harmonized parts, not true counterpoint. The harmonic chord changes arise as a byproduct of independent voice movement, not as the organizing principle driving it."

- question: "While listening to a Bach two-part invention, a student notices that the two voices frequently move in opposite directions. This contrary motion is best understood as:"
  type: multiple-choice
  options:
    - "A technique for avoiding dissonance by keeping the voices physically separated"
    - "An artifact of rules that forbid parallel perfect intervals"
    - "Evidence of voice independence — each line pursues its own melodic trajectory, resulting in contrasting contours"
    - "A way to emphasize harmonic changes by moving both voices to new chord tones simultaneously"
  answer: 2
  explanation: "Contrary motion is one of the clearest audible signs of voice independence: the two lines are not moving as a unit but following their own melodic trajectories. When voices move in contrary motion, each line has its own contour and direction, making them perceptually distinct. While contrary motion also helps avoid parallel fifths and octaves, the deeper reason it characterizes good counterpoint is that it reflects genuine melodic independence — both voices are behaving as separate melodies, not as harmonic parts."

- question: "In well-composed counterpoint, the harmonic intervals between voices are determined by the independent melodic movement of each line rather than being chosen in advance."
  type: true-false
  answer: true
  explanation: "This is the foundational principle of counterpoint as a compositional approach. The voices move according to their own melodic logic — shaping phrases, choosing smooth connections, maintaining characteristic rhythmic profiles — and the vertical intervals (thirds, sixths, dissonances, resolutions) result from that independent motion. This is why counterpoint is fundamentally different from homophonic writing, where voices are subordinated to a harmonic plan."

- question: "A skilled listener who understands counterpoint should hear the two voices of a Bach invention as a unified single texture, since that is how the composer intended it to be experienced."
  type: true-false
  answer: false
  explanation: "This reverses the listening goal for counterpoint. The perceptual skill required is figure-ground discrimination: the ability to hear each voice as a distinct melodic entity while also tracking their combination. Hearing counterpoint as a single undifferentiated texture means losing precisely the voice independence the composer worked to preserve. The trained ear can shift attention between voices, follow each one's melodic narrative independently, and then hear the whole. Losing individual voice identities in a blur of texture is a sign of insufficient contrapuntal listening."

- question: "Why does the counterpoint principle 'voices first, harmony emerges' matter for ear training? What specifically are you listening for when analyzing two-part counterpoint by ear?"
  type: short-answer
  answer: "Because harmony is emergent in counterpoint, you must listen to each voice as an independent melody — tracking its contour, rhythm, and motion — rather than just identifying chord labels. When analyzing by ear, you listen for: how each voice moves on its own (leaps, steps, rhythmic character, phrasing), how the voices relate in motion type (contrary, oblique, parallel, or similar), and how their independent movement creates dissonances and resolutions. The goal is sustaining awareness of two melodic streams simultaneously while remaining attentive to each."
  explanation: "The practical listening skill is selective auditory attention — the ability to focus on one voice without losing the other, then integrating both. This differs from listening for harmony, where you identify the chord at each vertical moment. In contrapuntal listening, the chord is secondary; what matters is how each voice earns its movement through melodic logic and how their independence produces both melodic interest in each line and harmonic tension-and-resolution in their combination."
