# Scale Degree Identification and Functional Hearing

## Core Idea
Each scale degree has a distinct functional identity within a key: do (1) is tonic (home), re (2) and fa (4) tend toward subdominant, sol (5) is dominant, and ti (7) is the leading tone pulling to tonic. Identifying scale degrees by ear trains functional tonal hearing—the ability to sense where you are in the key and what harmonic destination is implied. This is foundational for understanding harmonic analysis.

## How It's Best Learned
Sing scale degrees systematically within a major key, then hear them in harmonic and melodic contexts. Emphasize the stability of tonic and the pull of leading-tone to tonic.

## Common Misconceptions
Treating scale degrees as arbitrary labels rather than functional positions. Confusing relative-pitch identification (hearing scale degrees) with absolute-pitch identification (knowing the letter name). In minor keys, choosing which scale form to use (natural, harmonic, melodic) for solfège syllables.

## Questions

```yaml
- question: "A student hears a single note played over a C major tonic chord and identifies it as scale degree 7 (ti). What should the student expect this note to do harmonically?"
  type: multiple-choice
  options:
    - "Remain stable as an extension of the tonic harmony"
    - "Pull strongly upward by a half-step to scale degree 1 (do)"
    - "Fall to scale degree 5 (sol) as a common resolution"
    - "Have no particular tendency since scale degrees are just positional labels"
  answer: 1
  explanation: "The leading tone (scale degree 7, ti) is defined by its strong pull upward to tonic (scale degree 1, do) — it sits only a half-step below, creating the strongest possible resolution tendency in tonal music. This functional identity is why it is called the leading tone. Option D is the key misconception: scale degrees are not arbitrary positional labels but functional identities that imply harmonic direction."

- question: "A musician can identify every scale degree in a melody by ear. Does this mean the musician has absolute pitch?"
  type: multiple-choice
  options:
    - "Yes — knowing scale degrees requires knowing which absolute pitch each one is"
    - "No — scale degree identification is relative pitch: it only requires hearing where a note sits within the key, not its letter name"
    - "Yes, but only if the musician can identify scale degrees in multiple keys"
    - "No — scale degree identification requires no pitch perception at all, only rhythmic awareness"
  answer: 1
  explanation: "Scale degree identification is a relative pitch skill: it requires hearing how a note relates to the tonic, not knowing its absolute pitch-class (letter name). A musician with no absolute pitch can hear that a note is scale degree 3 in F# major without knowing that the note is A#. Absolute pitch — identifying notes as 'that's a G' without reference — is a separate and much rarer ability. The confusion between these two skills is a common misconception in ear training."

- question: "The leading tone (scale degree 7) has a stronger tendency toward tonic than scale degree 2, partly because it is only a half-step away rather than a whole step."
  type: true-false
  answer: true
  explanation: "The leading tone's pull toward tonic is one of the strongest tendencies in tonal music, and the half-step distance is a key reason. Half-step motion is the smallest melodic interval in Western tonal music, making ti→do feel like the shortest possible journey to resolution. Scale degree 2 (re) is a whole step above tonic and has some tendency toward tonic, but the pull is weaker. The leading tone's proximity creates an almost gravitational pull that drives harmonic resolution."

- question: "A musician who can identify scale degrees by ear in C major will need to develop a completely separate skill to identify scale degrees in F major."
  type: true-false
  answer: false
  explanation: "Scale degree identification is a transferable relative pitch skill. Once you internalize the functional identity of each scale degree — do as stable tonic, ti as the leading tone, sol as dominant — those identities hold in any major key. The physical pitches change when you change keys, but the relationships and functional characters remain the same. Thinking in scale degrees rather than letter names is what makes the skill generalize across all keys."

- question: "What does it mean to have 'functional tonal hearing,' and why does it matter more than simply knowing the names of scale degrees?"
  type: short-answer
  answer: "Functional tonal hearing means sensing where you are in the key and what harmonic motion is implied — not just labeling notes by position. When you hear ti (scale degree 7), functional hearing tells you 'this wants to resolve up to do'; when you hear sol (scale degree 5), you sense dominant tension implying return to tonic. This is different from simply knowing that the seventh note of a C major scale is called B. Functional hearing enables harmonic prediction, improvisation, and real-time analysis because you're tracking meaning and implication, not just position."
  explanation: "The distinction matters for performance and composition. A musician with functional hearing can anticipate chord changes, feel when a phrase is incomplete, and understand why certain note choices create tension or resolution. A musician who only knows scale degree names as positional labels has memorized a taxonomy without understanding the grammar — they can identify notes but can't yet hear the musical logic those notes participate in."
```
