# Chromatic Approach Notes in Voice Leading

## Core Idea
Chromatic approach notes approached by semitone from above or below create smooth chromatic voice leading. These can be added to basic diatonic voice leading to add interest and fluidity. Approach notes must resolve by step to the target note and typically occur on weak beats to avoid creating false harmonies.

## Explainer

Your voice-leading principles established the core grammar of smooth part-writing: voices move by step where possible, avoid parallel fifths and octaves, and resolve tendency tones (especially the leading tone and the seventh of dominant chords) in their expected directions. **Chromatic approach notes** extend this grammar into the half-step universe, adding a single non-diatonic pitch that leans into a target note from one semitone away. Because the half step is the most intense pull in tonal music — more urgent than a whole step, more directed than a leap — chromatic approach notes create heightened momentum toward their resolution.

The mechanism is straightforward: choose a target chord tone and approach it either from a **half step below** (a **lower chromatic neighbor**) or a **half step above** (an **upper chromatic neighbor**). In C major, if a voice is moving to E (the third of the tonic chord), a lower approach from Eb or an upper approach from F creates a chromatic slide into the target. These borrowed pitches — Eb and F are not both diatonic in C major — temporarily break the diatonic framework, but because they resolve immediately by half step to a chord tone, listeners hear them as ornamental rather than structurally significant. The key constraint is placement: approach notes belong on **weak beats or weak subdivisions**, so the target note lands on the strong beat. If an approach note arrives on a strong beat, it sounds like a chord tone itself — potentially implying a false harmony.

The two main types have distinct characters. A **lower chromatic approach** has the quality of a raised leading tone — tense, expectant, pulling upward. In jazz and bebop, lower approach notes are extremely common: a melodic line might approach every chord tone from a half step below in rapid succession, creating the characteristic "chromatic crawl" that defines bebop lines. An **upper chromatic approach** has more of a neighbor-note quality — like a brief upper appoggiatura resolving downward. You can also approach a target from **both sides simultaneously in different voices** (double chromatic approach), where one voice moves up a half step and another down, both landing on the same chord tone — a technique common in big band arranging.

The deeper principle is that **chromatic approach notes are voice-leading tools, not harmonic ones**. They do not create new chords; they create motion within existing chords. This is what separates an approach note from an actual chromatic harmony (like a borrowed chord or a secondary dominant). When you hear a brief dissonant pitch on a weak beat that immediately resolves by half step to a chord tone, that is an approach note — a surface ornament. When you hear that chromatic pitch sustained on a strong beat and harmonized by other voices that define a distinct chord quality, it has become structural. The ability to distinguish these two functions — ornamental approach vs. structural chord — is one of the central analytical skills in chromatic voice-leading analysis.
