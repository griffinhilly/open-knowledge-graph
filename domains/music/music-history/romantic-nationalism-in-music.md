# Musical Nationalism in the 19th Century

## Core Idea
Musical nationalism arose in the mid-19th century as composers sought to express national identities distinct from the dominant German tradition. Composers in Russia (Mussorgsky, Rimsky-Korsakov, the 'Mighty Five'), Bohemia (Smetana, Dvořák), Scandinavia (Grieg, Sibelius), Spain (Albéniz), and elsewhere incorporated folk melodies, dance rhythms, and national subjects into concert music. This movement both enriched the harmonic and melodic vocabulary of Western art music and reflected the broader political emergence of national states in 19th-century Europe. Folk elements often introduced modal scales, irregular meters, and pentatonic patterns that diversified the prevailing harmonic language.

## How It's Best Learned
Compare Smetana's Má vlast or Dvořák's New World Symphony with contemporaneous German Romantic works. Identifying the specific folk elements — modal inflections, characteristic dance patterns, programmatic national subjects — clarifies what 'nationalism' means as a musical phenomenon.

## Common Misconceptions
- Nationalist composers were not parochial or isolated — most were trained in the German tradition and were in conscious dialogue with it.
- Dvořák's New World Symphony incorporates African American and Native American influences encountered during his American residency, not exclusively Czech folk material.

## Explainer

From your study of the Romantic period, you know that the early Romantics — Schubert, Schumann, Chopin, Liszt — were largely working within a shared European art music tradition centered on German and Austro-Hungarian culture. By mid-century, however, a different impulse emerged: composers from peripheral nations began asking whether music could serve as a vehicle for national identity and cultural assertion. This wasn't purely aesthetic; it was political. The revolutions of 1848, the unification movements in Germany and Italy, the suppression of Slavic and Scandinavian cultures within multinational empires — all created pressure to articulate what it meant to be Czech, Russian, Norwegian, or Finnish through art.

The raw material was **folk music**: melodies, rhythms, and scales transmitted orally within peasant communities rather than through the written tradition of the conservatories. When Mussorgsky transcribed and harmonized Russian folk songs, or when Grieg built piano pieces around Norwegian folk dances, they were doing something technically interesting as well as politically meaningful. Folk music often used **modal scales** (neither major nor minor in the standard sense), **pentatonic patterns** (five-note scales common across many world traditions), and **irregular meters** that didn't fit neatly into the 3/4 or 4/4 grids of classical form. Incorporating this material into symphonies and tone poems didn't just add local color — it diversified the harmonic and rhythmic vocabulary of Western art music.

The **Russian Five** (Mussorgsky, Rimsky-Korsakov, Balakirev, Borodin, and Cui) were the most self-consciously nationalist composers of the movement. They distrusted the German academic tradition and sought a distinctly Russian musical voice: exotic harmonies, modal inflections, colorful orchestration, and programmatic subjects drawn from Russian history and folklore. Contrast them with Tchaikovsky, their contemporary, who was deeply trained in German classical forms and is often considered a Westernizer by comparison, even though he too used Russian folk elements. Smetana's cycle *Má vlast* (My Homeland) programmatically depicts Czech landscapes, rivers, and legends in purely orchestral terms — an assertion that Czech identity was fully capable of sustaining serious art music.

It is crucial to understand that nationalism was not about musical insularity. Dvořák studied in Prague and Vienna; Sibelius trained in Berlin and Vienna; Grieg knew Liszt personally. These composers were in dialogue with the German tradition — sometimes accepting its forms while filling them with national content, sometimes pushing against its conventions to make room for different musical logics. When Dvořák went to America and wrote the New World Symphony, he synthesized Czech folk influences with African American spirituals and Native American melodic contours he encountered during his residency. The result is neither purely Czech nor purely American, but a cosmopolitan synthesis — nationalism opened composers to other folk traditions precisely because it taught them to listen for non-canonical musical languages.

The legacy of musical nationalism runs directly into early modernism. The modal scales and irregular meters that Bartók and Stravinsky absorbed from their own folk-music fieldwork — Bartók in Hungary and Romania, Stravinsky in Russia — became tools for breaking the grip of late Romantic harmonic convention. Nationalism gave modernism much of its raw material: a library of non-Western-classical scales, rhythms, and forms that could be used to renovate a tradition whose internal development seemed exhausted.
