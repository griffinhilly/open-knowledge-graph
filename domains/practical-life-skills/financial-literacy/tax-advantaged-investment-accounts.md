# Tax-Advantaged Investment Accounts

## Core Idea
Different investment accounts—401k, Traditional IRA, Roth IRA, HSA, and 529 education plans—offer different tax benefits (upfront deductions, tax-free growth, or tax-free withdrawals) that vary by account type and income level, making account selection and contribution sequencing critical for wealth building.

## How It's Best Learned
For your income level, find the contribution limits and tax benefits for 401k, Traditional IRA, Roth IRA, and HSA. Create a prioritized contribution plan: maximize employer match first, then fill Roth IRA, then 401k excess, then HSA. Calculate 10-year tax savings.

## Common Misconceptions
You can't contribute to both Traditional and Roth IRAs when you can but deductions phase out. Roth is always better when it depends on current versus expected retirement tax bracket. HSAs are just healthcare accounts when they're triple-tax-advantaged investment accounts.

## Explainer

From your earlier work on investing and retirement accounts, you know that investing over time allows compound growth to do most of the work. Tax-advantaged accounts are the mechanism by which the government incentivizes this behavior — and understanding them is one of the highest-leverage financial moves available to a salaried worker, because the tax savings can compound alongside the investment returns.

The core idea is that taxes are normally owed in two potential places: when you earn money and when you realize gains from investments. Tax-advantaged accounts eliminate one or both of these, depending on the account type. A **Traditional 401(k) or IRA** gives you the deduction upfront — you contribute pre-tax dollars, reducing your taxable income today, and pay tax only when you withdraw in retirement. A **Roth IRA or Roth 401(k)** flips the timing: you contribute after-tax dollars now, and all future growth and withdrawals are completely tax-free. Neither is universally better; the choice hinges on whether your tax rate is higher now or expected to be higher in retirement. Young earners in low brackets almost always benefit more from Roth; high earners near peak income often benefit more from Traditional.

The **HSA** (Health Savings Account) is the most powerful account most people underuse. It requires enrollment in a high-deductible health plan, but it offers what's called **triple tax advantage**: contributions are pre-tax, growth is tax-free, and withdrawals for qualified medical expenses are tax-free. No other account in the tax code offers all three simultaneously. At 65, the restriction on qualified medical expenses disappears, and you can withdraw for any purpose (paying ordinary income tax, like a Traditional IRA). Funded aggressively and invested rather than spent, an HSA becomes a stealth retirement account. **529 plans** serve a similar targeted function for education: contributions grow tax-free and withdrawals for qualified education expenses are tax-free; many states also offer a state income tax deduction.

The strategic question is sequencing: in what order should you contribute to these accounts? A widely-used priority order is: (1) contribute to your employer's 401(k) up to the **employer match** — this is an immediate 50–100% guaranteed return, (2) max out a **Roth IRA** if your income qualifies (the annual limit is around $7,000 for most people under 50), (3) return to the 401(k) and contribute up to the annual maximum (~$23,000), (4) max out an **HSA** if eligible, (5) invest additional funds in a taxable brokerage account. The employer match is the non-negotiable first step because leaving it on the table is literally leaving free money uncollected. Each step beyond that involves judgment based on your tax situation, timeline, and liquidity needs — but understanding what each account offers lets you make those judgments deliberately rather than by accident.
