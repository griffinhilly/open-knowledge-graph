# Stock Market Investing Fundamentals

## Core Idea
Stock ownership represents fractional company ownership with returns from appreciation and dividends. The stock market has historically returned approximately 10% annually over long periods, but with significant year-to-year volatility; this volatility is acceptable only with time horizons of 10+ years to weather downturns.

## How It's Best Learned
Track the historical returns of a broad index (S&P 500) across 10, 20, and 30-year periods to observe how volatility decreases with longer time horizons.

## Common Misconceptions
- Thinking individual stock picking is necessary; diversified funds outperform for most investors. - Believing past returns guarantee future results; averages are guides, not guarantees. - Assuming market drops are losses; they're opportunities to buy more at lower prices if you maintain your plan.

## Explainer

From your prerequisite on stock market fundamentals, you know that a share of stock is fractional ownership of a company. When you buy stock, you're not lending money — you're becoming a part-owner, entitled to a proportional share of the company's profits and assets. Those returns arrive in two forms: **dividends** (periodic cash distributions when the company shares profits) and **capital appreciation** (the increase in the stock's market price as the company grows in value). Together, these have historically averaged about 10% per year for U.S. stocks before inflation, or roughly 7% after inflation.

The crucial context for that 10% average is its variability. In some years the market returns 30%; in others it falls 40%. Your risk-and-return prerequisite established why this matters: that variability is the price of the higher expected return. The stock market's long-run historical return is only accessible to investors who can hold through the down years without selling. This is why **time horizon** is the most important variable in stock investing — an investor with 30 years before needing the money can weather every historical bear market; an investor who needs the money in 18 months cannot. Holding stocks with a short time horizon converts statistical variability into a real risk of loss.

**Diversification** is how you eliminate the unnecessary portion of stock risk. Any single company can go bankrupt — Enron, Lehman Brothers, Kodak. If you own stock in one company, you bear the full risk of that company failing. If you own 500 companies spread across industries and geographies, the failure of any one barely registers. A **broad market index fund** (like one tracking the S&P 500 or total U.S. market) does this automatically, and because it is passively managed — just holding the market — its fees are a fraction of actively managed funds. Decades of evidence show that most active managers underperform a simple index fund after fees, which is why low-cost index investing is the default recommendation for most individual investors.

The psychological challenge of stock investing is treating paper losses as real losses. When the market drops 30%, you haven't lost money unless you sell. The shares still represent ownership in the same underlying companies, which still have buildings, employees, and customers. If your time horizon is intact, a drop is a sale on future ownership — the same monthly investment buys more shares at lower prices. The investors who earn the long-run average are the ones who stay invested through downturns; those who sell in fear and wait for stability tend to buy back in after the recovery, locking in the loss and missing the rebound. Discipline over decades, not stock-picking skill, is what produces the historical average.
