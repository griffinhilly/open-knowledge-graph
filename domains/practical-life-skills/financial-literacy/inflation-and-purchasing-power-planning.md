# Inflation and Purchasing Power Planning

## Core Idea
Inflation erodes the purchasing power of money; at 3% inflation, today's $100 buys only $74 in 10 years. Financial plans must account for inflation's impact on future spending needs, savings targets, real returns, and required asset levels.

## How It's Best Learned
Compare historical prices (college tuition, housing, gas) across decades. Project your future spending needs accounting for 2-3% inflation. Recalculate your emergency fund and financial independence targets.

## Common Misconceptions
Inflation is always 2-3% (it varies significantly); nominal returns are what matters (real returns do); inflation mainly affects retirees; inflation is too unpredictable to plan for.

## Explainer

From your prior work on inflation and purchasing power, you know the core mechanic: inflation is the gradual rise in the general price level, meaning each dollar buys less over time. The planning question is: given that reality, how do you build financial targets that will still be meaningful in 10, 20, or 30 years? The answer requires working in **real terms** (inflation-adjusted) rather than **nominal terms** (raw dollar amounts), because a savings goal that looks adequate today may fall far short when you actually need the money.

The most important application is setting forward-looking targets. If you need $50,000/year to live comfortably today and inflation averages 3%, you will need roughly $67,000/year in ten years to maintain the same lifestyle. Your **future spending needs**, **emergency fund target**, and **retirement number** must all be calculated using a future-value framework that includes an inflation assumption. A common mistake is setting a static target ("I need $1 million to retire") without adjusting it to the year you plan to retire — the same $1 million will buy very different amounts of consumption depending on when you reach it.

**Real return** — the return on an investment after subtracting inflation — is the number that actually matters for wealth building. A savings account earning 2% nominal return during a period of 4% inflation is producing a negative real return: your purchasing power is shrinking even though the balance is growing. This is why financial plans distinguish between "safe" accounts (appropriate for near-term needs) and growth-oriented accounts (appropriate for long-term goals) — near-term money needs stability, but long-term money needs to outpace inflation or it erodes in real terms.

Building inflation into your plan does not require precision. You cannot predict whether inflation will average 2% or 4% over the next 20 years, but you can use **scenario planning**: model your targets at both 2% and 4% and identify what changes. If the answer is "not much," your plan is robust. If the answer is "I fall significantly short at 4%," you now know where your vulnerability is and can address it — higher savings rate, higher-return investments, or later retirement. The goal is not to predict inflation correctly but to ensure your plan is not silently dependent on a single optimistic assumption.
