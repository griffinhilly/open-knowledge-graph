# Adapting Recipes and Substituting Ingredients

## Core Idea
Recipes are guides, not laws. Understanding the role of each ingredient lets you substitute intelligently—if a recipe calls for milk, you can choose cream, coconut milk, or broth depending on what you're making. Similarly, you can substitute vegetables if you match water content and texture. Understanding cooking science lets you adapt with confidence.

## How It's Best Learned
Make a simple recipe (like a soup or sauce) with exact ingredients first. Then make it again, deliberately substituting one or two ingredients based on what's in your kitchen. Observe what changes and what stays the same.

## Common Misconceptions
- Recipes must be followed exactly; any substitution will ruin the dish. - If an ingredient isn't in the recipe, you can't use it; recipes are rigid.

## Explainer

The key insight behind successful ingredient substitution is that every ingredient plays one or more functional roles in a dish, and a good substitute is one that replicates the relevant function — not necessarily the flavor or appearance. Eggs, for example, can bind (holding a patty together), emulsify (combining fat and water in a sauce), leaven (trapping air in a batter), or add moisture depending on the recipe. Substituting eggs with flax meal works for binding in a burger but fails for leavening in a soufflé, because flax meal cannot trap air. Before making any substitution, the question to ask is: what is this ingredient actually doing here?

Common ingredient functions fall into a few categories. Fats (butter, oil, cream) add richness, carry fat-soluble flavors, tenderize gluten, and help with browning. Acids (buttermilk, yogurt, vinegar, citrus) activate baking soda, tenderize proteins, and brighten flavor. Binders (eggs, flaxseed, cornstarch) hold structure together. Leaveners (baking soda, baking powder, yeast) produce gas that makes baked goods rise. Moisture sources (milk, broth, juice) hydrate dry ingredients and affect final texture. Once you categorize an ingredient by its function, the field of valid substitutes becomes much clearer.

Baking and stovetop cooking tolerate substitutions very differently. Stovetop dishes — soups, stir-fries, braises, sautés — are primarily flavor and texture problems. You can swap one vegetable for another, use chicken broth instead of beef broth, or omit an herb entirely, and the dish adapts. Baking is a chemistry problem. The ratios of acid to base (for leavening), fat to flour (for tenderness), liquid to dry ingredients (for hydration), and protein to structure matter precisely. A substitution that changes the pH, fat content, or moisture level can prevent a cake from rising or cause bread to come out dense. This is not a reason to avoid baking substitutions, but it is a reason to understand the chemistry before improvising.

Scaling a recipe (from your cooking-conversions-scaling work) interacts with substitution in an important way. Some ingredients scale linearly — if you double a soup recipe, you double the broth. Others do not — leavening agents often need less than double when doubling a baked recipe, because too much baking soda produces a metallic taste. When you are both scaling and substituting, consider each ingredient's function at the new quantity, not just its flavor contribution.

Building confidence with substitutions comes from practice and observation. Start with low-stakes stovetop dishes and make deliberate swaps — use coconut milk instead of cream in a curry, swap spinach for kale in a frittata — then note what changed in texture, flavor, and cooking behavior. Keep a mental or written log of what worked and why. Over time, this builds the intuition to improvise freely, which is the difference between following a recipe and actually knowing how to cook.
