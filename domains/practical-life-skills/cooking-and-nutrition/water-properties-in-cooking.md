# Water Properties in Cooking

## Core Idea
Water is a crucial ingredient in cooking that boils at 100°C (212°F), evaporates as food cooks, and affects texture and flavor. Understanding water properties—like how it carries heat and dissolves salt—is essential for controlling cooking outcomes. Quality of water matters too: filtered water may cook foods slightly differently than mineral-heavy tap water.

## Explainer

Water is the most ubiquitous ingredient in cooking, and understanding its physical behavior turns many kitchen mysteries into predictable, controllable outcomes. Its most important property is its fixed **boiling point**: at standard atmospheric pressure, liquid water always boils at 100°C (212°F) and holds that temperature as long as it remains liquid. A furiously boiling pot cooks food at exactly the same temperature as a gentle simmer — extra heat only creates more steam, not higher temperature. This means your cooking temperature in boiling water is fixed by physics; what you control is how quickly heat transfers to the food.

Water's **specific heat capacity** — the amount of energy needed to change its temperature — is unusually high compared to most substances. This is why a pot of water takes a long time to reach a boil but then holds a stable cooking temperature, and why braising (slow cooking submerged in water-based liquid) produces uniformly cooked results. Conversely, when water **evaporates** from food during cooking, it carries substantial energy away with it, which actively cools the food surface. This is why a wet piece of meat browns slowly — evaporation keeps the surface below the 150°C+ temperatures needed for browning. Patting food dry before searing is practical application of this principle.

Water is the universal **solvent** in cooking: salt, sugar, acids, and flavor compounds all dissolve in it and distribute evenly through whatever they contact. Salting pasta water doesn't just season the surface — the salt-water solution diffuses into the pasta as it absorbs water during cooking, seasoning it throughout. Brining meat works because a salt-water solution penetrates muscle fibers and alters protein structure, helping retain moisture during high-heat cooking. Marinating relies on water (and often acids) carrying flavor compounds into the outer layers of meat or vegetables. Understanding water as a carrier of dissolved substances explains why techniques that involve liquid — brining, braising, marinating — produce more thoroughly and evenly flavored results than surface-only seasoning.

**Mineral content** in tap water affects cooking in subtle but real ways. Hard water (high in calcium and magnesium ions) can toughen bean and legume skins during long cooking, because these minerals interfere with the breakdown of pectin in cell walls. The same beans cooked in soft or filtered water become tender in significantly less time. Mineral content also affects bread dough strength, beer fermentation character, and the clarity of delicate stocks. These effects are minor in most everyday cooking but become relevant when a recipe is sensitive to water chemistry, or when you're trying to replicate results from a recipe developed in a region with very different tap water.
