# Pan Selection and Heat Management

## Core Idea
Different pan materials absorb, retain, and distribute heat in fundamentally different ways, and matching the right pan to the task determines cooking success. Cast iron holds heat intensely and is ideal for searing and oven-to-stovetop transitions, but responds slowly to temperature changes. Stainless steel heats unevenly without a clad aluminum or copper core but excels at fond development for pan sauces. Nonstick coatings allow low-fat cooking of delicate foods like eggs and fish but cannot withstand high heat and degrade over time. Understanding how to preheat properly, manage flame level, and read visual cues (oil shimmer, water droplet behavior) gives the cook real-time control over what the food experiences.

## How It's Best Learned
Cook the same dish (e.g., a fried egg or seared chicken thigh) in cast iron, stainless steel, and nonstick back-to-back, noting differences in browning, sticking, and cleanup. Practice the water-drop test on stainless steel to find the Leidenfrost point. Learn to adjust burner output in response to food additions that temporarily lower pan temperature.

## Common Misconceptions
- Nonstick pans are the best all-purpose option — they cannot achieve the high heat needed for proper browning and their coatings degrade, making them unsuitable for many techniques.
- Stainless steel pans always stick — food sticks primarily because the pan is not properly preheated or oiled; a hot, well-oiled stainless pan releases food cleanly.
- Higher heat always cooks faster and better — excessive heat burns the exterior before the interior cooks through; most cooking benefits from moderate, well-controlled heat.

## Explainer

From sautéing, you know that high, direct heat is the engine of browning — the Maillard reaction that creates crust, color, and savory flavor requires the food's surface to reach around 150°C (300°F) or above. The pan is your interface between the burner and the food, and different pan materials create very different thermal environments even at the same flame setting. Understanding what each material does lets you choose deliberately rather than guessing.

**Cast iron** has very high **thermal mass** — it stores a lot of heat and releases it slowly. This has two consequences. First, cast iron takes a long time to preheat, but once hot, a cold steak dropped into it barely drops the pan's temperature. This is exactly what you want for searing: consistent, intense contact heat that drives browning rather than steaming. Second, cast iron responds slowly to adjustments — if you turn down the flame, the pan stays hot for a while. This makes it unforgiving for delicate, variable-temperature cooking. Cast iron also goes seamlessly from stovetop to oven, making it ideal for finishing thick cuts. Its one drawback is poor conductivity (heat doesn't spread evenly from the burner spot), so preheating evenly matters.

**Stainless steel** (particularly clad pans with aluminum or copper cores) heats more evenly and responds more quickly to flame adjustments. Its standout property is its ability to develop **fond** — the browned bits that stick to the stainless surface after searing meat or vegetables. When you deglaze with wine or stock, those bits dissolve and become the flavor base of a pan sauce. No other material develops fond like stainless. The common complaint that stainless sticks is almost entirely a technique problem: bring the pan to the right temperature before adding oil (the **Leidenfrost point**, where water droplets skitter across the surface rather than evaporating instantly, is the diagnostic test), add oil, let it shimmer, then add food. At that temperature, proteins bond briefly and then release cleanly.

**Nonstick** pans (PTFE or ceramic coatings) prevent food from bonding to the surface regardless of temperature management. This makes them excellent for eggs, fish, crêpes, and anything delicate that needs to slide freely. The trade-off is strict: nonstick coatings degrade above about 230°C (450°F), which makes high-heat searing impossible — the pan will smoke, the coating breaks down, and no fond develops anyway. Nonstick pans are a specialized tool for low-to-medium heat, not a universal substitute for other materials. Understanding each pan's purpose, rather than defaulting to one, is what gives a cook real control at the stove.
