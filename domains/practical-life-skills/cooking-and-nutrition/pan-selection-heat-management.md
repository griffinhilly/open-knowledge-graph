# Pan Selection and Heat Management

## Core Idea
Different pan materials absorb, retain, and distribute heat in fundamentally different ways, and matching the right pan to the task determines cooking success. Cast iron holds heat intensely and is ideal for searing and oven-to-stovetop transitions, but responds slowly to temperature changes. Stainless steel heats unevenly without a clad aluminum or copper core but excels at fond development for pan sauces. Nonstick coatings allow low-fat cooking of delicate foods like eggs and fish but cannot withstand high heat and degrade over time. Understanding how to preheat properly, manage flame level, and read visual cues (oil shimmer, water droplet behavior) gives the cook real-time control over what the food experiences.

## How It's Best Learned
Cook the same dish (e.g., a fried egg or seared chicken thigh) in cast iron, stainless steel, and nonstick back-to-back, noting differences in browning, sticking, and cleanup. Practice the water-drop test on stainless steel to find the Leidenfrost point. Learn to adjust burner output in response to food additions that temporarily lower pan temperature.

## Common Misconceptions
- Nonstick pans are the best all-purpose option — they cannot achieve the high heat needed for proper browning and their coatings degrade, making them unsuitable for many techniques.
- Stainless steel pans always stick — food sticks primarily because the pan is not properly preheated or oiled; a hot, well-oiled stainless pan releases food cleanly.
- Higher heat always cooks faster and better — excessive heat burns the exterior before the interior cooks through; most cooking benefits from moderate, well-controlled heat.
