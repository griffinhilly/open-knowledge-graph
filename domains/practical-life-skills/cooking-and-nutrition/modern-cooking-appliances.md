# Modern Cooking Appliances

## Core Idea
Modern appliances like pressure cookers, air fryers, and slow cookers use different heat transfer mechanisms and require adjusted cooking times and techniques. Pressure and air fryers enable faster cooking; slow cookers enable hands-off preparation. Each excels for different ingredient types and cooking goals.

## How It's Best Learned
Choose one appliance and master it with 5-10 recipes before moving to others. Keep detailed notes on timing, temperature settings, and results.

## Common Misconceptions
Pressure cookers are inherently dangerous; air fryers replace all other cooking methods; slow cooking works only for tough cuts of meat.

## Explainer

Your foundation in kitchen equipment has given you a mental model of conventional heat transfer: a burner heats a pan, which conducts heat into food, or an oven heats air that surrounds food. Modern appliances manipulate the same fundamental heat transfer mechanisms — **conduction**, **convection**, and radiation — but at different pressures, temperatures, and speeds to achieve results that are difficult or impossible with conventional methods. Understanding the mechanism behind each appliance lets you predict when to reach for it and how to adapt conventional recipes.

A **pressure cooker** (or instant pot) seals the cooking vessel and allows steam pressure to build. Under elevated pressure, water boils at higher temperatures — at 15 psi above atmospheric pressure (typical for a stovetop pressure cooker), water boils at about 121°C instead of 100°C. That 21°C increase sounds small, but because chemical reactions roughly double in rate for every 10°C rise in temperature, this translates to cooking that is roughly four times faster for many foods. A beef stew that takes 3 hours at a simmer is done in under 40 minutes in a pressure cooker. The key tradeoff: because food is cooking in steam at high temperature, it is always moist — pressure cookers cannot brown or caramelize food, so you get tenderness without crust. Modern electric pressure cookers have safety interlocks that prevent opening under pressure, making the old dangers (explosive releases from faulty seals) essentially obsolete.

An **air fryer** is fundamentally a small, powerful convection oven. A fan circulates very hot air (160–230°C) at high velocity around food placed in a basket. The rapid air movement strips away the thin cool layer of air that normally insulates food surfaces in a conventional oven, accelerating the **Maillard reaction** — the browning process that creates the flavor compounds we associate with fried and roasted foods. The result is crisp, browned exteriors without submerging food in oil, using only the small amount of oil applied as a coating. Air fryers do not replace all cooking methods: they struggle with large cuts of meat (insufficient volume), wet batters (the batter blows off), and delicate items. Their strength is rapid high-heat cooking of foods that benefit from surface crispness: vegetables, reheated leftovers, chicken pieces, and frozen foods.

A **slow cooker** maintains a steady low temperature (typically 77–93°C on the "low" setting) for an extended period without supervision. This targeted temperature range is ideal for breaking down **collagen** — the tough connective tissue in cheaper cuts of meat like chuck, shoulder, and shank. Collagen begins converting to gelatin at around 70°C, but the process requires sustained heat over several hours. The result is fork-tender meat that would be chewy and tough if rushed. Contrary to common belief, slow cookers work well for more than just meat: dried beans (no overnight soaking needed), lentil soups, oatmeal, and grain dishes all benefit from slow-cooked hydration and gentle heat. The key discipline is scaling your recipes for the appliance: because slow cookers trap moisture (the lid captures evaporation), sauces reduce very little, so you typically use less liquid than stovetop equivalents call for.
