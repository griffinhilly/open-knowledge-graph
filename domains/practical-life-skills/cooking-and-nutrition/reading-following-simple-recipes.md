# Reading and Following Recipes

## Core Idea
Recipes are carefully designed sets of instructions that list ingredients and explain steps in order. Learning to read a recipe completely before starting and follow each step sequentially is essential for successful cooking.

## Explainer

You already know how to measure ingredients accurately — cups, tablespoons, grams. A recipe is the document that tells you *what* to measure, *how much*, and *what to do with it*. But reading a recipe is a skill distinct from simply reading text. The most important habit you can build is reading the entire recipe before you touch a single ingredient. This sounds obvious, but it prevents the most common cooking failures: discovering mid-way that you need a two-hour marinade, that the butter needs to be room-temperature (and yours is cold), or that you are supposed to have preheated the oven twenty minutes ago.

Every recipe has two structural parts: the **ingredients list** and the **method**. The ingredients list tells you quantities and often contains hidden preparation instructions in the description — "1 onion, finely diced" means you need to dice before you start cooking, not improvise during. "2 cups flour, sifted" means sift before measuring. "4 garlic cloves, minced" means the prep work happens in advance. Read the ingredients list as a checklist of preparation tasks, not just a shopping list. This is the beginning of **mise en place** — a French culinary term meaning "everything in its place" — the practice of preparing and arranging all ingredients before beginning to cook.

The method gives you the sequence of steps, and sequence matters. Cooking is a time-sensitive activity with multiple things happening simultaneously once you begin: a pan is heating while you chop, something is simmering while you prepare the next addition. Read the method with attention to timing signals — "meanwhile," "while the onions cook," "reduce until" — which tell you when to start parallel tasks. If a step says "cook until golden, about 5 minutes," the time is a guide, not a command; use the visual cue (golden) as the actual indicator and treat the number as a rough expectation.

When something goes wrong — and sometimes it will — a brief mental reread of the recipe often reveals where the process diverged. Did you add an ingredient too early? Skip a step? Use the wrong measurement? Recipes are tested, so if your result looks nothing like the description, the most likely explanation is a procedural difference rather than a recipe error. As you cook more, you will start to understand *why* steps are ordered the way they are — why fat goes in before aromatics, why you deglaze after browning, why you fold rather than stir a batter. That understanding transforms a recipe from a set of instructions to follow blindly into a template you can adapt and improvise from.
