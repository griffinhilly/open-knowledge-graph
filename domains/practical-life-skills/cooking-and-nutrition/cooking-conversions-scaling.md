# Cooking Conversions and Scaling

## Core Idea
Cooking conversions translate between measurement systems (cups to milliliters, ounces to grams, tablespoons to teaspoons) so that any recipe from any source can be followed accurately. Scaling a recipe — doubling, halving, or adjusting to a different serving count — requires understanding which ingredients scale linearly (most do) and which do not (leavening agents, salt, and spices often need less-than-proportional increases). Weight-based measurements are more reliable than volume for dry ingredients because packing density varies.

## How It's Best Learned
Memorize the core equivalents first (1 cup = 240 mL, 1 oz = 28 g, 3 tsp = 1 tbsp, 16 tbsp = 1 cup) and keep a conversion chart posted in the kitchen. Practice by halving a familiar recipe, then doubling it, noting where the results differ from expectations. Use a kitchen scale to weigh flour measured by volume versus by weight and observe the variance.

## Common Misconceptions
- Doubling a recipe means doubling everything equally — seasonings, leavening agents, and cooking times often need adjustment rather than strict doubling.
- A cup is a cup worldwide — US customary cups (240 mL), imperial cups (284 mL), and metric cups (250 mL) differ enough to throw off baking recipes.
- Volume measurements are precise enough for baking — a cup of flour can vary by 30% depending on scooping method, which is why professional bakers measure by weight.

## Explainer

Cooking a recipe as written is straightforward, but two skills open up far more flexibility: converting between measurement systems and scaling recipes up or down. Both rely on the ratio and proportion concepts you've already learned — applied to a practical, edible context.

**Unit conversions** are chain multiplications. The key equivalents to have in memory are: 3 teaspoons = 1 tablespoon, 16 tablespoons = 1 cup, 1 cup ≈ 240 mL (US), and 1 ounce ≈ 28 grams. To convert, multiply by a fraction equal to 1 — for example, to convert ¾ cup to tablespoons: ¾ cup × (16 tbsp / 1 cup) = 12 tablespoons. The units cancel just like variables in algebra. When a recipe from a different country uses metric measurements, the same chain-multiplication approach applies; the only trap is that "cup" means different volumes in US, UK, and Australian recipes, so weight-in-grams is far more reliable for anything precision-sensitive.

**Scaling** is proportional reasoning applied to ingredients. To scale a recipe, calculate your scale factor (desired servings ÷ original servings) and multiply every ingredient quantity by that factor. Most ingredients — flour, sugar, butter, eggs, liquids — scale linearly and perfectly. A 2× recipe simply uses twice as much of each. This is the rule for the majority of what you'll scale.

The exceptions matter most in baking. **Leavening agents** (baking powder, baking soda, yeast) do not scale linearly: doubling a recipe doesn't require doubling the leavening. Too much leavening makes baked goods taste bitter or collapse after rising too fast. The standard guidance is to use about 75–80% of the proportional amount when scaling up significantly. **Salt and strong spices** behave similarly — scale them conservatively and adjust to taste. **Cooking time and temperature** almost never scale with batch size; a larger pan or more portions usually needs the same temperature but requires checking doneness empirically.

Finally, the hidden variable in all of this is measurement precision. Volume measurements — especially of dry ingredients — are surprisingly unreliable because packing density varies. A "cup" of flour can range from 120 g (lightly spooned) to 160 g (scooped and packed) depending on technique. For everyday cooking, this variation is harmless. For baking, where the ratio of flour to liquid determines whether your cake rises or turns to a brick, it matters enormously. A kitchen scale measuring in grams removes this uncertainty completely, which is why professional bakers almost never use volume for dry ingredients. When you read a recipe in grams, trust it — it's being precise on purpose.
