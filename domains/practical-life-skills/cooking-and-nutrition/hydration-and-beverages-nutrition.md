# Hydration and Beverages Nutrition

## Core Idea
Water and beverages significantly impact overall health and nutrition outcomes. Hydration needs vary by activity level, climate, and health status. Beyond water, tea and coffee offer polyphenols; milk and plant-based drinks provide protein and minerals; sugary drinks contribute excess calories. Strategic beverage choices support long-term nutritional goals.

## How It's Best Learned
Track water and beverage intake for one week, then calculate total calories, sugars, and nutrients from all drinks consumed.

## Common Misconceptions
All beverages hydrate equally; water is the only healthy drink; electrolyte drinks are necessary for routine exercise.

## Explainer

Your foundation in basic nutrition tells you that the body is mostly water and that nearly every biological process — from digesting food to regulating temperature to transporting nutrients in the blood — depends on adequate fluid. **Hydration** is the process of maintaining that fluid balance. The body loses water continuously through breath, sweat, urine, and digestion, and those losses must be replaced. The baseline recommendation of roughly 2–3 liters of total fluid per day is just a starting point; the actual amount needed varies meaningfully with body size, physical activity, climate, and health conditions like fever or pregnancy.

Water is the gold standard for hydration because it replaces lost fluid with zero caloric cost and no competing substances to process. But it is not the only option, and framing it as "the only healthy drink" leads people to ignore the nutritional content — both positive and negative — of everything else they consume. **Coffee and tea**, for example, are mild diuretics but contribute net fluid gain despite their caffeine content; they also deliver **polyphenols**, plant compounds associated with reduced inflammation and cardiovascular risk. Unsweetened coffee and tea are nutritionally active beverages, not just flavored water.

**Milk and fortified plant-based milks** (soy, oat, almond) serve a different role: they provide protein, calcium, and fat alongside their fluid content. Soy milk most closely approximates cow's milk in protein content; almond milk is primarily water with added vitamins and very little protein. These differences matter when beverages are functioning as a meaningful source of nutrients — for children, older adults, or people with limited food variety. **Sugary drinks** (soda, juice, sports drinks, sweetened coffee) are where beverage choices most visibly affect overall nutrition: a single 350ml soda contributes 35–40g of sugar — nearly the daily added-sugar limit — with no other nutritional benefit.

Electrolyte drinks like sports beverages are designed for a specific scenario: prolonged exercise (typically more than 60–90 minutes) where significant sodium and potassium are lost through sweat. For routine daily exercise or moderate activity, plain water replaces fluid loss adequately; the sodium and potassium in a normal varied diet replenish electrolytes without supplementation. The practical approach is to use water as the default, treat coffee and tea as contributors to daily fluid intake, read the nutrition labels on plant-based milks to understand what you're actually getting, and treat sugary drinks as an occasional food rather than a hydration source.
