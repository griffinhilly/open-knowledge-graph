# Nutrition for Athletic Performance

## Core Idea
Athletic performance benefits significantly from strategic nutrition—timing carbohydrates and protein around workouts, maintaining proper hydration, and balancing macronutrient ratios for recovery. Pre-workout meals emphasize carbs and some protein; post-workout meals prioritize protein and carbs for muscle repair. Individual needs vary by sport, intensity, and personal goals.

## How It's Best Learned
Experiment with different pre- and post-workout meals and track energy levels, performance metrics, and recovery quality over several weeks.

## Common Misconceptions
High protein alone builds muscle; carbohydrates should be minimized for fitness; supplements replace whole foods.

## Explainer

From your work with food groups and macronutrients, you know that carbohydrates are the body's primary energy source, protein builds and repairs tissue, and fat provides slow-burning fuel and supports cell function. Athletic performance draws on all three, but what changes for active people is not primarily *what* to eat so much as *when* to eat it and in what proportions to match the demands of training and recovery.

The most important concept to understand is **glycogen** — the form in which your body stores carbohydrates in muscles and the liver. During moderate-to-intense exercise, glycogen is the dominant fuel. When glycogen runs low, performance drops sharply — this is what endurance athletes call "bonking" or "hitting the wall." A **pre-workout meal** two to three hours before exercise prioritizes complex carbohydrates (oats, rice, bread) to top off glycogen stores, with moderate protein and minimal fat or fiber (which slow digestion and can cause discomfort during activity). The goal is a steady glucose supply during the workout, not a sudden spike and crash.

The post-workout window is when protein timing becomes most critical. During resistance training and high-intensity effort, muscle fibers sustain microscopic damage — the basis of **muscle protein synthesis** and the adaptation that makes you stronger. Consuming 20–40 grams of high-quality protein within one to two hours after training supplies the amino acids needed for repair and growth. Carbohydrates in the post-workout meal also matter: they replenish glycogen stores and trigger an insulin response that helps drive amino acids into muscle cells. The combination of protein plus carbohydrates accelerates recovery more effectively than either alone — this is why "protein only" post-workout strategies are less effective than the research suggests many athletes believe.

**Hydration** is the nutrient most athletes underestimate. Even 2% dehydration — about 1.5 kg for a 75 kg person — measurably impairs endurance and cognitive function. Electrolytes lost through sweat (sodium, potassium, magnesium) affect muscle contraction and fluid balance, which is why water alone is insufficient for extended high-intensity efforts. Individual needs vary substantially by sport, body size, sweat rate, and environment: what works for a marathon runner differs sharply from what works for a strength athlete. Supplements can fill specific gaps (creatine for high-intensity repeated efforts, iron for endurance athletes with deficiency) but cannot replace the foundation of adequate whole-food macronutrient and hydration intake.
