# Understanding and Cooking Eggs

## Core Idea
Eggs are a versatile and nutritious protein source that transform dramatically when cooked. Children learn about different egg parts, multiple cooking methods from scrambling to boiling, and how eggs change from liquid to solid.

## Explainer

Eggs are one of the best foods for understanding a fundamental truth about cooking: **heat changes food chemically, not just physically**. A raw egg is liquid and clear; a cooked egg is solid and white. Unlike melting ice (which can refreeze) or dissolving sugar (which can recrystallize), this transformation is irreversible — you cannot uncook a scrambled egg. Understanding why this happens gives you control over one of the most versatile ingredients in the kitchen.

The transformation happens because of **protein denaturation**. Eggs are rich in proteins — mostly in the white (called the **albumen**) and in a different form in the yolk. At room temperature, these protein molecules are folded into compact, stable shapes suspended in water, which is why raw egg is liquid and flows freely. When heat is applied, the proteins absorb energy, their folded structures unfold and uncoil, and the now-extended protein strands begin to tangle together. As more strands link up into a network, the egg sets from liquid into a solid. This happens at precise temperatures: egg whites begin to set around 62°C (144°F) and are fully firm by about 80°C; yolks set in a slightly higher range. This is why a soft-boiled egg has a fully set white but a still-runny yolk — the center hasn't reached yolk-setting temperature yet.

Different cooking methods control *how* heat reaches the egg, producing very different results. **Scrambled eggs** are stirred constantly while heating over moderate heat — this breaks the protein network into small, soft curds before it can set into a rigid slab, resulting in creamy, tender texture. A **fried egg** receives heat only from below: the white, closest to the heat source, sets first while the yolk, shielded above, can remain runny. **Hard-boiled eggs** are submerged in water long enough that heat penetrates all the way to the center, setting both white and yolk completely. **Soft-boiled eggs** are timed so the white is fully set but the yolk, which is at the center and reaches temperature last, stays gel-like. In every case, the same protein transformation is happening — the only variable is how much of the egg reaches what temperature for how long.

The **yolk** and **white** have very different compositions, which is why they behave differently and serve different roles in cooking. The white is almost pure protein in water — nearly no fat — which is why it can be whipped into foam (air gets trapped between unfolded protein strands) or used as a clear, neutral binder. The yolk is rich in fat and contains **lecithin**, a natural emulsifier that allows fat and water to blend rather than separate. This is why egg yolks are the basis for sauces like hollandaise and mayonnaise, and why they bind together baked goods: the lecithin helps unite ingredients that would otherwise stay separate.
