# Cooking for Dietary Restrictions

## Core Idea
Cooking for dietary restrictions means understanding not just what to remove from a recipe but what to replace it with and why the original ingredient was there. Substitutions must account for the functional role of each ingredient: eggs provide binding, leavening, and moisture; dairy adds fat and tenderness; gluten gives structure. A flax egg replaces binding but not leavening; oat milk substitutes liquid dairy but behaves differently when heated. Cross-contamination is a serious concern for allergies — shared cutting boards, oil, or utensils can transfer allergens even in trace amounts.

## How It's Best Learned
Pick a familiar recipe and adapt it for one restriction at a time (e.g., make a cookie recipe vegan, then separately make it gluten-free). Taste the results side-by-side with the original to understand what each substitution changes. Practice reading ingredient labels for hidden allergens (whey in crackers, soy lecithin in chocolate, wheat in soy sauce).

## Common Misconceptions
- Gluten-free flour is a one-to-one swap for all-purpose flour — gluten-free flours lack the protein network that provides structure, so recipes typically need binders like xanthan gum or different ratios.
- Vegan food is automatically healthier — vegan junk food exists in abundance; removing animal products does not inherently improve nutritional quality.
- A small amount of an allergen is harmless — for people with true allergies (not intolerances), even trace amounts can trigger serious or life-threatening reactions.
