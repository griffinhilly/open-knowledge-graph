# Basic Nutrition Fundamentals

## Core Idea
Nutrition is the study of how food supplies the body with energy and building materials. Calories measure energy content, while nutrients — vitamins, minerals, proteins, fats, carbohydrates, and water — serve structural, regulatory, and metabolic functions. No single food provides everything the body needs, which is why dietary variety is the foundation of good nutrition.

## How It's Best Learned
Compare nutrient profiles of common foods side by side. Track meals for 3–5 days and identify gaps (e.g., consistently low fiber or calcium). Use visual guides like the USDA MyPlate to anchor proportional thinking before moving to detailed macronutrient analysis.

## Common Misconceptions
- Fat is inherently bad — dietary fat is essential for hormone production, vitamin absorption, and brain function.
- 'Natural' or 'organic' labels indicate nutritional superiority — these terms refer to production methods, not nutrient content.

## Explainer

Nutrition is simply the science of what food does inside your body. Every food you eat is doing one of three things: supplying energy (calories), providing building materials (proteins, fats, minerals), or enabling chemical reactions (vitamins, minerals, water). The three macronutrients — carbohydrates, fats, and proteins — are called "macro" because you need them in large amounts. Micronutrients (vitamins and minerals) are needed in small amounts but are no less critical.

Calories are a unit of energy, not a measure of goodness or badness. Carbohydrates and proteins each provide 4 calories per gram; fat provides 9 calories per gram, which is why fat-rich foods are calorie-dense. This density doesn't make fat harmful — in fact, dietary fat is essential for absorbing fat-soluble vitamins (A, D, E, K), building every cell membrane in your body, and producing hormones. The problem is quantity and type, not fat itself.

One of the most important insights in nutrition is that no single food covers all your needs. Eggs are rich in protein and fat-soluble vitamins but low in vitamin C and fiber. Spinach is packed with iron and folate but lacks significant calories or B12. This is why variety is the foundational principle: eating across food groups — grains, vegetables, fruits, proteins, and dairy or alternatives — is the practical way to fill all nutritional gaps.

If you've used fractions and percentages, you already have the tools to read nutrition labels. When a label says "20% Daily Value of calcium," that means one serving delivers a fifth of the recommended daily intake for an average adult. Tracking these percentages across a few days of eating quickly reveals gaps — perhaps you're consistently low on fiber or vitamin D — and lets you adjust before deficiencies become problems.

Finally, be skeptical of simple labels. "Natural," "organic," and "low-fat" describe production methods or single nutrients, not overall nutritional quality. A low-fat cookie may still be high in sugar; an organic chip is still mostly refined starch. Learning to read the actual nutrient information, rather than front-of-package claims, is the core skill of nutritional literacy.
