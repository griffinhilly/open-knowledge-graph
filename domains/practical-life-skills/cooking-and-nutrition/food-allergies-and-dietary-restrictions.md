# Food Allergies and Dietary Restrictions

## Core Idea
Food allergies are immune responses to specific proteins in foods — the "big nine" allergens (milk, eggs, fish, shellfish, tree nuts, peanuts, wheat, soybeans, and sesame) account for the vast majority of serious reactions. Dietary restrictions also include intolerances (like lactose intolerance), medical conditions (like celiac disease), and personal or cultural choices (vegetarian, vegan, halal, kosher). Knowing how to identify allergens in ingredient lists, prevent cross-contamination during preparation, and make safe substitutions is essential when cooking for others.

## How It's Best Learned
Practice reading ingredient labels for hidden allergens (like whey for dairy, or hydrolyzed vegetable protein for soy) and cook a meal that accommodates a specific restriction to build confidence with substitution strategies.

## Common Misconceptions
- A food allergy and a food intolerance are the same thing — allergies involve the immune system and can be life-threatening, while intolerances cause digestive discomfort but are not dangerous in the same way.
- Small amounts of an allergen are safe — for people with true allergies, even trace amounts can trigger anaphylaxis.
- Gluten-free automatically means healthier — gluten-free products often contain more sugar, fat, and processed ingredients to compensate for texture and flavor changes.

## Explainer

From reading nutrition labels, you already know how to scan ingredient lists for what's inside a packaged food. That skill becomes critical when someone at the table has a food allergy. The most important distinction to understand is the difference between an **allergy** and an **intolerance**. An allergy is an immune system response to a specific protein: the body identifies it as a threat and mounts a defense reaction that can escalate to **anaphylaxis** — a systemic, potentially fatal response involving throat swelling, airway constriction, and cardiovascular collapse — within minutes of exposure. A **food intolerance**, like lactose intolerance, causes digestive discomfort (cramping, bloating) but does not involve the immune system and is not life-threatening in the same way. When cooking for someone with a true allergy, the safety standard is categorically higher.

The **Big Nine allergens** — milk, eggs, fish, shellfish, tree nuts, peanuts, wheat, soybeans, and sesame — account for the vast majority of serious reactions and are legally required to appear on U.S. food labels. But they hide under many names, and this is where label-reading fluency pays off. Dairy appears as whey, casein, lactalbumin, and ghee. Soy appears as hydrolyzed vegetable protein, textured soy protein, and miso. Wheat appears as semolina, spelt, farro, kamut, bulgur, and durum. Scanning for "contains: milk" at the bottom of a label is a good first step, but full safety requires reading every ingredient individually, because minor ingredients and flavorings often carry hidden allergens.

**Cross-contamination** is equally important to allergen presence in ingredients. A dish that contains no allergen ingredients can still trigger a reaction if it was prepared on a surface, with utensils, or in oil that previously contacted an allergen. In practice this means using separate cutting boards, washing hands thoroughly between handling different foods, and using fresh cooking oil if a previous batch fried something containing the allergen. A person with a peanut allergy can react to trace residue left on a knife that touched peanut butter an hour earlier. Restaurants use color-coded cutting boards and designated prep areas for exactly this reason.

Beyond allergies, dietary restrictions follow their own logic. **Celiac disease** is an autoimmune condition triggered by gluten (a protein in wheat, barley, and rye) — even small amounts cause intestinal damage over time, so "gluten-free" for celiac patients is a medical requirement, not a preference. **Vegetarian and vegan** diets exclude animal products to varying degrees (vegetarian avoids meat; vegan avoids all animal-derived ingredients including dairy, eggs, and honey). **Halal** and **kosher** are religious dietary frameworks with rules about permitted foods, slaughter methods, and food pairings. When adapting a recipe for any of these, the reliable approach is to understand the underlying principle — what's excluded and why — so you can evaluate substitutions correctly rather than guessing. A recipe substitution that works for a dairy intolerance might still contain casein and be unsafe for someone with a milk allergy.
