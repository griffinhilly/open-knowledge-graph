# Reading Nutrition Labels

## Core Idea
Nutrition Facts labels report nutrient content per serving, not per package — the first step is always checking serving size and servings per container. The % Daily Value (%DV) benchmarks each nutrient against a 2,000-calorie reference diet: 5%DV is considered low, 20%DV is high. The ingredients list, ordered by weight, reveals added sugars and sodium even when the Nutrition Facts panel seems favorable. Comparing labels between similar products is one of the most practical decision-support tools available while shopping.

## How It's Best Learned
Compare labels on three versions of the same product (e.g., regular, reduced-fat, and 'natural' yogurt) and identify which is genuinely healthiest across multiple dimensions. Practice spotting added sugar under alternate names (dextrose, corn syrup, maltose). Calculate total sodium in a full meal by summing multiple packaged components.

## Common Misconceptions
- Low fat means low calorie — fat-free products often add sugar to compensate for lost palatability.
- The first ingredient listed is the most prominent — for sugars added in multiple forms, the combined total may exceed the leading ingredient.

## Explainer

The Nutrition Facts label is one of the most information-dense tools available to consumers, but it only works if you read it in the right order. The very first thing to check is always the serving size and servings per container. Every number on the panel — calories, sodium, fat, everything — is reported per serving, not per package. A container of soup labeled "250 calories" may contain 2.5 servings, meaning you consume 625 calories if you eat the whole thing. Until you know the serving size, no other number on the label is interpretable.

The % Daily Value (%DV) column benchmarks each nutrient against a 2,000-calorie reference diet. The practical shortcut: 5%DV or less is considered low for that nutrient; 20%DV or more is considered high. This lets you quickly assess whether a food is a meaningful source of, say, fiber (aim high) or sodium (aim low) without doing arithmetic. One important caveat: the 2,000-calorie reference is an average adult estimate. Children, athletes, and people with different metabolic needs have different baselines, so %DV is a rough guide, not a precision tool.

The ingredients list operates on a different logic and catches what the Nutrition Facts panel can obscure. Ingredients are listed in descending order by weight, so the first ingredient is the most abundant by mass. This sounds straightforward, but two traps are common. First, sugars can appear under many names — corn syrup, dextrose, maltose, evaporated cane juice, fruit juice concentrate, honey — and each is listed separately. A product where no single sweetener ranks first may still be predominantly sweetened; you have to mentally sum all the sugar-derived entries. Second, "low fat" claims on the front of the package do not mean low calorie. Fat-free products frequently compensate for the lost richness and palatability of fat by adding sugar, modified starch, or other fillers, which restore calories even as fat grams drop.

Comparing labels between competing products is where this skill pays off most directly. When choosing between two yogurts, two breakfast cereals, or two pasta sauces, placing them side by side and checking serving size, calories, sodium, added sugars, and the ingredients list gives you a multi-dimensional picture that no individual marketing claim can provide. Treat the label as a specification sheet, not a health endorsement — and remember that the front of the package is advertising, while the back panel is regulated information.
