# Interior Wall and Ceiling Assessment and Repair

## Core Idea
Interior walls and ceilings define your home's living spaces and can be made of drywall, plaster, tile, or wood paneling. These surfaces protect insulation and utilities behind them and indicate the home's condition when they show water stains, cracks, or deterioration. Most interior damage is cosmetic but can indicate underlying problems like water leaks or structural movement. Understanding how to assess damage and perform basic repairs keeps your home looking good and helps identify serious issues early.

## Explainer

Your walls and ceilings function as both the finished surface of your living space and the diagnostic dashboard of your home. Cosmetic damage — small holes, scuffs, minor cracks — is expected over time and repairable with the drywall and painting skills from your prerequisites. The higher-order skill developed in this topic is reading damage as evidence: determining whether what you see on the surface reflects a surface-only problem or a symptom of something more significant happening behind or above it.

The most important diagnostic question is: **where is the damage, and what is above or behind it?** A hairline crack running diagonally from the corner of a window or door opening is extremely common and almost always cosmetic — caused by the slight seasonal movement of the building as humidity and temperature change. **Stair-step cracks** following the mortar joints in a brick chimney, or horizontal cracks in a basement wall, are different: these can indicate foundation settlement or lateral soil pressure and warrant professional evaluation. The distinction between cosmetic cracking (random, spider-web, or corner-diagonal) and structural cracking (patterned, wide, or with displacement on one side) is the most important judgment you'll develop through observation.

Water stains deserve the most immediate attention. A **brown or yellow ring stain** on a ceiling directly below a bathroom, or along an exterior wall, is telling you that water has traveled through a material and evaporated, leaving minerals behind. The ring marks the perimeter of repeated wet-dry cycles. The critical question is not the stain — it's whether the source is still active. Press the stained area lightly; if it feels soft or spongy, the drywall is saturated or has already deteriorated. If it's firm, the source may have resolved. Either way, identify and fix the source before repairing the surface — painting over an active leak just hides the evidence while damage continues behind the wall.

For the actual repairs, your drywall and painting prerequisites cover the mechanics. For small holes (under 4 inches), a self-adhesive mesh patch and joint compound, sanded and painted, is the standard approach. For larger holes, a backing piece is needed to support the patch. The hardest part of wall repair is not the structural patching but the **texture matching** and paint blending — smooth walls show every imperfection, and even perfectly repaired drywall can be visible if the paint sheen or texture doesn't match the surrounding area. Feathering joint compound broadly (12–18 inches) rather than just over the hole, and repainting the full wall rather than spot-patching, produces the most invisible results. On older homes with **plaster walls**, the material is more brittle and repairs require compatible plaster or setting-type compounds rather than standard joint compound, which doesn't adhere as well to the lime-based substrate.
