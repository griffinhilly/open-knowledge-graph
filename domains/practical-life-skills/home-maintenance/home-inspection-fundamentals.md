# Home Inspection Fundamentals and Documentation

## Core Idea
A thorough home inspection examines each system systematically and documents current conditions, defects, and maintenance needs. Understanding what inspectors look for and keeping detailed records of your home's condition helps you track deterioration, plan repairs, and establish a maintenance timeline. Inspection reports are also valuable when selling or insuring your home.

## How It's Best Learned
Review a professional home inspection report and walk through each room noting visible conditions. Take photos of problem areas and keep a maintenance log with dates and findings.

## Common Misconceptions
- Thinking a home inspection is only necessary when buying a home.
- Assuming visual inspection can catch all problems (some require specialized tools).
- Overlooking the age and remaining lifespan of major components.

## Explainer

You already know from understanding home systems how a house's major components — structure, roofing, plumbing, electrical, HVAC, and envelope — function individually. A home inspection is the practice of examining all of these systems together, systematically, to document their current condition. The key word is *systematically*: the value of an inspection comes not from expertise in any one system but from a consistent, repeatable process that doesn't skip anything because it looked fine at a glance.

Professional inspectors follow a standardized sequence — typically exterior, then roof, then interior room by room, then mechanical systems. This sequence matters because it prevents the most common inspection failure mode: spending twenty minutes examining one interesting problem while missing three routine issues because you ran out of time. For your own periodic home inspections, adopting a consistent checklist achieves the same discipline. The checklist doesn't have to be elaborate — it just has to hit every system every time, including the ones that almost never have problems, because "almost never" is not "never."

**Documentation** is the other half of inspection value, and it's where homeowners most often fall short. A mental note that "the bathroom caulk looked a little worn" in 2023 is useless in 2026 when you're trying to determine whether the moisture stain appeared recently or has been there for years. Photographs with dates, brief written notes, and a maintenance log convert an inspection into a **baseline** — the starting point against which future inspections are compared. Deterioration that is gradual (concrete settling, paint fading, siding cracking) is only visible as a pattern over time. Without a documented baseline, you're always evaluating current condition in isolation rather than rate of change.

The third dimension of inspection is **component lifespan awareness**. Every major home system has an expected service life: asphalt shingle roofs typically last 20-30 years, water heaters 8-12 years, HVAC systems 15-25 years, electrical panels 25-40 years. Knowing both the installation date and the expected lifespan lets you anticipate failures before they become emergencies — and budget for replacements before the system fails at the worst possible time. A water heater that is 14 years old isn't broken, but it's in its final years; a roof installed in 2005 on a house you're considering buying deserves close scrutiny even if it looks fine. Age alone is information. An inspection that only records current defects, without tracking component age and remaining life, misses half the picture.
