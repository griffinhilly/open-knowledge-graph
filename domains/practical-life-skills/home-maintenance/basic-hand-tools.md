# Basic Hand Tools and Their Uses

## Core Idea
Every homeowner needs a small set of hand tools: hammer, flathead and Phillips screwdrivers, adjustable wrench, needle-nose pliers, tape measure, and a level. Each tool has a specific mechanical purpose — a hammer drives nails, a wrench turns fasteners, a level checks flat surfaces. Owning and knowing how to use these tools lets you handle the majority of minor household repairs without calling a professional.

## How It's Best Learned
Handle each tool physically and practice its primary motion — drive a nail, tighten a bolt, measure a board. Keep tools organized in a basic toolbox so they're easy to find when needed.

## Common Misconceptions
- Using the wrong tool for a job (e.g., using a knife as a screwdriver) damages both the tool and the work surface.
- 'A bigger hammer solves every problem' — forcing a fastener strips it and creates a worse problem.

## Explainer

Think of hand tools as a vocabulary — each word (tool) has a specific meaning (function), and knowing which word to use in which situation is the core skill. You already understand measurement from working with standard units; the tape measure is how that skill shows up at the workbench, letting you mark cut lines, check spacing, and verify fit before you commit.

The hammer and screwdriver are the workhorses of the toolbox. A hammer's job is to transfer force from your arm to a nail through a controlled swing — the weight of the head does the work, not your grip strength. A screwdriver converts rotational force into clamping force between fastener and material. The critical detail is matching tip to screw: flathead for slotted screws, Phillips for cross-slotted. Mismatch causes the tip to "cam out" (slip), stripping the slot and turning a one-minute job into a frustrating extraction problem.

The adjustable wrench and needle-nose pliers both grip objects, but differently. A wrench applies torque to hex-shaped nuts and bolts — tighten the jaws snugly before turning or you'll round the corners off the fastener. Needle-nose pliers reach into tight spaces and hold small parts or bend wire; they're not designed for heavy torque. Using pliers on a nut instead of a wrench is a classic mistake that ruins the nut's corners.

A level is a decision-making tool rather than a shaping tool — it tells you whether a surface follows gravity's definition of horizontal (or vertical). Even a wall that looks straight to your eye may slope by several degrees. When hanging a shelf, a misread of even two degrees means items placed on it roll toward one end. The bubble centered between the lines means the surface is true.

The deeper lesson connecting all these tools is that each one is designed around a specific mechanical principle — driving, rotating, gripping, measuring, verifying. Using the right tool respects that principle and protects both the tool and the work. Improvising with the wrong tool (using a knife as a flathead, pliers as a wrench) degrades both, often creating a bigger problem than the one you started with.
