# Hand Tools: Identification and Basic Use

## Core Idea
Hand tools are simple devices that help us do repairs and maintenance. Common tools include hammers (for nails), screwdrivers (for screws), wrenches (for nuts and bolts), pliers (for gripping), tape measures (for measuring), and levels (for checking straightness). Each has a specific purpose, and using the right tool safely makes work easier and prevents injury.

## How It's Best Learned
Explore a toolbox or hardware store's tool section with an adult. Hold and learn the name and purpose of each tool. Practice using tools safely on supervised projects like hanging a picture.

## Common Misconceptions
Any tool can do any job. (Using the wrong tool is unsafe and damages tools or work.) Heavier tools are always better. (The right tool for the job matters more than weight.)

## Explainer

A hand tool is a simple machine: it multiplies your force, extends your reach, or gives you mechanical advantage that your bare hands cannot. Understanding the physics of each tool makes it much easier to use correctly — and explains why using the wrong tool is both ineffective and unsafe. Every tool you pick up is optimized for a particular task, and matching the tool to the task is the foundational skill.

The **hammer** is the tool most beginners over-apply. Its job is to drive fasteners using kinetic energy from a swinging motion — the weight of the head, not arm strength, does the work. A proper hammer swing lets the tool fall along its arc; fighting this motion wastes energy and reduces control. For driving screws, a hammer is the wrong tool entirely — a **screwdriver** or drill turns the fastener, using rotational force to pull threads into wood or metal rather than impact force. Phillips and flat-head screwdrivers are not interchangeable: the wrong type damages the fastener head and makes removal much harder later. Always match the screwdriver to the screw head size as well as type — a too-small driver will strip the head immediately.

**Wrenches and pliers** both grip and turn, but for different situations. A wrench applies torque to a nut or bolt with a fixed, precise fit — this distributes force evenly and doesn't slip. Pliers grip irregularly shaped objects with adjustable jaws. Using pliers on a hex nut when a wrench is available is a common beginner mistake: the rounded plier jaws contact only two points of the nut, applying uneven force that rounds off the corners and makes the nut impossible to remove later. The right fit is important.

The **tape measure** and **level** are measuring tools that belong in every home toolkit. Reading a tape measure correctly — understanding the fractional markings (each inch is divided into halves, quarters, eighths, and sixteenths) — is a prerequisite for any cutting or building task. A level tells you whether a surface is truly horizontal (**plumb**) or vertical. When hanging shelves or picture frames, relying on eye judgment alone produces results that look visibly wrong from a distance. Two minutes with a level prevents work you will need to redo. Building comfort with these tools — learning their names, their purposes, and the consequences of using the wrong one — is what makes all downstream maintenance tasks approachable rather than intimidating.
