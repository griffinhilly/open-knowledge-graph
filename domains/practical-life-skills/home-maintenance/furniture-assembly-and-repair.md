# Furniture Assembly and Repair

## Core Idea
Furniture assembly and basic repair are practical skills that save money and extend the life of your belongings. Assembly — whether flat-pack furniture or items requiring partial construction — follows a consistent approach: inventory all parts and hardware first, read the full instructions before starting, work on a clean and spacious surface, and tighten fasteners in stages rather than fully torquing each one immediately. Common repairs include tightening loose joints with wood glue and clamps, fixing wobbly legs by shimming or re-drilling, touching up scratches with wood filler or markers, and reinforcing sagging shelves with additional brackets or supports.

## How It's Best Learned
Assemble a piece of flat-pack furniture following the instructions carefully, then practice a simple repair like re-gluing a loose chair joint — these two experiences cover the most common real-world scenarios.

## Common Misconceptions
- Power tools are necessary for furniture assembly — most flat-pack furniture is designed for hand tools, and over-tightening with a power drill is a leading cause of stripped holes and cracked particle board.
- Wood glue is a weak, temporary fix — modern wood glue, when properly clamped and cured, creates a bond stronger than the surrounding wood fibers.
- Wobbly furniture is ruined and needs replacing — most wobbles are caused by a single loose joint or uneven leg that takes minutes to fix with basic tools.

## Explainer

Furniture assembly and repair draw directly on your basic hand tool skills — the difference here is understanding *why* each step matters, not just following a sequence blindly. Flat-pack furniture (IKEA and similar) is engineered for unskilled assembly, but it is also engineered with minimal tolerances. This means small mistakes — tightening one side fully before the other, skipping the order specified in the instructions — can misalign joints that are very difficult to correct once locked in. The professional habit is to assemble loosely first, check alignment and squareness, then tighten in stages from the center outward.

**Cam and dowel fasteners**, the most common hardware in flat-pack furniture, work differently from screws. The dowel pin goes into a pre-drilled hole; the cam (a disk that rotates inside a recess) locks onto a small ridge on the dowel when you turn it with a flat-head screwdriver. The failure mode is over-tightening: too much torque strips the cam housing in the particle board, leaving a hole that no longer holds. The correct tightness is "snug plus a quarter turn" — the joint should be immovable but you should not feel the cam resist heavily. This is exactly where the misconception about power tools matters: a drill will over-tighten these before you notice, while a hand screwdriver gives you tactile feedback that prevents it.

For **repair**, the most important concept is identifying whether a joint is loose because the fastener has failed or because the joint itself is damaged. A loose screw in solid wood can often be fixed by removing it, filling the hole with a wooden toothpick and wood glue, letting it dry, and re-driving the screw. A cam fastener in stripped particle board is harder — the surrounding material is too soft to hold again, and you may need to add a backer piece of wood behind it. A loose mortise-and-tenon or dowel joint in older furniture is usually a **wood glue job**: open the joint as far as possible without breaking it, work glue into the gap, clamp across the joint (not along it), and hold for at least an hour.

**Wobbly legs** have two common causes: a loose leg-to-apron joint, or a warped or uneven floor. Before disassembling anything, place the furniture on a known flat surface (concrete floor) and identify which leg is causing the rock. If one leg is shorter, a self-adhesive furniture pad (furniture "foot") is faster and more effective than shimming. If the wobble is a joint, flip the piece over and look for the fastener — most leg-to-apron joints use a large bolt into a hanger bolt embedded in the leg, and they just need tightening with a wrench.
