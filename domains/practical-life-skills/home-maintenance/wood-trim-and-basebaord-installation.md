# Wood Trim and Baseboard Installation

## Core Idea
Installing baseboards and trim requires accurate measuring, cutting at proper angles for inside and outside corners, securing with nails or screws into studs, and filling gaps with caulk or wood filler for a finished appearance.

## How It's Best Learned
Start with simple straight runs before tackling corners. Use a miter saw for angle cuts and a stud finder to locate walls for secure fastening. Practice on less visible areas first before prominent rooms.

## Common Misconceptions
All corners are exactly 45-degree angles (real corners are often imperfect); gaps between trim and walls are acceptable (they should be caulked); trim doesn't need to be secured well if it's glued.

## Explainer

With your hand tool skills and knowledge of fastener selection, you have what you need to install trim — but the skill that separates clean results from frustrating failures is accurate angle measurement and the patience to test-fit before fastening. Baseboard installation is fundamentally a geometry problem: you are covering the gap between a vertical wall surface and a horizontal floor surface using pieces of molding that must connect cleanly at corners. The challenge is that walls are not perfectly plumb, floors are not perfectly level, and corners are almost never exactly 90 degrees. Your job is to measure what actually exists and cut to fit it.

The two corner types require different cuts. **Inside corners** — where two walls meet inward, like the corner of a room — are best handled with a **cope joint**, not a miter. A coped joint cuts one piece straight (butt cut) against the wall, then cuts the second piece to the profile of the first, so the second piece overlaps the first. The reason carpenters prefer coping over mitering inside corners is that miter joints open up when the wood moves with humidity changes or the framing settles, leaving a visible gap. A coped joint remains tight because the overlap hides any movement. **Outside corners** — where walls project outward — require mitered joints; both pieces are cut at opposing 45-degree angles (or whatever the actual corner angle measures, which you determine with a digital protractor or a scrap-wood test bevel). Always cut outside miters slightly long and sneak up on the fit with small adjustments.

Fastening correctly keeps trim from pulling away from the wall over time. Your stud-finder locates the framing behind drywall — nails or screws driven into studs hold far better than drywall anchors, which are not rated for the constant tension trim creates as wood expands and contracts seasonally. Use a **finish nailer** (pneumatic or electric) for speed and clean results, or drive **16-gauge finish nails** by hand and set them below the surface with a nail set. For outside corners where the joint must stay tight under stress, drive a nail through the miter on each side at opposing angles — this "cross nails" the joint and prevents it from opening. Fill nail holes with painter's putty or wood filler, allow to dry fully, then sand flush.

The final step — caulking and painting — is what makes the installation look professional. **Paintable latex caulk** fills the inevitable small gaps between trim and wall surface, trim and floor, and at inside corners. Run a thin bead, smooth it with a wet finger, and wipe away excess before it skins over. Do not caulk outside corner miter joints — caulk there will crack as the joint moves; instead, ensure the miter fits tightly and prime the joint before painting. Prime all cut ends of paint-grade wood molding before installation to prevent moisture absorption from the end grain, which causes swelling and paint failure. Sand lightly with 120-grit after the primer coat, then finish-paint for a crisp, durable result.
