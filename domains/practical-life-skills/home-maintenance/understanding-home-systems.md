# Understanding Home Systems and How They Work Together

## Core Idea
A home functions through interconnected systems: electrical (power distribution), plumbing (water supply and drainage), HVAC (heating and cooling), structural (foundation and framing), and exterior (roof, siding, windows). Each system has components, maintenance schedules, and failure points. Homeowners who understand how these systems interact can identify problems early, maintain their home effectively, and know when to call a professional.

## How It's Best Learned
Walk through your own home and identify components of each system. Locate your electrical panel, main water shutoff, HVAC unit, and foundation walls. Refer to your home's blueprints or inspection report if available.

## Common Misconceptions
- Thinking one system failure won't affect others (e.g., poor drainage affecting foundation).
- Assuming maintenance is only needed when something breaks.
- Not understanding that older systems may have different standards than new building codes.

## Explainer

A house is not a single object — it is a collection of interacting systems, each engineered to perform a specific function, each with its own lifespan, maintenance requirements, and failure modes. The most useful mental model is to think of a house the way you think of a human body: the structural system is the skeleton, the plumbing is the circulatory system, the electrical is the nervous system, the HVAC is the respiratory system, and the exterior envelope is the skin. Each system can develop its own problems, but the systems are interdependent in ways that mean a failure in one often produces symptoms in another.

The **structural system** is the foundation and framing — the load-bearing elements that transfer the weight of the building to the ground. Foundation walls, footings, load-bearing interior walls, floor joists, and roof rafters are all structural. What matters for a homeowner is knowing which walls are load-bearing (and therefore cannot be removed without engineering review) and being able to recognize structural distress symptoms: cracks in foundation walls, floors that bounce or sag, doors and windows that stick or won't close, visible deflection in ceiling joists. The **exterior envelope** — roof, siding, windows, and exterior doors — is what keeps weather out. Its job is to shed water, resist air infiltration, and maintain a thermal boundary. Failures here are often slow and cumulative: a cracked caulk joint, a missing shingle, a failed window seal.

The **electrical system** distributes power from the utility connection through the **electrical panel** — a bank of circuit breakers — to outlets, switches, and fixtures throughout the house. Each circuit breaker protects a specific circuit from overload. Knowing where your panel is and how to reset a tripped breaker is basic operational knowledge. The **plumbing system** has two distinct halves: the supply side (pressurized clean water coming in, controlled by the **main shutoff valve**) and the drain-waste-vent side (gravity-fed wastewater leaving the house). These are physically separate systems, and knowing the difference matters when diagnosing a problem. A leak from a supply pipe is continuous and pressurized; a blocked drain affects only drainage from fixtures above the clog.

The **HVAC system** conditions the air: heating it in winter, cooling and dehumidifying it in summer, and circulating it through the house via ductwork. Most residential systems share a single air handler and duct network between heating and cooling. The homeowner's primary maintenance responsibility is filter replacement — a clogged filter restricts airflow, reduces efficiency, and can cause equipment failure. Understanding these five systems at even a basic level — knowing where the main components are, what they do, and what symptoms indicate a problem — is what enables effective home ownership. You don't need to be able to repair every system; you need to be able to recognize a problem early, describe it accurately to a professional, and assess whether it is urgent. That early recognition is almost always the difference between a minor repair and a major one.
