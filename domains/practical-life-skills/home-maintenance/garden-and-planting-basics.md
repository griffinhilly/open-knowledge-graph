# Garden and Planting Basics

## Core Idea
Successful gardening starts with understanding your soil, sun exposure, and hardiness zone — the USDA zone map tells you which plants survive your winters. Amending soil with compost before planting improves drainage in clay soils and water retention in sandy soils. Planting depth, spacing, and sun requirements are printed on seed packets and plant tags and should be followed, not guessed.

## How It's Best Learned
Begin with a small raised bed or container — these provide controlled soil conditions and reduce weeding. Choose plants rated for your hardiness zone and match sun requirements to your actual yard exposure.

## Common Misconceptions
- More water always helps struggling plants — overwatering is the most common cause of houseplant and garden plant death, causing root rot.
- Fertilizer fixes all plant problems — fertilizer feeds healthy plants; it cannot compensate for wrong sun, poor drainage, or disease.

## Explainer

Gardening rewards people who work with conditions rather than against them. The first step is understanding the three variables that determine whether a plant thrives or struggles: **soil quality**, **sun exposure**, and **climate compatibility**. These aren't aesthetic preferences — they're biological requirements. A shade-loving plant placed in full sun will burn and wilt regardless of how well you water it. A sun-loving tomato planted in a shaded corner will produce almost nothing. Reading the label on a plant or seed packet and matching it to your actual yard conditions is the single most predictive factor in success.

**Hardiness zones** are a formal system for communicating climate compatibility. The USDA Plant Hardiness Zone Map divides North America into zones based on average annual minimum winter temperatures — Zone 5 gets colder than Zone 8, for example. A plant labeled "hardy to Zone 6" will survive winter in Zone 6 or warmer, but not in Zone 5. You can look up your zone by zip code. This matters primarily for **perennials** (plants that come back each year) — annuals are replanted each season and zone hardiness is less relevant. When buying perennial plants or shrubs for a permanent garden, only choose plants rated for your zone or hardier.

Soil is the foundation that determines drainage, nutrient availability, and root development. The two most common soil problems are **clay soil** (dense, drains slowly, roots struggle to penetrate, compacts when wet) and **sandy soil** (drains too fast, doesn't hold water or nutrients long enough for roots to absorb them). The solution to both is the same: **compost**. Incorporating 2–4 inches of compost into the top 6–12 inches of soil before planting improves drainage in clay and water retention in sand, while adding nutrients and supporting beneficial soil microbes. You don't need to understand soil chemistry deeply — adding compost reliably moves almost any soil toward a better middle ground. Your measurement skills apply here: knowing your bed's area lets you calculate how many bags of compost to buy.

**Planting depth and spacing** are the two instructions most often ignored. Planting too deep buries the crown (where stem meets root), causing rot in some plants. Planting too shallow leaves roots exposed. Spacing too closely creates competition for water and nutrients, reduces airflow (inviting fungal disease), and produces smaller yields. The information is printed on the seed packet or plant tag and reflects the plant's mature size, not its size at planting. **Watering** follows a consistent principle: most garden plants prefer deep, infrequent watering (which encourages roots to grow deeper, making plants more drought-tolerant) over frequent shallow watering (which keeps roots near the surface). Check soil moisture by pushing your finger an inch into the soil — water when it's dry at that depth, not on a fixed schedule. This one habit prevents overwatering, which is the most common cause of plant failure.
