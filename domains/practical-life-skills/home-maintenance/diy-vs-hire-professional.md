# When to DIY vs. Hire a Professional

## Core Idea
The DIY/hire decision balances cost, skill level, safety risk, and permit requirements. Painting, patching, caulking, unclogging drains, and replacing fixtures are generally safe DIY territory. Electrical panel work, gas line modifications, structural changes, and most permit-required work carry serious risk if done incorrectly and legally require licensed contractors in most jurisdictions. A bad DIY repair can void homeowner's insurance coverage and create liability when selling the home.

## How It's Best Learned
Before starting any repair, search '[repair type] permit required [your state/city]' — permits exist because inspections catch life-safety errors. Use the rule: 'Can a mistake here injure someone or burn down the house?' — if yes, strongly consider professional work.

## Common Misconceptions
- Professional work is always more expensive than DIY — for complex jobs, professionals' efficiency and warranty make their total cost competitive.
- Unpermitted work only matters if you get caught — it creates real problems at home sale, during insurance claims, and when a subsequent owner encounters the code violation.

## Explainer

Every home repair presents the same basic decision: do it yourself or hire someone. The key is knowing which factors actually matter and how to weigh them. The four dimensions to evaluate are cost, skill level, safety risk, and permit requirements — and they are not equally important. Safety and permits act as hard gates; cost and skill are comparisons.

Safety is the primary filter. Some repairs, if done incorrectly, can injure or kill — either immediately (electrical work on live circuits) or later (improperly supported structural members, gas line connections that develop slow leaks). The useful rule of thumb: "Can a mistake here injure someone or burn down the house?" If yes, the job is a professional job, full stop. This includes electrical panel work, any work on gas lines, structural modifications (moving or removing load-bearing walls), and anything involving your home's main water shutoff or sewer connection.

Permit requirements are the second gate. Permits exist not to generate revenue but to ensure that an inspector verifies safety-critical work meets building code. Work that requires a permit — additions, electrical service upgrades, HVAC replacements, deck construction — is work where errors have been deemed serious enough by regulators to warrant oversight. The misconception that permits are only a problem if you get caught during construction is dangerous: unpermitted work appears on title records, is flagged by home inspectors during sales, may void insurance coverage for related damage, and can require costly remediation at your expense years later.

Once you have confirmed a job is safe and permit-compliant to DIY, the cost-versus-skill comparison becomes the main consideration. For trades jobs (electrical, plumbing, HVAC), professionals' efficiency often makes their all-in cost more competitive than it appears — what takes a licensed plumber one hour might take an inexperienced DIYer a full day plus multiple trips to the hardware store. Conversely, cosmetic and finishing work — painting, caulking, tile replacement, fixture swaps, drywall repair — is reliably cost-effective to DIY because the skill ceiling is low, mistakes are reversible, and labor is the dominant cost component in a professional quote. Building your DIY skills on this category of work gives you real savings while keeping risk minimal.
