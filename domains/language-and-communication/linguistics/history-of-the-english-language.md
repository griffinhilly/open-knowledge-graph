# History of the English Language

## Core Idea
English is a West Germanic language brought to Britain by Germanic settlers, traditionally the Angles, Saxons, and Jutes, from the fifth century CE. Scholars divide its history into Old English (to about 1100), Middle English (about 1100 to 1500), Early Modern English (about 1500 to 1700), and Modern English. Over that time English lost most of its noun and adjective inflections, borrowed heavily from Norse, French, and Latin, and went through the Great Vowel Shift, a set of changes to long vowels. Printing fixed many spellings before or during that shift, which is a major reason English spelling and pronunciation often disagree.

## How It's Best Learned
Read the same short passage, such as the Lord's Prayer, in Old, Middle, and Early Modern English and list what changed. Sort a list of everyday words by source (Germanic, Norse, French, Latin) to see layers of borrowing. Map the Great Vowel Shift on a vowel chart and use it to explain spellings such as "bite," "house," and "meet." Connect each period to the historical events that shaped it.

## Common Misconceptions
- Shakespeare wrote in Old English. (Shakespeare wrote Early Modern English; Old English, the language of "Beowulf," is unreadable to modern speakers without study.)
- English descends from Latin. (English is Germanic; it borrowed many Latin and French words, but its core vocabulary and grammar come from Germanic ancestors.)
- English spelling is irregular because it was never standardized. (Much spelling was standardized in the age of printing, before or while the pronunciation of long vowels was still changing.)

## Explainer

English began as the speech of Germanic peoples who crossed the North Sea to Britain in the fifth and sixth centuries CE. Its closest relatives are Frisian, Dutch, and German, not French or Latin, even though English later borrowed from both.

**Old English (about 450 to 1100).** Old English looks foreign to modern readers. Nouns had cases and grammatical gender, and verbs had many endings. Its best-known poem is "Beowulf." From the late eighth century, Vikings raided and then settled in northern and eastern England. Their language, Old Norse, gave English everyday words such as "sky," "egg," "skin," and even the pronouns "they," "them," and "their."

**Middle English (about 1100 to 1500).** The Norman Conquest of 1066 put a French-speaking elite in charge. For about three centuries French dominated law, government, and high culture, and thousands of French words entered English: "court," "judge," "government," "beef," "pork." Meanwhile, most inflectional endings faded, and word order became more fixed. Geoffrey Chaucer, writing in the late fourteenth century, is the most famous Middle English author.

**Early Modern English (about 1500 to 1700).** William Caxton set up the first printing press in England in 1476, and print helped standardize spelling. Across roughly the same centuries, the **Great Vowel Shift** changed the long vowels. The highest ones, /iː/ and /uː/, became diphthongs: "bite" once sounded like "beet," and "house" like "hoose." The other long vowels moved up a step. Because many spellings settled before the shift was complete, English letters still reflect older sounds. This period includes Shakespeare and the King James Bible of 1611. Learned writers also borrowed many words directly from Latin and Greek.

**Modern English (from about 1700).** Samuel Johnson's dictionary appeared in 1755 and became a widely used reference. British colonization spread English worldwide, and English in turn borrowed from many languages, producing distinct varieties such as American, Indian, and Nigerian English.

Across these periods, two large trends stand out. English moved from a language that marked grammar with endings to one that relies on word order and small function words. And its vocabulary became layered, with a Germanic core, a French layer, and a learned Latin layer. That layering explains synonym sets such as "kingly" (Germanic), "royal" (French), and "regal" (Latin), or "ask," "question," and "interrogate," where the native word usually sounds plainest and the Latin one most formal.
