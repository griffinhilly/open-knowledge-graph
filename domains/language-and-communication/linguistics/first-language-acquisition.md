# First Language Acquisition

## Core Idea
First language acquisition traces the remarkably uniform developmental trajectory through which children master their native language. Infants progress from canonical babbling (6–10 months) through the one-word (holophrastic) stage, the two-word stage, and into telegraphic speech, rapidly building toward adult-like grammatical competence by age five. The poverty of the stimulus argument holds that the linguistic input children receive is too incomplete and noisy to explain their achievement through imitation alone, motivating Chomsky's proposal of an innate Language Acquisition Device (LAD) that constrains the hypothesis space for grammar learning.

## How It's Best Learned
Study longitudinal transcripts from the CHILDES database to observe how a single child's language unfolds over months. Track systematic overgeneralization errors (like "goed" or "foots") as evidence that children are constructing rules rather than memorizing forms. Compare the acquisition timeline across typologically different languages to see which milestones are universal.

## Common Misconceptions
- The stages are not rigid gates — children show overlap between stages and considerable individual variation in timing, though the sequence itself is remarkably consistent.
- Babbling is not random noise; it increasingly reflects the phonological patterns of the ambient language, and deaf infants exposed to sign language "babble" with their hands.
- Parents do not explicitly teach grammar to children; corrective feedback ("No, say 'went'") is rare and largely ineffective compared to the child's internal rule-building process.

## Explainer

Your prerequisite study of language acquisition gave you the broad landscape: children learn language, and they do so without formal instruction. First language acquisition narrows the lens to trace exactly how that process unfolds, stage by stage, and to ask the deeper question — how is it even possible? The developmental sequence is one of the most reliably documented facts in all of linguistics, and the explanation for it remains one of the most contested.

The trajectory begins before birth: newborns already show a preference for their mother's voice and for the rhythms of their native language, demonstrating that language exposure shapes perception in the womb. From birth to six months, infants produce vegetative sounds and cooing. Between six and ten months, **canonical babbling** begins — the repetitive consonant-vowel strings ("bababa," "mamama") that sound like language even though they are not yet words. Crucially, the phonology of babbling begins to narrow toward the sounds of the ambient language. Japanese-learning infants start dropping the contrast between /r/ and /l/ around this time; English-learning infants keep it. Babbling is not random practice — it is already language-specific phonological tuning.

The **holophrastic stage** (around 12–18 months) produces single-word utterances that function as whole sentences in context. When a child says "milk," they may mean "I want milk," "the milk spilled," or "that's milk" — the word is underdetermined, and the caregiver uses context to interpret. The **two-word stage** (around 18–24 months) introduces the first syntactic structure: children begin combining words in consistent patterns that reflect grammatical relations ("more milk," "daddy go," "no bed") rather than random pairing. By age two and a half to three, **telegraphic speech** appears — multi-word utterances with function words and inflections largely absent but content words ordered correctly ("Want big cookie," "Doggy eating bone"). Full grammatical competence, including complex clauses and irregular morphology, is largely in place by age five.

The most theoretically significant observation is **overgeneralization**: children who correctly use "went" at age two will later produce "goed," then eventually return to "went." This u-shaped learning curve is powerful evidence that children are not memorizing forms — they are constructing rules. A child who produces "goed" has acquired the rule for English past tense (add -ed) and is applying it productively, without knowing it is irregular. This is what Chomsky's **Language Acquisition Device (LAD)** hypothesis is responding to: children extract abstract grammatical rules from messy, incomplete input with remarkable speed and accuracy. The **poverty of the stimulus argument** makes this precise — the input children hear is too fragmentary, noisy, and limited to fully specify the grammar they end up with. They never hear many of the sentence types they later produce correctly, and they are never told which sentence types are ungrammatical. Something in the learner must be doing cognitive work that the input alone cannot explain.

The competing view — that language learning is a sophisticated statistical learning task, not requiring a dedicated grammatical module — is equally serious and has gained empirical traction from studies showing that infants are powerful probabilistic learners. The theoretical debate between nativist and constructivist accounts is unresolved, but the empirical facts are not in dispute: children across all languages, all cultures, and all levels of explicit instruction converge on a shared developmental timeline and make the same types of systematic errors. Whatever explanation ultimately proves correct must account for both the universality and the apparent ease of first language acquisition in the face of impoverished input.
