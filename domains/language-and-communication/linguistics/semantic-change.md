# Semantic Change

## Core Idea
Semantic change is change in what a word means over time, while its form may stay recognizable. Historical linguists classify the common patterns: broadening (a meaning becomes more general), narrowing (more specific), amelioration (more positive), pejoration (more negative), and shifts driven by metaphor and metonymy. Old English "dēor," the ancestor of "deer," meant any wild animal and later narrowed to one kind; "silly" once meant blessed or happy. These changes are gradual, usually pass through a stage where old and new senses coexist, and follow recurring paths across languages.

## How It's Best Learned
Give students a set of etymologies from a historical dictionary and have them classify each change by type. Build timelines for one word, such as "nice," to show the intermediate senses. Look for change in progress in current slang, such as words that have broadened or flipped in evaluation within living memory. Connect each change to a mechanism: metaphor, metonymy, social attitudes, or reinterpretation in context.

## Common Misconceptions
- The original meaning of a word is its true or correct meaning. (This is the etymological fallacy; a word means what its current speakers use it to mean.)
- Semantic change is a modern sign of careless usage. (Meanings have changed throughout recorded history; many familiar English words, such as "nice," "meat," and "silly," have shifted meaning over the centuries.)
- Meaning changes happen suddenly. (Changes usually spread gradually, with old and new senses overlapping for generations.)

## Explainer

Words keep their sounds more or less recognizable for centuries, but their meanings often wander. Studying where they go reveals patterns that recur across languages.

**Broadening** (generalization) widens a word's range. "Holiday" comes from "holy day," a religious feast, but now covers any day off. In Middle English "dogge" seems to have named a particular kind of dog before it became the general word.

**Narrowing** (specialization) shrinks the range. Old English "mete" meant food in general; "meat" now means animal flesh. Old English "dēor" meant any wild animal; "deer" now names one family of animals. In Middle English "girl" could mean a child of either sex.

**Amelioration** moves a word toward a more positive meaning. "Knight" comes from Old English "cniht," a boy or servant. "Nice" traces back through French to Latin "nescius," meaning ignorant, and in Middle English meant foolish before passing through senses such as "precise" on the way to "pleasant."

**Pejoration** moves a word toward a negative meaning. "Silly" comes from Old English "gesǣlig," meaning happy or blessed. "Villain" once meant a farm worker. "Hussy" developed from "housewife."

Two mechanisms drive many of these changes.

- **Metaphor** extends a word by similarity: we "grasp" an idea as we grasp an object, and a computer "mouse" resembles the animal.
- **Metonymy** extends a word by association: "the crown" stands for the monarchy, and "the press" stands for journalists.

Other changes come from **reinterpretation in context**. If a word often appears in situations with a certain flavor, listeners may take that flavor as part of its meaning. Social attitudes also matter. Words for groups with low status, such as rural laborers, have often picked up negative meanings.

Two points guard against common mistakes. First, change is gradual. Older and newer senses overlap, sometimes for centuries, as with "meat" in the phrase "meat and drink." Second, an earlier meaning is not the "real" one. Claiming that "nice" should still mean ignorant, or that "decimate" can only mean killing one in ten, is the **etymological fallacy**. Linguists describe meaning by present use.

Semantic change also connects to grammaticalization, in which words with concrete meanings, such as "going to" for motion, gradually become grammatical markers, such as a future tense.
