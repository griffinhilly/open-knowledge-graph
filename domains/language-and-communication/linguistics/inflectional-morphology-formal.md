# Formal Analysis of Inflectional Morphology

## Core Idea
Formal inflectional morphology encodes grammatical features—tense, aspect, mood, agreement, case—through systematic affixation or stem changes. Inflected forms are generated by feature-checking: a verb agrees with its subject by marking the subject's person and number. Features are abstract and universally motivated; languages differ in which features require overt morphological realization.

## Explainer

You already know from studying inflectional morphology that languages mark grammatical distinctions like tense, number, and case through affixes and stem changes. You also know from feature-agreement checking that grammatical features must match across related words — verbs agree with their subjects, and certain forms require corresponding forms elsewhere in the clause. Formal analysis brings these threads together by treating morphology not as a list of patterns to memorize but as a **feature calculus**: words carry abstract feature bundles, and morphological realization is the process by which those features receive phonological form.

The central concept is the **feature**. Grammatical features like [TENSE: past], [PERSON: 3rd], [NUMBER: singular], and [CASE: nominative] are abstract, language-independent categories. What varies across languages is not which features exist — tense, agreement, and case appear throughout the world's languages — but which features require **overt morphological realization**. English marks past tense overtly with "-ed" but has a thin agreement system (only the third-person singular "-s" in the present). Spanish marks both tense and agreement richly across all persons and numbers; Mandarin marks neither overtly. The features are present in all three languages; they differ only in whether they are phonologically visible on the surface.

**Feature-checking** provides the formal mechanism for agreement. In "She runs," the verb must be licensed by its subject. Formally, "she" carries features [PERSON: 3rd, NUMBER: singular], and the verb checks its agreement features against those values. The "-s" suffix is the overt spell-out of a successful agreement check. When a language requires case marking, the same logic applies: a noun in subject position carries nominative case features; one in object position carries accusative case features; the case suffix spells out which case the noun was assigned. Mismatches — "she run" — are ungrammatical precisely because the agreement features fail to check correctly.

An important insight is that **absence of overt morphology is not absence of features**. When English uses the bare verb form after a modal ("She can run"), the verb still carries tense features — they simply are not realized by a suffix in this context. When English nouns lack case suffixes, they still receive case from their syntactic position. Formal morphology separates two questions that surface descriptions typically run together: (1) which features are present in the syntax, and (2) whether those features receive phonological expression. This separation explains why speakers reliably track agreement and case even in languages where marking is sparse — the features are doing syntactic work regardless of whether they appear on the surface.
