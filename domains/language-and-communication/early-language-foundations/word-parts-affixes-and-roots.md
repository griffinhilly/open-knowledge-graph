# Word Parts: Prefixes, Suffixes, and Roots

## Core Idea
Many English words are built from smaller meaningful parts: a base or root, plus prefixes added to the front and suffixes added to the end. Knowing common parts lets a reader work out unfamiliar words, as in "un + help + ful" or "tele + graph." Prefixes usually change a word's meaning (happy, unhappy), while suffixes often change its word class or grammatical form (teach, teacher). Many roots in academic English come from Latin and Greek, so a small set of roots unlocks a large number of words.

## How It's Best Learned
Start with a few high-frequency prefixes and suffixes (un-, re-, dis-, pre-, -ful, -less, -er, -able) and have students take real words apart and put them back together. Build "word families" on a chart around one root, such as port (carry): transport, import, export, portable. Always check the meaning a student builds from the parts against the sentence the word appears in. Point out when word parts mislead.

## Common Misconceptions
- Any word that starts with the same letters as a prefix has that prefix. (The "re" in "read" and the "un" in "uncle" are not prefixes; removing them does not leave a base word with a related meaning.)
- Each prefix has exactly one meaning. (Un- can mean "not," as in unkind, or "reverse an action," as in unlock.)
- Adding up the parts always gives the exact meaning. (Word parts give a clue; context confirms the meaning. "Understand" does not mean "stand under.")

## Explainer

When you meet a long, unfamiliar word, you do not have to treat it as one big puzzle. Many English words are made of parts, and each part carries meaning. Learning to spot those parts is one of the most useful vocabulary skills a reader can have.

There are three kinds of parts.

- A **base word** or **root** carries the main meaning. "Help" is a base word that can stand alone. "Port," meaning carry, is a root that usually needs other parts attached.
- A **prefix** goes at the start of a word and changes its meaning. Common prefixes include un- (not), re- (again), dis- (not, opposite of), pre- (before), and mis- (wrongly).
- A **suffix** goes at the end. Suffixes often change how a word works in a sentence. The suffix -er turns the verb "teach" into the noun "teacher." The suffix -ful turns the noun "hope" into the adjective "hopeful." Others include -less (without), -able (can be), and -ly (in a certain way).

Here is the strategy. Find the base or root first. Then look at what is added before and after it. Put the meanings together, and check that your meaning makes sense in the sentence. For "unbreakable," the base is "break," -able adds "can be," and un- adds "not." The word means "cannot be broken."

Roots from Latin and Greek are especially useful in upper elementary grades. A few examples:

- port (carry): transport, portable, export
- dict (say): predict, dictionary, contradict
- rupt (break): erupt, interrupt, rupture
- tele (far) and phon (sound): telephone, microphone, phonics
- bio (life): biology, biography

Once a student knows "tele" and "graph," words such as "telegraph" and "telescope" become less mysterious.

Word parts are a clue, not a guarantee. Some words only look as if they contain a prefix. The "re" in "ready" is not a prefix, and the "dis" in "dish" is not either. Some words have drifted from the meaning of their parts over centuries. That is why the last step, checking the meaning against the sentence, always matters. Used together with context clues, word parts give readers a powerful way to grow their vocabulary on their own.
