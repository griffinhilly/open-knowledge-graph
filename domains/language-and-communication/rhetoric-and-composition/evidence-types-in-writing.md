# Evidence Types in Writing

## Core Idea
Writers draw on several categories of evidence — statistics and data, direct quotations, illustrative examples, expert testimony, personal anecdotes, and analogies — each with distinct rhetorical strengths and limitations. Statistics lend empirical weight but can obscure context; quotations preserve an author's exact language but require interpretation; examples make abstractions concrete but may not generalize; expert testimony invokes authority but depends on the expert's relevance. Choosing the right type of evidence for a given claim and audience is itself a rhetorical decision, and strong essays typically layer multiple evidence types to address different dimensions of a claim.

## How It's Best Learned
Catalog the evidence types in a published argument and evaluate which are most persuasive and why. Then revise a draft paragraph by swapping one evidence type for another — replacing a statistic with a narrative example, or an example with expert testimony — to see how the paragraph's persuasive character changes.

## Common Misconceptions
- Statistics are the strongest form of evidence in all contexts; in narrative or humanistic arguments, a well-chosen example or quotation may be far more persuasive than a number.
- Personal anecdotes are never appropriate in academic writing; they are inappropriate as sole evidence for general claims, but they can effectively illustrate a phenomenon or establish exigence.

- question: "A student writing about homelessness opens with the statistic: '500,000 people are homeless in the U.S. on any given night.' A classmate suggests opening instead with a brief personal narrative about one person's experience. Why might the narrative be MORE effective as an opening?"

    - "Statistics are only appropriate in scientific writing, not essays about social issues"
    - "The statistic is likely inaccurate, making the narrative a safer choice"
    - "The narrative answers 'has this happened to a real person?' and creates human stakes that make readers care before the argument begins"
    - "Personal narratives are inherently more persuasive than statistics in all contexts"
  answer: 2
  explanation: "An essay's opening establishes exigence — why the reader should care. A personal anecdote answers 'has this happened to a real person?' and creates emotional stakes, which is the job an opening typically needs to do. This doesn't make the statistic wrong or inappropriate later in the essay; it makes it the wrong tool for this particular rhetorical moment. Statistics answer 'how widespread?' which is a question readers will want answered after they already care, not before."

- question: "An essayist arguing that social media harms teenagers' mental health supports her claim with only peer-reviewed statistics. Her essay receives feedback that it is 'technically correct but strangely unconvincing.' What is most likely missing?"
  type: multiple-choice
  options:
    - "More statistics from additional studies to build cumulative empirical weight"
    - "Expert testimony from therapists confirming the statistics' interpretation"
    - "Illustrative examples or anecdotes that make the abstract pattern concrete and emotionally real"
    - "An analogy comparing social media to a more familiar substance addiction"
  answer: 2
  explanation: "Statistics establish scale and empirical weight, but they answer 'how widespread?' not 'what does this actually look like?' Without examples or narrative, a pattern remains abstract — the reader understands the claim without feeling it. The missing layer is evidence that makes the statistics concrete. This illustrates the key insight: evidence types complement each other rather than substitute. The misconception is that statistics alone are sufficient because they are 'strongest'; in humanistic and persuasive writing, a well-chosen example may do more persuasive work than a pile of numbers."

- question: "Expert testimony is most persuasive when the expert's specific area of expertise is directly relevant to the claim being made."
  type: true-false
  answer: true
  explanation: "Expert testimony borrows credibility from recognized authority — but that authority only transfers within the expert's actual domain. A neuroscientist's testimony about rhetoric, or an economist's opinion on nutritional science, is opinion from outside the relevant field, not expert testimony. The specific domain relevance is what makes the evidence type work. A writer invoking 'a Harvard professor' without verifying the professor's relevant specialty has confused credential with expertise."

- question: "Personal anecdotes are never appropriate in academic writing because they cannot support general empirical claims."
  type: true-false
  answer: false
  explanation: "Personal anecdotes are inappropriate as the sole support for general empirical claims — one person's experience cannot prove universal truth. But they serve legitimate rhetorical functions in academic writing: establishing exigence, making an abstract pattern concrete, or illustrating why a question matters. Strong essays often open with an anecdote and immediately follow with statistical or expert evidence. The misconception overgeneralizes a real limitation (anecdotes can't prove universals) into a blanket prohibition (anecdotes are never appropriate)."

- question: "Why is choosing which type of evidence to use itself a rhetorical decision, rather than simply a practical one?"
  type: short-answer
  answer: "Because different evidence types answer different reader doubts — statistics answer 'how widespread?', anecdotes answer 'has this happened to a real person?', expert testimony answers 'who authoritative agrees?' — and the right choice depends on which doubt your specific audience is most likely to have about your specific claim."
  explanation: "Evidence effectiveness is relative to audience and claim. A skeptical empiricist needs data; a skeptical humanist may need a well-chosen example; a general audience may need a narrative to care before they'll engage with numbers. Treating evidence selection as purely practical — any evidence counts the same — leads to technically supported but rhetorically ineffective arguments. The discipline of rhetorical analysis begins by asking: what doubt is my reader most likely to have, and which evidence type dissolves exactly that doubt?"

## Explainer

You already understand that claims require support — that writing without evidence remains mere assertion. The next layer is that not all evidence does the same work. Each evidence type addresses a different question a skeptical reader might ask, and choosing poorly means your claim may technically be supported without being believed.

**Statistics and data** answer the question "how widespread or measurable is this?" They carry the authority of empirical observation and lend precision to claims about scale, frequency, or change over time. But a statistic stripped of context can mislead — a 50% increase sounds dramatic until you learn the base was two cases. **Quotation** answers "what exactly did the source say?" Quoting preserves an author's specific language, which matters when the precise wording is itself the subject of analysis or when paraphrase would blur the meaning. Quotation alone, however, is not interpretation — every quotation needs a sentence before it and after it: context, then the quote, then your reading of what it shows.

**Illustrative examples** answer "can you show me one case?" They make abstractions tangible and help readers understand how a principle operates in practice. A well-chosen example can do more persuasive work than a paragraph of abstract reasoning, especially for readers encountering a concept for the first time. The risk is overgeneralization: one vivid example does not prove a universal claim. **Expert testimony** answers "who authoritative agrees?" It invokes credibility borrowed from a recognized specialist, but its strength depends entirely on the expert's relevance to the specific claim. A neuroscientist's testimony about rhetoric is not expert testimony — it is an opinion from someone outside the relevant field.

**Personal anecdote** answers "has this actually happened to a real person?" It creates immediate connection and establishes human stakes, making it effective for introducing a problem or establishing why a reader should care. Its limitation is the same as the example: one person's experience cannot support a general empirical claim. The anecdote builds interest and opens inquiry; it does not close the argument. **Analogy** is different in kind from the others — it does not add new evidence but clarifies the logical structure of a claim by mapping it onto a familiar domain. Analogies can illuminate and persuade, but they can also mislead when the two domains differ in important ways.

Strong essays don't pick a single evidence type and stay there. They layer: a statistic establishes scale, a quotation locates the problem in an authoritative source, an example makes it concrete, and a brief analogy connects it to what the reader already understands. The decision about which to use where is itself a **rhetorical decision** — it depends on your claim, your audience's expectations, and the kind of doubt you are trying to dissolve. Analyzing evidence choices in published writing trains you to see this architecture clearly before building your own.
