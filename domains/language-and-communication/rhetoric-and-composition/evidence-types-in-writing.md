# Evidence Types in Writing

## Core Idea
Writers draw on several categories of evidence — statistics and data, direct quotations, illustrative examples, expert testimony, personal anecdotes, and analogies — each with distinct rhetorical strengths and limitations. Statistics lend empirical weight but can obscure context; quotations preserve an author's exact language but require interpretation; examples make abstractions concrete but may not generalize; expert testimony invokes authority but depends on the expert's relevance. Choosing the right type of evidence for a given claim and audience is itself a rhetorical decision, and strong essays typically layer multiple evidence types to address different dimensions of a claim.

## How It's Best Learned
Catalog the evidence types in a published argument and evaluate which are most persuasive and why. Then revise a draft paragraph by swapping one evidence type for another — replacing a statistic with a narrative example, or an example with expert testimony — to see how the paragraph's persuasive character changes.

## Common Misconceptions
- Statistics are the strongest form of evidence in all contexts; in narrative or humanistic arguments, a well-chosen example or quotation may be far more persuasive than a number.
- Personal anecdotes are never appropriate in academic writing; they are inappropriate as sole evidence for general claims, but they can effectively illustrate a phenomenon or establish exigence.
