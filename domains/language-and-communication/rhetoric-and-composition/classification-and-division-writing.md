# Classification and Division Writing

## Core Idea
Classification and division is a pattern of development that organizes a subject into parts or types. Division breaks one whole into its components (the branches of government, the parts of a cell). Classification sorts many individual items into groups by a shared characteristic (types of voters, kinds of friends). A good classification uses one consistent principle for sorting, creates categories that do not overlap, and covers the subject well enough for the writer's purpose. The thesis should say why the categories matter.

## How It's Best Learned
Give students a mixed set of items, such as twenty songs or restaurants, and have them sort by two different principles; discuss how each principle produces different categories and serves a different purpose. Then have them check a draft scheme for overlap and missing cases. Model a thesis that adds a point ("Most diets fall into three types, and only one is supported by long-term evidence") rather than a bare list. Read a published classification essay and map its principle and categories.

## Common Misconceptions
- Any set of groups counts as a classification. (Groups must come from one consistent principle; "fast food, healthy food, and Italian food" mixes speed, health, and cuisine.)
- More categories make a stronger essay. (Too many categories turn the essay into a list; the right number serves the writer's purpose.)
- Classification and division are the same operation. (Division starts with one thing and breaks it into parts; classification starts with many things and groups them.)

## Explainer

People sort things all the time. Grocery stores group food into aisles. Libraries sort books by subject. Biologists classify living things. Classification and division writing turns this everyday habit into a way of organizing an essay.

The pattern has two related moves.

**Division** begins with one subject and breaks it into parts. A writer might divide a school day into morning classes, lunch, and afternoon activities, or divide the federal government into legislative, executive, and judicial branches.

**Classification** begins with many items and sorts them into groups. A writer might classify the people at a gym into regulars, January newcomers, and social visitors. Each person goes into one group.

Three rules make the pattern work.

1. **Use one principle of classification.** The principle is the basis for sorting. Cars can be sorted by size, fuel type, or price, but not by all three in one scheme. Mixing principles produces overlapping groups: "electric cars, luxury cars, and compact cars" fails because a car can be all three.
2. **Make categories distinct.** Each item should fit one category. If many items fit two, the principle needs tightening.
3. **Cover the subject well enough for your purpose.** A classification of transportation that leaves out walking may be fine for an essay on commuting costs, but not for one on public health. The categories need not include every rare case, but they should not leave out a major group.

The thesis is where many drafts fall short. "There are three types of teachers" announces a structure but makes no claim. A stronger thesis adds a point: "Students meet three types of teachers, and the one who seems strictest often teaches the most." Now the categories serve an argument.

Organization usually follows the categories. Each body paragraph or section introduces one category, defines it, gives examples, and explains how it differs from the others. Writers often order categories for effect, saving the most important or surprising for last. Transitions such as "a second type" or "unlike the first group" help readers see the structure.

Classification also supports other kinds of writing. A research paper might classify approaches to a problem before evaluating them. An argument might classify objections before answering them. Learning to sort cleanly is a thinking skill as much as a writing skill.
