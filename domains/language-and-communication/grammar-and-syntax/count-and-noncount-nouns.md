# Count and Noncount Nouns

## Core Idea
English nouns fall into two grammatical groups. Count nouns name things English treats as separate units (one apple, three apples), so they have plural forms and can take "a/an" and numbers. Noncount nouns, also called mass or uncountable nouns, name things treated as an undivided whole (water, rice, furniture, advice), so they normally have no plural and take a singular verb. The difference controls which articles and quantifiers fit: "many apples" but "much water," "fewer cars" but "less traffic."

## How It's Best Learned
Sort nouns into two columns, then test each with "a ___" and "two ___s." Show how a noncount noun is counted with a unit word: "a cup of coffee," "a piece of advice," "two loaves of bread." Pair the nouns with quantifiers (many/much, few/little, number/amount) in sentence-building practice. Point out nouns that switch groups with a change in meaning, such as "light" and "a light."

## Common Misconceptions
- Whether a noun is countable depends on whether you could count the thing in real life. (Rice grains can be counted, yet "rice" is noncount; the category is grammatical and varies between languages.)
- "Informations," "furnitures," and "advices" are correct plurals. (These are noncount in standard English; use "pieces of information," "items of furniture," or "some advice.")
- A noun is always one type or the other. (Many nouns work both ways with different meanings: "Paper is cheap" versus "I read two papers.")

## Explainer

Try putting "a" in front of these words: a chair, a dog, a water, a furniture. The first two sound normal. The last two sound wrong to a native speaker. That reaction reveals a grammatical difference between **count nouns** and **noncount nouns**.

**Count nouns** refer to things English treats as separate units. They have singular and plural forms and work with numbers.

- a book, two books
- an idea, several ideas

**Noncount nouns** refer to things treated as a single mass or a general category. They have no ordinary plural and take a singular verb.

- water, sand, rice, milk (substances)
- furniture, luggage, equipment (categories of objects)
- advice, information, happiness (abstract ideas)

"The furniture is new," not "the furnitures are new."

The surprising part is that the category is grammatical and does not track the real world. A pile of furniture contains individual chairs you could count. Rice comes in grains you could count. English grammar still treats both as masses. Other languages draw the line differently. The word "information" is noncount in English, but its equivalents in some other languages are countable, which is one reason English learners say "informations."

To count a noncount noun, English adds a **unit word**: a glass of milk, a grain of sand, a piece of furniture, an item of news.

Countability decides which small words go with a noun:

| With count nouns | With noncount nouns |
|---|---|
| a / an (singular) | (no a / an) |
| many | much |
| few, a few | little, a little |
| fewer | less |
| number of | amount of |

"How many chairs?" but "How much furniture?" In formal writing, "fewer" goes with plural count nouns ("fewer mistakes") and "less" with noncount nouns ("less noise"). In everyday speech, "less" often appears with plural nouns, and phrases such as "10 items or less" are widespread. Many usage guides accept "or less" in that fixed phrase but still recommend "fewer" before plural count nouns in formal writing.

Finally, many nouns live in both groups with a shift in meaning. "Chicken" is noncount as food ("We had chicken") and count as an animal ("three chickens"). "Time" is noncount as a quantity ("not much time") and count as an occasion ("three times"). When a noun seems to break the rules, check whether its meaning has changed.
