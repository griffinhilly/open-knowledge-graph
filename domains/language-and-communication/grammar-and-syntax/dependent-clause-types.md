# Dependent Clause Types: Nominal, Adverbial, and Adjectival

## Core Idea
Dependent clauses are classified by the grammatical role they play in a sentence. A nominal (noun) clause functions as a subject, object, or complement (What she said surprised everyone). An adverbial clause modifies a verb, adjective, or adverb, answering when, why, how, or under what condition (Because the road was icy, we drove slowly). An adjectival clause modifies a noun, typically introduced by a relative pronoun (The book that you recommended was excellent). Identifying clause function — not just clause structure — is what enables writers to build complex, precise sentences.

## How It's Best Learned
Replace each dependent clause with a single word that performs the same function: a noun for nominal clauses, an adverb for adverbial clauses, an adjective for adjectival clauses. If "What she said" can be replaced by "Her statement" (a noun), the clause is nominal. This substitution test makes abstract clause classification concrete.

## Common Misconceptions
- Classifying dependent clauses by their introductory word alone; "that" can introduce nominal clauses (I know that she left) or adjectival clauses (The car that I bought is blue), so the word itself does not determine function.
- Confusing adverbial clauses with adjectival clauses when both appear after a noun; the key question is whether the clause modifies the noun (adjectival) or the verb (adverbial).
