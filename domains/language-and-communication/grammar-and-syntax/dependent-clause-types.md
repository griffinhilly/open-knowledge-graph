# Dependent Clause Types: Nominal, Adverbial, and Adjectival

## Core Idea
Dependent clauses are classified by the grammatical role they play in a sentence. A nominal (noun) clause functions as a subject, object, or complement (What she said surprised everyone). An adverbial clause modifies a verb, adjective, or adverb, answering when, why, how, or under what condition (Because the road was icy, we drove slowly). An adjectival clause modifies a noun, typically introduced by a relative pronoun (The book that you recommended was excellent). Identifying clause function — not just clause structure — is what enables writers to build complex, precise sentences.

## How It's Best Learned
Replace each dependent clause with a single word that performs the same function: a noun for nominal clauses, an adverb for adverbial clauses, an adjective for adjectival clauses. If "What she said" can be replaced by "Her statement" (a noun), the clause is nominal. This substitution test makes abstract clause classification concrete.

## Common Misconceptions
- Classifying dependent clauses by their introductory word alone; "that" can introduce nominal clauses (I know that she left) or adjectival clauses (The car that I bought is blue), so the word itself does not determine function.
- Confusing adverbial clauses with adjectival clauses when both appear after a noun; the key question is whether the clause modifies the noun (adjectival) or the verb (adverbial).

## Explainer

You already know that a dependent clause has a subject and verb but cannot stand alone as a sentence — it needs an independent clause to complete its meaning. The next question is: what work is the dependent clause doing in the sentence? That is the question of clause type, and it has three answers: nominal, adverbial, and adjectival.

A **nominal clause** (or noun clause) does the job of a noun. Wherever a noun can appear — as a subject, direct object, indirect object, or complement — a nominal clause can appear too. "What she said surprised everyone" — here "what she said" is the subject of the sentence. You could replace it with a single noun like "Her statement" and the sentence would still work: "Her statement surprised everyone." That substitution test is the reliable shortcut: if the clause behaves like a noun, it is nominal. Common introducers are *that*, *what*, *whether*, *who*, *whoever*, and *if* in embedded questions.

An **adverbial clause** does the job of an adverb — it modifies a verb, adjective, or entire clause, answering when, where, why, how, or under what condition. "Because the road was icy, we drove slowly" — "because the road was icy" could be replaced by a single adverb like "Consequently" or omitted, and the main clause would survive. Adverbial clauses are introduced by subordinating conjunctions: *because*, *although*, *when*, *while*, *if*, *unless*, *since*, *after*, *before*. The logical relationship they encode — cause, concession, time, condition — is expressed by the subordinating conjunction itself.

An **adjectival clause** (or relative clause) does the job of an adjective — it modifies a noun. "The book that you recommended was excellent" — "that you recommended" modifies "book," narrowing down which book. Replace it with a single adjective and the structure still works: "The recommended book was excellent." Adjectival clauses are typically introduced by relative pronouns: *that*, *which*, *who*, *whom*, *whose*. Notice that the introductory word alone does not identify clause type: *that* introduces both nominal clauses ("I know *that* she left") and adjectival clauses ("The car *that* I bought is blue"). The function in the sentence, not the connector word, is definitive.
