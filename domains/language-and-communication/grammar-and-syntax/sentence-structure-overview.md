# Sentence Structure: Simple, Compound, and Complex

## Core Idea
Sentences are built in three main structural patterns: simple sentences (one independent clause), compound sentences (two or more independent clauses joined by a conjunction), and complex sentences (an independent clause joined with one or more dependent clauses). Understanding these patterns helps you write varied and interesting prose.

## How It's Best Learned
Diagram sentences to identify the clauses and their relationships. Practice converting simple sentences into compound and complex sentences.

## Common Misconceptions
- Longer sentences are more complex; complexity is determined by the number of clauses and their relationships, not length.
- Complex sentences are better than simple sentences; all three patterns are valuable and serve different purposes.

## Explainer

From your study of sentence basics, you know that every sentence needs at minimum a subject and a predicate. The three structural patterns — simple, compound, and complex — are just ways of counting and connecting clauses. Mastering these patterns gives you conscious control over the rhythm and logic of your writing.

A **simple sentence** contains exactly one **independent clause**: a subject, a verb, and optionally additional information. "The storm arrived." "The exhausted runner crossed the finish line." Length doesn't disqualify a sentence from being simple — even a long sentence with many phrases but only one clause is still simple. Simple sentences hit hard because they carry no qualifications. They work especially well for emphasis, drama, or clarity after complicated material.

A **compound sentence** joins two or more independent clauses, typically with a **coordinating conjunction** (for, and, nor, but, or, yet, so — the FANBOYS) or a semicolon. "The storm arrived, and the power went out." Each clause could stand alone; the conjunction signals the relationship between them (addition, contrast, cause-effect). Compound sentences imply that both ideas carry equal weight — they stand side by side. Use this pattern when two events or ideas genuinely belong on equal footing.

A **complex sentence** pairs one independent clause with at least one **dependent (subordinate) clause** — a clause that cannot stand alone because it begins with a subordinating conjunction (although, because, when, if, since, while, etc.) or a relative pronoun (who, which, that). "Although the storm arrived, we stayed outside." The dependent clause signals that one idea is subordinate to — less central than, conditional on, or explanatory of — the main idea. This is the structural backbone of analytical writing: you can embed conditions, causes, concessions, and qualifications without losing your main claim. Varying all three patterns fluidly is what separates mechanically correct prose from writing that readers actually enjoy.
