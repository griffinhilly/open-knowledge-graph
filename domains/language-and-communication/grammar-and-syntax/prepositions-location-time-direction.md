# Prepositions: Location, Time, and Direction

## Core Idea
Prepositions are small words that show relationships between nouns and other words in a sentence, particularly indicating location ('in', 'on', 'under'), time ('before', 'after', 'during'), and direction ('toward', 'through', 'across'). Choosing the right preposition ensures your meaning is precise and unambiguous.

## How It's Best Learned
Look for prepositions in sentences and identify which type (location, time, direction) each one represents. Create sentences using each type of preposition.

## Common Misconceptions
- Prepositions always indicate spatial relationships; they can indicate time, direction, and many other relationships.
- Prepositions are always followed immediately by a noun; they are always followed by a noun phrase, which can include adjectives and other modifiers.

## Explainer

You already know that prepositions are **function words** — small words that connect a noun phrase to the rest of the sentence and express a relationship between them. This topic sharpens that knowledge by mapping the three core relationship types: location, time, and direction. Each type answers a different question the listener might have about a noun: *Where is it? When did it happen? Which way is it going?*

**Location prepositions** answer "where?" by placing something in space relative to a reference point. *In* indicates enclosure or containment ("the keys are in the drawer," "she lives in Chicago"). *On* indicates surface contact ("the book is on the table," "the app is on your phone"). *Under* places something below a reference point; *above* reverses it. *Between* requires two reference points; *among* works for three or more. The choice of location preposition is often idiomatic — English says "in the morning" and "on Tuesday" and "at noon," not because the logic demands it but because convention has fixed those pairings. When you learn prepositions, you are partly learning vocabulary, not just grammar.

**Time prepositions** answer "when?" — and interestingly, they borrow the same three core words English uses for space. *In* marks large time spans ("in the 1990s," "in March," "in the afternoon"). *On* marks specific calendar days ("on Monday," "on your birthday," "on March 15"). *At* marks precise clock times or moments ("at 3 PM," "at midnight," "at noon"). Beyond this triad, *before* and *after* sequence events relative to each other; *during* marks a span that contains another event; *since* marks a starting point extending to now; *until* marks an endpoint. Recognizing which type of time reference is being expressed — span, sequence, point — will help you select the right preposition.

**Direction prepositions** answer "which way?" and describe movement along a path. *Toward* points in the direction of a destination without implying arrival; *away from* points in the opposite direction. *Through* describes passage into and out of a space ("drive through the tunnel"). *Across* describes traversal of a surface or gap ("swim across the lake"). *Into* describes movement ending inside a container; *out of* reverses it. *Along* describes movement parallel to a length. The difference between direction and location prepositions is often a matter of whether the sentence implies movement: "She walked *to* the store" (direction, movement) versus "She was *at* the store" (location, static). A prepositional phrase built on a direction preposition typically modifies a verb of movement; one built on a location preposition typically modifies a state or position.
