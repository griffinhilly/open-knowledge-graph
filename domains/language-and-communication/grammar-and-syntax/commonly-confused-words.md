# Commonly Confused Words and Homophones

## Core Idea
Some English words sound alike or nearly alike but have different spellings and meanings, so writers mix them up: there, their, they're; its, it's; your, you're; to, too, two. Other pairs, such as affect and effect or then and than, are confused because they look or sound similar. The reliable fix is to know each word's part of speech and job in the sentence, and to test contractions by expanding them. Spell-checkers often miss these errors because each word is spelled correctly.

## How It's Best Learned
Teach one set at a time with a memory test tied to grammar, such as "If you can say 'they are,' write 'they're.'" Have students sort sentences, then edit their own drafts by searching for each problem word. Pair contractions with their expanded forms (it's = it is) so the apostrophe has a clear reason. Revisit the sets during editing, since errors return under time pressure.

## Common Misconceptions
- "It's" is the possessive form of "it," like "the dog's." (Personal possessive pronouns take no apostrophe: its, yours, theirs. "It's" always means "it is" or "it has.")
- Spell-check will catch these mistakes. (Each word is a real word, so many checkers accept "their going" or "your welcome" without complaint.)
- "Affect" and "effect" are interchangeable. (In most uses "affect" is a verb meaning to influence, and "effect" is a noun meaning a result.)

```yaml
- question: "Which sentence is correct?"
  type: multiple-choice
  options:
    - "The team lost it's first game."
    - "The team lost its first game."
    - "The team lost its' first game."
    - "The team lost it is first game."
  answer: 1
  explanation: "The sentence needs the possessive pronoun 'its,' which has no apostrophe. Test 'it's' by expanding it: 'The team lost it is first game' makes no sense, so 'it's' is wrong. 'Its'' is never a correct form."

- question: "Choose the word that completes the sentence: 'The new rule will ___ every student in the school.'"
  type: multiple-choice
  options:
    - "effect"
    - "affect"
    - "effects"
    - "affection"
  answer: 1
  explanation: "The blank needs a verb meaning 'to influence,' so 'affect' is correct. 'Effect' is usually a noun ('The rule had a big effect'). One memory aid: Affect is the Action, Effect is the End result."

- question: "In the sentence 'She is taller then her brother,' the word 'then' is used correctly."
  type: true-false
  answer: false
  explanation: "Comparisons use 'than': 'She is taller than her brother.' 'Then' refers to time or sequence: 'We ate, and then we left.'"

- question: "Explain how to decide between 'your' and 'you're' in the sentence '___ going to love this book.'"
  type: short-answer
  answer: "Try expanding to 'you are.' 'You are going to love this book' makes sense, so the answer is the contraction 'you're.' 'Your' shows ownership, as in 'your book.'"
  explanation: "The expansion test works for every contraction pair: it's/its, they're/their, who's/whose, you're/your. If the expanded form fits, use the contraction; if not, use the possessive."
```

## Explainer

English has many words that sound the same but are spelled differently and mean different things. These are called **homophones**. Because they sound alike, writers mix them up when they write quickly. The good news is that a few tests sort out nearly all of them.

**Test 1: Expand the contraction.** Several confusing sets pair a contraction with a possessive word.

- it's (it is, it has) and its (belonging to it)
- you're (you are) and your (belonging to you)
- they're (they are) and their (belonging to them)
- who's (who is, who has) and whose (belonging to whom)

Read the sentence with the long form. "The cat licked it is paw" does not work, so the answer is "its." "It is raining" works, so "it's raining" is correct. Remember that personal possessive pronouns (its, yours, hers, ours, theirs) never use an apostrophe.

**Test 2: Know the third member.** "There" points to a place or starts a sentence ("There is a problem"). It contains the word "here," which can help you remember the place meaning. With "their" and "they're" handled by Test 1, "there" is the remaining choice.

**Test 3: Count and compare.** "Two" is the number 2. "Too" means "also" or "more than enough" ("too hot"). "To" goes with a destination or a verb ("go to school," "to swim"). "Than" makes comparisons, and "then" tells when.

**Test 4: Check the part of speech.** "Affect" is usually a verb: "Rain can affect the game." "Effect" is usually a noun: "The rain had an effect on the game." There are less common uses. "Effect" can be a verb meaning "to bring about," as in "effect change." "Affect" can be a noun in psychology. In everyday school writing, the verb-noun rule works.

Other common pairs include **accept** (to receive) and **except** (leaving out), **lose** (to misplace) and **loose** (not tight), and **principal** (head of a school, or main) and **principle** (a rule or belief).

Spell-checkers often miss these errors. "Their going too the park" contains only real words, so a basic checker may accept it. The best defense is a targeted editing pass. After drafting, search your writing for each tricky word and apply the test that fits. Over time the right choices become automatic.
