# Subject-Verb Agreement Rules

## Core Idea
The subject and verb of a sentence must agree in number: singular subjects take singular verbs, and plural subjects take plural verbs (e.g., 'The dog runs' not 'The dog run'). Mastering agreement prevents errors that mark writing as grammatically incorrect.

## How It's Best Learned
Identify the subject and verb in sentences, then check if they match in number. Practice correcting sentences with agreement errors.

## Common Misconceptions
- Collective nouns (team, family, group) are always plural; they can be singular or plural depending on whether you're treating them as one unit or as individuals.
- Words between the subject and verb change agreement; the subject and verb agree regardless of intervening words.

## Explainer

You already know the fundamental rule from your prerequisite work: the subject and predicate of a sentence must agree in number, and you can identify the subject and verb in basic sentences. This topic develops the skill further by working through the hard cases — the situations where the correct agreement is non-obvious because the grammatical structure is complex or counterintuitive.

The most important rule for navigating hard cases is to **find the true grammatical subject**. In practice, this means mentally stripping away everything that sits between the subject and verb. "The quality of his speeches improves with practice" — "speeches" is plural and sits right next to "improves," but the real subject is "quality" (singular). The prepositional phrase "of his speeches" is modifying "quality," not acting as the subject. Agreement tracks the head noun of the subject noun phrase, not the nearest noun. This is why diagramming sentences or asking "what is the verb's logical actor?" helps — it forces you to locate the true subject.

**Collective nouns** require a conceptual judgment. Words like "team," "committee," "audience," and "government" name groups. In American English, these are typically treated as singular when the group acts as a unified entity: "The jury has reached a verdict." But when the emphasis falls on individual members acting separately, plural agreement is acceptable: "The jury are arguing amongst themselves." The question is: one unified thing, or a collection of separate individuals? The same logic applies to organizational names — "The company is expanding" (singular, one entity) versus informal contexts where the members are separately in view.

**Indefinite pronouns** form two groups that learners often mix up. The always-singular group includes: each, every, either, neither, one, anyone, anyone, everyone, someone, nobody, and compounds ending in -body, -one, or -thing. "Everyone in both classes is required to attend" — even though "classes" is plural, the subject "everyone" is singular. The always-plural group includes few, many, both, others, and several. Some indefinite pronouns — some, any, none, all, most — are **variable**: they take their number from the noun phrase they refer to. "Some of the water is frozen" (water is uncountable, singular) but "Some of the students are absent" (students is plural).

**Compound subjects** follow a simple rule: subjects joined by and take a plural verb ("The cat and the dog are outside"), because two things are now one combined subject. But when compound subjects are joined by or or nor, the verb agrees with the **closer** subject: "Either the manager or the employees are responsible" — the verb agrees with "employees" (plural). "Neither the employees nor the manager is responsible" — the verb agrees with "manager" (singular). In all cases, agreement is a contract with the grammatical subject — not with the nearest word, not with what sounds natural, but with the true logical subject of the predication.
