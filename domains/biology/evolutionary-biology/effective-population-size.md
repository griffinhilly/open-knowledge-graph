# Effective Population Size

## Core Idea
Effective population size (Ne) is the size of an idealized population that would experience the same rate of genetic drift as the actual population. Ne is typically much smaller than census population size because not all individuals reproduce equally, and reproductive success varies stochastically. Ne is critical for predicting evolutionary rates, genetic diversity loss, and conservation outcomes.

## How It's Best Learned
Calculate Ne from census data with unequal sex ratios and variable reproduction. Compare Ne across populations with different reproductive systems (monogamous vs. polygynous).

## Common Misconceptions
- Effective population size equals census population size; Ne is usually much smaller due to variance in reproduction.
- Ne matters only for tiny populations; Ne affects all populations and is important for understanding neutral evolution.

## Explainer

You have already seen that genetic drift — the random sampling of alleles each generation — is strongest in small populations and can fix or eliminate alleles by chance alone. But what does "small" mean in practice? Real populations are never the idealized, equal-opportunity breeding groups that drift models assume. Effective population size (Ne) translates the messy reality of unequal reproduction, skewed sex ratios, and historical bottlenecks into a single number: the size of a hypothetical ideal population that would undergo genetic drift at the same rate as the actual one.

The key driver of reduced Ne is variance in reproductive success. In the idealized Wright-Fisher model, every individual has an equal and independent chance of contributing to the next generation. In real populations, many individuals fail to reproduce at all, while a few individuals reproduce prolifically. This skew concentrates the genetic ancestry of future generations into fewer lineages, amplifying stochastic loss of alleles just as if the population were much smaller. A polygynous species where one dominant male sires most offspring while others contribute nothing will have an Ne set largely by the effective number of breeding males — far below the total census count. The sex-ratio formula Ne = 4NmNf/(Nm + Nf) makes this quantitative: with 20 males and 80 females, Ne = 64 rather than 100.

Population history also shapes Ne. A bottleneck — a brief, severe reduction in breeding numbers — leaves a lasting genomic signature. During the bottleneck, drift is so intense that rare alleles are purged and heterozygosity collapses. Even after the population grows back to thousands or millions, the genetic diversity lost in that one narrow passage is not quickly restored by growth alone. Ne calculated across multiple generations still reflects the bottleneck, which is why conservation geneticists find that recently recovered species often retain very low genetic variation. Northern elephant seals, for example, were hunted to near-extinction in the 1890s; today their population exceeds 100,000 but their genetic diversity remains near zero — a direct consequence of that bottleneck.

Why does all this matter beyond academic interest? Genetic diversity is the raw material for adaptation. Populations lose heterozygosity at a rate of approximately 1/(2Ne) per generation. For a species with Ne = 50, that is 1% of remaining diversity lost every generation — a pace that, over centuries, severely limits evolutionary response to disease, climate change, or novel environments. This is why conservation programs now set minimum viable population sizes not by census numbers alone, but by estimated Ne, and why managed breeding programs actively track genealogies to maintain diversity.
