# Narrative Analysis: Meaning in Life Stories

## Core Idea
Narrative analysis examines how individuals construct coherent stories about experience, identity, and social worlds. Stories have structure: beginnings, middles, endings; protagonists and turning points. Analysis focuses on how narratives make experience meaningful and reflect social structures.

## Explainer

Human beings do not simply experience events — they story them. When asked "how did you end up in this job?" or "what happened in your marriage?" people do not recite a chronological fact list. They select, arrange, and frame events into a coherent arc with a protagonist (themselves), motives, turning points, and a point. **Narrative analysis** takes this story-making activity seriously as a subject of social scientific inquiry. The central claim is that how people narrate their lives is not just a reflection of what happened — it is itself a form of social action and meaning-construction.

If you've worked with grounded theory, you're familiar with analyzing qualitative data through systematic comparison and category development — a process that moves from particulars toward abstract conceptual patterns. Narrative analysis works differently: it holds the story together as a unit rather than fracturing it into codes. The story's *structure* is analytical data. **Emplotment** — Aristotle's term for how events are configured into a plot — is the mechanism by which raw experience becomes intelligible. Ask someone about a difficult period in their life and notice how they impose order: there is a before, a crisis, and a resolution or an ongoing struggle. These narrative forms are not just personal; they draw on culturally available story templates — the redemption arc, the tragedy, the bildungsroman of growth and learning.

**Biographical meaning** is built at the intersection of personal experience and social structure. When someone tells you why they became a nurse, or how they left their hometown, or why their marriage ended, they are not just reporting facts — they are positioning themselves within moral and cultural frameworks: "I always cared about helping people" or "I had to escape the limitations I grew up with." Analysts look for the **identity work** in these accounts: how does the story construct a coherent self? What is emphasized, minimized, or conspicuously absent? Whose language does the narrator borrow? Silences and contradictions are as analytically significant as confident accounts.

Narrative analysis also reads upward from individual stories to social structures. The stories people can and cannot tell are shaped by available cultural scripts, power relations, and what counts as a legitimate life narrative in a given time and place. Women narrating their careers in the 1950s had different narrative resources than women narrating them today. Immigrants constructing accounts of their migration are drawing on (and sometimes resisting) dominant cultural framings of mobility, belonging, and loss. The analyst's task is not just to understand what the person experienced but to interpret how their story is socially located — what it reveals about the broader social world in which it was produced.
