# Bias, Positionality, and Reflexivity: Researcher Influence

## Core Idea
Researchers are not neutral observers. Social positions, identities, and assumptions influence research questions, data collection, and interpretation. Reflexivity—systematic reflection on these influences—is a methodological resource. Acknowledging positionality enhances credibility and validity.

## Questions

- question: "A researcher studying urban poverty chooses to study only inner-city neighborhoods, frames interview questions around individual financial decisions, and consistently interprets ambiguous responses as confirming her hypothesis about irresponsible spending.   explanation: "Positionality-derived bias is systematic and directional, not random. This is different from random error (which averages out) and is not fully captured by the concept of confirmation bias (which frames bias as a cognitive quirk rather than a product of social location). The researcher's class and cultural position are shaping what she sees and how she interprets it at every stage."

    - "To demonstrate that the researcher has identified her biases and therefore eliminated them from the research process"

Crucially, disclosure does not eliminate bias — it makes the bias assessable and the interpretive authority legible."

- question: "The goal of reflexivity in qualitative research is to eliminate researcher bias, allowing findings to approach the objectivity of natural science."
  type: true-false

Pretending to a neutrality that is not achievable is itself a methodological failure — it hides the conditions of knowledge production rather than examining them."

An insider researcher may recognize the significance of behaviors that an outsider would overlook. The limitation of insider positionality is the reverse: normalization of patterns that warrant scrutiny, difficulty maintaining analytic distance, and the risk that participants perform conformity rather than authenticity. Both advantages and limitations should be addressed in reflexive practice."

Reflexivity accomplishes this through positionality memos, documentation of access and rapport dynamics, and explicit examination of interpretive choices, giving readers the information needed to evaluate the situatedness of the findings."
  explanation: "The philosophical point is that all knowledge is produced from somewhere — from a perspective shaped by social location, disciplinary training, and prior experience. Claiming to have eliminated this is not a methodological achievement; it is an unexamined assumption that hides the conditions of knowledge production. Reflexivity moves from pretending to transcend position (which is impossible) to working transparently from within it (which is achievable and more honest). ## Explainer

Your research design training taught you to translate questions into methods — but it assumed a relatively neutral starting point. The concept of **positionality** challenges that assumption directly. Every researcher occupies a social location defined by factors like race, class, gender, professional training, and cultural background. These locations are not mere biographical details; they shape what questions seem worth asking, which populations feel accessible, and what counts as a satisfying explanation. A researcher studying urban poverty from an affluent background will notice different things, gain access to different conversations, and be trusted differently by participants than one who grew up in poverty. Neither perspective is automatically more valid, but both are positioned.

**Bias** in this context means something more systematic than random error. It refers to the directional pull that positionality exerts on research — the tendency to notice confirming evidence, to phrase questions that elicit particular answers, or to code ambiguous data in ways consistent with existing expectations. Bias can enter at every stage: in the choice of research question, the operationalization of concepts, the selection of interview settings, the interpretation of silences, and the writing of findings. Critically, bias is not fully eliminable — the goal is not a view from nowhere, but a clearly articulated view from somewhere.

**Reflexivity** is the methodological response to this condition. It means systematically examining your own assumptions, emotional reactions, and interpretive moves as a researcher, and treating those examinations as data-worthy in themselves. A reflexive researcher keeps a positionality memo that tracks how their identity shaped access and rapport; they document moments when informants reacted to who they are rather than what they asked; they flag interpretations that feel uncomfortably convenient. This is not navel-gazing — it is an audit trail that allows readers to assess the situatedness of the findings.

The practical implication is that positionality statements are not confessions of contamination — they are claims about interpretive authority and limitation. A researcher who is an outsider to the community being studied brings the possibility of fresh comparison but risks misreading insider meaning; an insider brings tacit knowledge but may normalize patterns that warrant scrutiny. Acknowledging this openly is not a weakness in the research design — it is evidence that the researcher has done the harder work of honest situational analysis rather than pretending to a neutrality that was never available.
