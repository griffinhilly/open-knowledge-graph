# Philosophy of Social Science Research

## Core Idea
Examines foundational questions about the nature of social reality, the observer-observed relationship, and how social knowledge differs from natural science. Explores debates between positivism, interpretivism, critical realism, and constructivism, establishing philosophical foundations for methodological choices.

## How It's Best Learned
Engage with classic debates (Durkheim vs. Weber), read contemporary philosophy of science texts, discuss how philosophical positions shape research design decisions in real projects.

## Common Misconceptions
- Assuming positivism is universally rejected in social science
- Conflating interpretivism with lack of rigor
- Treating methodology as separate from philosophical worldview

## Explainer

Every research design rests on philosophical commitments, whether the researcher articulates them or not. Philosophy of social science makes those commitments explicit and asks whether they are defensible. There are three foundational questions: What is social reality? (ontology) How can we know it? (epistemology) And what methods follow from those answers? (methodology). The major positions in this debate — positivism, interpretivism, critical realism, and constructivism — are not competing preferences but genuinely different answers to these questions, each with distinctive implications for how research should be designed and what counts as a good explanation.

**Positivism** holds that social phenomena can be studied using the same logic as natural science: observable facts, replicable measurements, causal laws. Durkheim's study of suicide rates exemplifies the positivist commitment — aggregate patterns reveal social facts that exist independently of individual consciousness. Positivism privileges quantitative methods because they produce the kind of law-like generalizations that qualify as scientific explanations. Critics object that social phenomena are constituted by meaning — a suicide is different from a death from other causes not because of its physical features but because of the intentions and interpretations attached to it — and that statistical regularities describe correlations without explaining the underlying processes.

**Interpretivism** (associated with Weber, Geertz, and contemporary ethnographers) holds that social reality is irreducibly meaningful: human action is action under a description, and understanding it requires grasping the meanings, intentions, and cultural frameworks that make actions intelligible. Weber's Verstehen — interpretive understanding — is not just empathy; it is a method of reading the subjective meanings that motivate action and situating them within broader cultural and historical contexts. The methodological implication is that good social science requires thick description, attention to context, and proximity to subjects — the goals of qualitative research. The risk, from a positivist perspective, is that interpretive findings are idiosyncratic and non-generalizable.

**Critical realism** attempts a synthesis. It agrees with positivism that a real social world exists independently of our descriptions of it, but insists with interpretivists that social structures are not observable in the way physical objects are — they are real mechanisms that generate observable outcomes under certain conditions. The goal is to identify **generative mechanisms**: the causal structures (class relations, institutional rules, psychological tendencies) that produce the regularities we observe. This shifts the model of explanation from "X correlates with Y" to "under what conditions does mechanism M produce outcome Y?" Critical realism supports mixed methods: quantitative analysis identifies patterns, qualitative work traces mechanisms.

**Constructivism** occupies the most radical position: social reality is not discovered but produced through language, interaction, and institutional practice. Berger and Luckmann's "social construction of reality" argues that what counts as a fact, a category, or a valid explanation is itself a social achievement. This does not mean social phenomena are invented arbitrarily, but that the categories we use to study them (race, mental illness, crime) are historically contingent and politically loaded. The methodological implication is reflexivity — researchers must examine how their own categories and practices shape what they find. The practical risk is paralysis: if all knowledge is constructed, on what grounds do we prefer one account to another? The best constructivist research answers this by specifying the social processes through which particular constructions gain authority.
