# Philosophy of Social Science Research

## Core Idea
Examines foundational questions about the nature of social reality, the observer-observed relationship, and how social knowledge differs from natural science. Explores debates between positivism, interpretivism, critical realism, and constructivism, establishing philosophical foundations for methodological choices.

## How It's Best Learned
Engage with classic debates (Durkheim vs. Weber), read contemporary philosophy of science texts, discuss how philosophical positions shape research design decisions in real projects.

## Common Misconceptions
- Assuming positivism is universally rejected in social science
- Conflating interpretivism with lack of rigor
- Treating methodology as separate from philosophical worldview

## Questions

```yaml
- question: "A sociologist studies poverty rates by analyzing census data, identifying correlational patterns between income and educational attainment across hundreds of cities. This researcher is most likely operating from which philosophical framework?"
  type: multiple-choice
  options:
    - "Interpretivist — it seeks to understand the lived meanings that people attach to poverty"
    - "Constructivist — poverty is a socially constructed category that shapes what the data can reveal"
    - "Positivist — it uses observable, measurable data to identify law-like patterns across cases"
    - "Critical realist — it traces the generative mechanisms that produce income-education correlations"
  answer: 2
  explanation: "The approach is positivist: observable aggregate data, quantitative measurement, replicable methods, correlational law-like generalizations. This matches Durkheim's model — studying social facts through patterns in data that exist independently of individual consciousness. A critical realist (D) would accept the data but push further to ask 'what causal mechanism produces this correlation?' and would likely combine quantitative pattern-finding with qualitative tracing of mechanisms. The positivist is satisfied with the correlation as explanation; the critical realist treats it as a puzzle to explain."

- question: "A constructivist researcher critiques a study that uses 'mental illness' as a variable in analyzing hospital admissions over several decades. The constructivist's most likely objection is..."
  type: multiple-choice
  options:
    - "The data is qualitative rather than quantitative, making it unsuitable for analysis"
    - "The researcher has not verified that mental illness has a biological basis"
    - "The category 'mental illness' is historically contingent and politically loaded — using it uncritically risks reproducing the power relations embedded in its construction"
    - "The researcher should have used Verstehen rather than statistical analysis"
  answer: 2
  explanation: "Constructivism holds that the categories used in social research — race, mental illness, crime, poverty — are not neutral descriptions of a pre-given reality but are historically produced through language, institutional practice, and power relations. What counted as 'mental illness' in 1950 was determined differently than in 2020, shaped by medical authority, insurance coding, and social norms. Using the category across decades without examining this history treats a social construction as a stable natural fact. Options A, B, and D represent different frameworks' concerns — constructivism's distinctive contribution is this focus on category production and reflexivity."

- question: "Interpretivism sacrifices scientific rigor for subjective storytelling, making its findings less reliable than those produced by quantitative research methods."
  type: true-false
  answer: false
  explanation: "This is a listed misconception. Interpretivism has its own rigorous standards: thick description, triangulation across methods and sources, negative case analysis, member-checking with participants, and transparent documentation of analytical procedures. The difference is not rigor versus lack of rigor but different ontological and epistemological commitments about what social reality is and what counts as a good explanation. Interpretivists argue that quantitative methods achieve 'reliability' by stripping out the very meaning structures that constitute social action — a methodologically rigorous way of missing the point."

- question: "A researcher's philosophical commitments about the nature of social reality (ontology) have real implications for how they design their study and what counts as valid evidence."
  type: true-false
  answer: true
  explanation: "This is the core insight of philosophy of social science: the chain runs from ontology (what is social reality?) to epistemology (how can we know it?) to methodology (what methods follow?). A positivist ontology — social facts exist independently of observers and can be measured — leads naturally to quantitative survey methods. An interpretivist ontology — social reality is constituted by meaning — leads to ethnography, interviews, and discourse analysis. The same phenomenon (say, 'crime') studied from different philosophical starting points will be operationalized differently, measured differently, and explained differently. Treating methodology as separable from philosophical worldview is itself a philosophical position (implicitly positivist)."

- question: "Why can't a researcher simply choose research methods based on practical convenience or 'what works,' independent of philosophical commitments about the nature of social reality?"
  type: short-answer
  answer: "Every method embeds assumptions about what social reality is and how it can be known. A survey questionnaire assumes that attitudes and experiences can be standardized, that respondents' self-reports accurately represent their states, and that patterns across respondents constitute meaningful social facts. An ethnographic study assumes that meaningful understanding requires sustained immersion in context, that actions must be interpreted within cultural frameworks, and that numbers strip away the very meaning structures being studied. Choosing methods 'pragmatically' doesn't escape these commitments — it just makes them implicit and unexamined. When commitments are implicit, they cannot be scrutinized or defended, which is a methodological weakness. Philosophy of social science forces these assumptions into the open so researchers can evaluate whether their methods are appropriate to what they are actually trying to explain."
  explanation: "The deeper point is that 'what works' already presupposes a criterion for success — and that criterion is itself a philosophical commitment. Does a study 'work' if it produces law-like generalizations? Interpretive understanding? Identification of causal mechanisms? Reflexive deconstruction of categories? Different philosophical frameworks give different answers, and the researcher who thinks they're being atheoretical is actually smuggling in a framework unexamined."
```

## Explainer

Every research design rests on philosophical commitments, whether the researcher articulates them or not. Philosophy of social science makes those commitments explicit and asks whether they are defensible. There are three foundational questions: What is social reality? (ontology) How can we know it? (epistemology) And what methods follow from those answers? (methodology). The major positions in this debate — positivism, interpretivism, critical realism, and constructivism — are not competing preferences but genuinely different answers to these questions, each with distinctive implications for how research should be designed and what counts as a good explanation.

**Positivism** holds that social phenomena can be studied using the same logic as natural science: observable facts, replicable measurements, causal laws. Durkheim's study of suicide rates exemplifies the positivist commitment — aggregate patterns reveal social facts that exist independently of individual consciousness. Positivism privileges quantitative methods because they produce the kind of law-like generalizations that qualify as scientific explanations. Critics object that social phenomena are constituted by meaning — a suicide is different from a death from other causes not because of its physical features but because of the intentions and interpretations attached to it — and that statistical regularities describe correlations without explaining the underlying processes.

**Interpretivism** (associated with Weber, Geertz, and contemporary ethnographers) holds that social reality is irreducibly meaningful: human action is action under a description, and understanding it requires grasping the meanings, intentions, and cultural frameworks that make actions intelligible. Weber's Verstehen — interpretive understanding — is not just empathy; it is a method of reading the subjective meanings that motivate action and situating them within broader cultural and historical contexts. The methodological implication is that good social science requires thick description, attention to context, and proximity to subjects — the goals of qualitative research. The risk, from a positivist perspective, is that interpretive findings are idiosyncratic and non-generalizable.

**Critical realism** attempts a synthesis. It agrees with positivism that a real social world exists independently of our descriptions of it, but insists with interpretivists that social structures are not observable in the way physical objects are — they are real mechanisms that generate observable outcomes under certain conditions. The goal is to identify **generative mechanisms**: the causal structures (class relations, institutional rules, psychological tendencies) that produce the regularities we observe. This shifts the model of explanation from "X correlates with Y" to "under what conditions does mechanism M produce outcome Y?" Critical realism supports mixed methods: quantitative analysis identifies patterns, qualitative work traces mechanisms.

**Constructivism** occupies the most radical position: social reality is not discovered but produced through language, interaction, and institutional practice. Berger and Luckmann's "social construction of reality" argues that what counts as a fact, a category, or a valid explanation is itself a social achievement. This does not mean social phenomena are invented arbitrarily, but that the categories we use to study them (race, mental illness, crime) are historically contingent and politically loaded. The methodological implication is reflexivity — researchers must examine how their own categories and practices shape what they find. The practical risk is paralysis: if all knowledge is constructed, on what grounds do we prefer one account to another? The best constructivist research answers this by specifying the social processes through which particular constructions gain authority.
