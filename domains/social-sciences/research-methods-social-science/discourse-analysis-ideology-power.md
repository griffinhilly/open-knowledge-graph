# Discourse Analysis: Language, Power, and Ideology

## Core Idea
Discourse analysis examines how language, rhetoric, and communication patterns construct meaning, reproduce power, and enable or constrain action. Analysts investigate how discourses define what is 'normal,' possible, or speakable, serving ideological functions.

## Explainer

Your prerequisite — critical discourse analysis — introduced the core move: language is not a neutral medium for expressing pre-formed ideas but a site where power operates. This topic deepens that foundation by asking how language functions *ideologically* — how it naturalizes social arrangements and makes particular distributions of power seem inevitable or commonsensical. The central insight is that **discourse** is not just text or talk but a system of meanings that produces the objects it appears merely to describe. A medical discourse does not just name diseases; it constructs what counts as pathological, who has authority to define it, and which bodies are subjected to clinical scrutiny.

The concept of **ideology** in discourse analysis differs from its everyday usage (as "false belief" or "propaganda"). Here, ideology refers to the way systems of meaning organize social reality in the interests of dominant groups — not necessarily through lies, but through selective framing, naturalization, and the organization of what can be said. When unemployment is consistently described as a personal failure rather than a structural condition, that framing does ideological work: it distributes blame, deflects attention from institutions, and reproduces beliefs that sustain existing arrangements. The ideology is embedded in the discourse itself, not in a separate realm of false consciousness.

**Power** in this framework operates through what Michel Foucault called **regimes of truth** — the rules that determine what can count as knowledge, who has authority to speak, and what is excluded from legitimate discourse. A discourse defines not just what is said but what is unspeakable: topics that cannot be raised without appearing unintelligible, extreme, or illegitimate. Analysts trace these silences as carefully as they trace explicit claims, because what is systematically absent from discourse often reveals its ideological function most clearly.

Practically, discourse analysis at this level involves examining patterns across texts — recurring metaphors, presuppositions, nominalization (turning processes into nouns to obscure agency), and the way social actors are represented or absent. For example, passive voice constructions like "mistakes were made" suppress agency in ways that active constructions do not. Repetition across institutional contexts — in news, policy documents, and official speech — signals not individual choice but discursive convention. By mapping these patterns, analysts expose how power works not through coercion alone but through the manufacture of common sense.
