# Mixed Methods: Sequential Designs and Expansion

## Core Idea
Sequential mixed methods begin with one approach (often qualitative) to explore and develop understanding, then expand with another (often quantitative) to test and generalize findings. This design leverages exploratory depth and confirmatory breadth.

## Explainer

From your prerequisite work on mixed methods integration, you know that the central challenge is not just conducting two types of research but creating genuine dialogue between them. Sequential designs address this by making the output of one phase the input of the next — a tighter coupling than simply running parallel qualitative and quantitative strands and comparing results afterward.

The **exploratory sequential design** moves from qualitative to quantitative. You begin with open-ended fieldwork, interviews, or document analysis to understand how participants make sense of a phenomenon, what categories they use, what variations matter to them. This inductive phase generates hypotheses, conceptual frameworks, or instrument items grounded in participant experience rather than researcher assumptions. The quantitative phase then tests whether these inductively derived concepts hold more broadly across a larger, more representative sample. A classic application: a researcher interviews healthcare workers about barriers to using a new protocol, uses those interviews to develop a survey instrument in participants' own language, and then deploys that survey nationally. The qualitative phase prevents the common failure of surveys that measure the wrong things because they were designed without understanding the domain.

The **explanatory sequential design** reverses the sequence: quantitative first, qualitative second. You run a large survey or administrative data analysis and identify a surprising or puzzling finding — say, that an educational intervention worked significantly better in rural schools than urban ones. The qualitative phase then investigates the mechanisms behind this pattern through case studies or interviews. Here the quantitative phase defines what requires explanation, and the qualitative phase provides the explanatory resources — the "why" behind the "what." The sequential logic forces you to be disciplined about the relationship: you must specify, before beginning the qualitative work, exactly which quantitative findings you are trying to explain and what you will look for.

The key skill in sequential design is managing **phase transitions** — the moments where you hand off across methodological traditions. Transition decisions include: which quantitative findings to "follow up" (you cannot investigate everything), how to select cases for qualitative work (purposive sampling to maximize variation, to probe outliers, or to study extreme cases), and how to translate what you found qualitatively back into the broader quantitative picture. Poor transitions produce designs where the two phases are technically sequential but intellectually disconnected. Strong transitions produce what Creswell calls **expansion** — where the second phase genuinely extends and deepens what the first phase established, creating knowledge neither approach could generate alone.
