# Grounded Theory: Constant Comparison and Coding

## Core Idea
Grounded theory develops theories from qualitative data through iterative coding and comparison. Constant comparison—identifying similarities and differences across instances—builds increasingly abstract conceptualizations. Theory emerges from data rather than being imposed beforehand.

## Explainer

If you have worked through grounded theory methods as a framework, the concept of **constant comparison** answers the practical question: how exactly does theory emerge from data? The answer is through a disciplined process of repeatedly asking "how does this piece of data compare to everything else I've seen?" Every time you encounter a new interview excerpt, observation note, or document, you compare it — explicitly and systematically — to prior instances. Similarities reveal patterns; differences force you to refine your categories or generate new ones.

The process works in layers. **Open coding** is the first pass: you break data into fragments and attach labels that capture what is happening. You might label a passage from an interview as "expressing distrust of authority," and then label a similar passage from a different interview the same way. When you have enough instances labeled the same way, you have identified a preliminary category. But constant comparison immediately presses further: are all these instances really the same thing? Are there subtypes? Does the distrust show up differently in different contexts? This questioning prevents premature closure — you do not lock in categories too early.

As you accumulate categories, **axial coding** examines the relationships among them. How does one category influence another? What conditions trigger it? What are its consequences? Here the comparison extends beyond individual data points to the connections between abstractions. You are no longer just comparing "this interview excerpt" to "that interview excerpt," but asking how the category of "distrust of authority" relates to the category of "withdrawal from civic participation." This relational work is what begins to look like a theory.

**Theoretical sampling** is the discipline that drives the data collection process: you collect new data specifically to test and refine the emerging categories, not according to a predetermined sampling plan. If your theory is developing around a distinction between active and passive distrust, you deliberately seek out cases that will clarify that distinction — going back to old informants, seeking new ones, revisiting documents. Data collection ends when you reach **theoretical saturation**: new data no longer produces new categories or refines existing ones. The theory is as grounded as it can be.

The power of constant comparison is that it produces theories that are genuinely accountable to data, not just illustrated by convenient examples. The risk is that without discipline, the comparison becomes implicit and the researcher's assumptions quietly determine what gets compared to what. Strong grounded theory practice requires making comparisons explicit, keeping detailed analytic memos documenting why categories were split or merged, and remaining open to the data disconfirming the emerging theory — which is exactly what distinguishes it from ordinary qualitative description.
