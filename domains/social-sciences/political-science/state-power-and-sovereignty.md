# State Power and Sovereignty

## Core Idea
A state is a political unit with a defined territory and population that exercises legitimate authority over its members. Sovereignty refers to the state's supreme power to make binding decisions within its territory without external interference. Understanding the state and sovereignty is fundamental to all political analysis, as it defines the basic unit of political organization in the international system.

## Explainer

The **state** is the foundational concept in political science, but it is easy to confuse it with related ideas. A state is not the same as a nation (which is a cultural or ethnic community) or a government (which is the particular set of people currently in power). The state is the permanent institutional structure — territory, population, administrative apparatus, and coercive capacity — that persists across changes in government. France as a state outlasted the monarchy, the republic, Napoleon, and Vichy. The government changed; the state endured.

**Sovereignty** is the attribute that distinguishes a state from other political units like cities, provinces, or corporations. A sovereign state claims final authority within its borders — no higher earthly power can override its laws without its consent. The classic formulation comes from the Peace of Westphalia (1648), which established a system of mutually recognizing sovereign states as the organizing principle of international order. In practice, sovereignty has two faces: internal sovereignty (supreme authority over citizens and territory) and external sovereignty (recognition and non-interference by other states). Both are necessary. A government that cannot project authority inside its own borders lacks internal sovereignty; a government unrecognized by other states lacks external sovereignty.

Max Weber's definition sharpens the picture: a state is an organization that successfully claims a **monopoly on legitimate violence** within a given territory. The word "legitimate" is doing real work here. Many actors can use force — gangs, militias, corporations — but only the state claims the exclusive *right* to do so. When that claim is widely accepted, the state is stable. When it is contested — rival armed groups, warlords, secessionist movements — the state is said to be weak or failed. This is why state-building in conflict zones is so difficult: it is not just a matter of building institutions, but of constructing the social belief that only one authority has the right to use force.

Sovereignty also has an internal tension: a state powerful enough to provide order is powerful enough to become tyrannical. This is why political theorists from Hobbes onward have asked how sovereign power can be constrained without being destroyed. Constitutions, separation of powers, federalism, and international human rights law are all partial answers to this problem. But they all presuppose a prior answer to the question of who holds sovereign authority in the first place — which is why sovereignty remains the starting point for all other political analysis.

The concept of sovereignty is under strain in the contemporary world. Globalization, international institutions (the UN, WTO, ICC), and non-state actors (multinational corporations, transnational terrorist networks) all challenge the simple model of fully autonomous sovereign states. Scholars debate whether sovereignty has been eroded or merely transformed. What is clear is that you cannot understand these debates — about international law, humanitarian intervention, global governance, or national self-determination — without a firm grasp of what sovereignty means and why states claimed it in the first place.
