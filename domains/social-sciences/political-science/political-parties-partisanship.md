# Political Parties and Party Systems

## Core Idea
Political parties are organizations that aggregate citizen preferences, recruit candidates, structure competition, and typically aim to win elections and govern. Parties reduce information costs for voters, organize legislatures, and create stable policy platforms. Party systems vary from dominant-party systems to two-party systems to multiparty systems, with different consequences for representation and stability.

## Explainer

You've already studied electoral systems — the rules by which votes are translated into seats. Political parties are the organizations that make electoral systems work in practice. Without parties, elections would be a chaotic aggregation of individual candidates making individual promises with no reliable way for voters to predict what any of them would actually do in office. Parties solve several coordination problems in democratic politics simultaneously.

The first problem parties solve is **information and coordination**. Individual voters cannot realistically evaluate every candidate for every office in every election cycle. Parties provide labels — shorthand summaries of a bundle of policy positions, values, and track records. When a voter sees "Democrat" or "Conservative" or "Social Democrat" on a ballot, they can infer a rough policy package from a single word. This dramatically reduces the cognitive burden of democratic participation. For candidates, parties solve the reverse coordination problem: running as a party member means access to organizational resources, donor networks, and voter mobilization infrastructure that would take years to build independently. Parties thus serve as matchmakers between aspiring politicians and voters, and as bundlers of citizen preferences that might otherwise never find coherent political expression.

The second function is **structuring legislative competition**. In any legislature, hundreds of representatives face thousands of decisions. Without parties, legislatures would be chaotic — shifting coalitions, unpredictable outcomes, extreme difficulty in holding anyone accountable for results. Parties create stable coalitions: members generally vote together on key issues, making legislative outcomes more predictable and governance more coherent. The tradeoff is party discipline — members who deviate face sanctions, which sometimes means voting against constituency preferences to maintain coalition cohesion. This tension between party loyalty and constituency responsiveness is a permanent feature of representative government.

The structure of the **party system** — how many parties exist and how they compete — has major consequences for governance. **Two-party systems**, as in the United States, tend to produce centrist competition as parties fight for median voters, but they also exclude many policy preferences from representation and can calcify into stable duopolies resistant to new political forces. **Multiparty systems**, common in proportional representation countries, better represent diverse preferences but often produce coalition governments that may be unstable or produce policy paralysis. **Dominant-party systems**, where one party wins repeatedly, may deliver stable governance but erode accountability — without credible electoral competition, the governing party loses the incentive to remain responsive to citizens. At the individual level, **partisanship** — strong psychological identification with a party — provides political orientation and simplifies decision-making, but can also produce polarization, where party identity becomes more salient than the actual policy questions parties are ostensibly fighting about.
