# The State: Concept, Definition, and Sovereignty

## Core Idea
A state is a political organization with defined territory, permanent population, effective government, and capacity to enter relations with other states. Sovereignty refers to supreme authority within a state's territory and independence in the international system.

## How It's Best Learned
Examine the formation of new states (South Sudan, Kosovo, Palestinian territories) and understand what makes them states or near-states. Compare how states claim and exercise sovereignty. Investigate supranational organizations that limit sovereignty.

## Common Misconceptions
A state is not the same as a nation; nations are cultural/ethnic communities, states are political organizations. Sovereignty is not absolute; it can be limited by treaties, international law, and practical constraints. Recognition as a state is partially a matter of international acceptance.

## Explainer

The concept of the state emerges from the problem your prerequisite concept — power and political order — sets up. Power must be organized somewhere, held by someone, and enforced over a territory. The **state** is the answer to where that organized power lives. Defined most influentially by Max Weber, the state claims a **monopoly on the legitimate use of physical force** within a given territory. This distinguishes states from other power-holders: gangs, corporations, and warlords may exercise force, but only the state claims it as a right recognized by those over whom it is exercised.

Four criteria define statehood, codified in the 1933 Montevideo Convention: a **permanent population**, a **defined territory**, an **effective government** capable of controlling that territory, and the **capacity to enter into relations with other states**. These criteria explain why contested cases are analytically interesting. Kosovo has a population, territory, and government — but lacks universal diplomatic recognition, especially from Russia and China. Palestine exercises governance over parts of the West Bank and Gaza but lacks full territorial control. Effectiveness matters: a state that cannot govern its own territory (a "failed state") raises questions about whether sovereignty is real or merely nominal.

**Sovereignty** operates at two levels. **Internal sovereignty** is supreme authority within the state's borders — the claim that no other power overrides the state's law within its territory. **External sovereignty** is independence in the international system — the right not to be interfered with by other states. These two levels can come apart. A state may have full internal sovereignty (complete domestic control) but reduced external sovereignty if it has signed treaties that limit its freedom of action, joined supranational institutions like the EU, or been placed under international sanctions. Sovereignty is thus not binary — it is a bundle of claims that can be partially transferred, pooled, or constrained.

The most important conceptual distinction for beginners is **state versus nation**. A **nation** is a cultural or ethnic community — a group that shares identity, often language, history, and a sense of common belonging. A **state** is a political-legal institution that governs territory. The two often overlap but are analytically distinct. France is both a state (a sovereign political entity) and a nation (a French cultural community). But the Kurdish people constitute a nation without a state; and Switzerland is a state containing multiple national communities. **Nationalism** is the ideology that holds that nations deserve their own states. Much of modern political conflict — from self-determination movements to secession crises to border disputes — follows from the tension between these two concepts and the aspiration to align them.
