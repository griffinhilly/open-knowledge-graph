# Electoral Systems and Voting Mechanisms

## Core Idea
Electoral systems translate votes into seats or power using different rules—plurality, proportional representation, mixed systems—each with distinct consequences for party system structure, representation, and governance. The choice of electoral system shapes which candidates win, how fragmented parliament becomes, and what coalitions must form to govern.

## Explainer

You already know from democracy fundamentals that democratic legitimacy rests on the idea that government should reflect popular will, and from political representation concepts that this translation involves real tensions — between delegates and trustees, between individual and geographic representation. Electoral systems are the specific mechanical rules that translate citizen preferences (votes) into political power (seats, offices). Different rules produce systematically different outcomes, and understanding this is essential for comparative politics.

The most fundamental distinction is between **plurality/majoritarian** systems and **proportional representation (PR)** systems. In a **plurality** system (like the U.S. House or British Parliament), candidates compete in single-member geographic districts, and the candidate with the most votes wins — even if that is only 35% of a crowded field. This creates strong incentives for strategic voting: supporting your second-choice candidate if your first-choice is unlikely to win. French political scientist Maurice Duverger observed that plurality rules consistently produce **two-party systems** — a pattern now called **Duverger's Law** — because minor parties fail to convert votes into seats and voters rationally abandon them. The result is clear accountability (voters know which party governed) but potentially severe distortions between vote share and seat share.

**Proportional representation** allocates seats in proportion to vote shares across a larger constituency, often national or regional. A party winning 15% of votes gets roughly 15% of seats. This produces **multiparty systems** because small parties can survive — there is no "wasted vote" problem. The benefits are more accurate translation of public opinion into representation, greater inclusion of minority viewpoints, and typically higher voter turnout. The costs are coalition governments requiring negotiation among multiple parties, which may be unstable or slow to make decisions. Voters in PR systems sometimes lose the clear link between their vote and a specific geographic representative.

**Mixed systems** — used in Germany, New Zealand, and Japan — try to capture both virtues: some seats are elected from geographic districts (accountability, local representation) while others are allocated proportionally (accurate overall seat distribution). Germany's mixed-member proportional system compensates parties underrepresented in district races with extra list seats, producing a parliament that closely mirrors overall vote shares while maintaining local representation. Understanding electoral system mechanics matters practically: debates over electoral reform in any country are ultimately debates about these tradeoffs — who gets represented, how governments form, and whether political competition stays within two parties or expands to include multiple viewpoints.
