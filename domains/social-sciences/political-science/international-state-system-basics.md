# The International State System and Anarchic Order

## Core Idea
The international state system is an anarchic order of sovereign states with no overarching authority, requiring states to ensure their own security and interests through diplomacy, alliances, and military power. The Westphalian system created the principle of sovereign equality, though this ideal is modified by power asymmetries and international institutions.

## Explainer

From state power and sovereignty, you know what a state is: a political unit claiming supreme authority over a defined territory and population, backed by a monopoly on legitimate force. The **international state system** is what exists when many such units coexist without a higher authority above them. This structure has a formal name in international relations theory: **anarchy** — not chaos, but the absence of a world government. Understanding this single structural fact generates most of the core logic of international politics.

The **Westphalian system** is conventionally dated to the 1648 Peace of Westphalia, which ended the Thirty Years' War in Europe. Its lasting contribution was the legal principle of **sovereign equality**: each state is the supreme authority within its own territory, and no external authority — not the Pope, not the Holy Roman Emperor, not a stronger neighboring state — has the right to intervene in its internal affairs. This was a radical proposition in 1648, and it remains legally foundational today. The United Nations Charter codifies Westphalian sovereignty in Articles 2(1) and 2(7). The critical insight is that sovereign equality is a *norm*, not a description of power: Switzerland and the United States are formally equal sovereigns despite enormous differences in military and economic capacity. The norm doesn't eliminate power disparities, but it shapes the terms on which they operate — even powerful states must generally justify interventions in terms that acknowledge other states' formal sovereignty.

Because there is no world government to enforce contracts, adjudicate disputes, or punish aggressors, security in the international system is fundamentally **self-help**: states cannot rely on a higher authority to protect them, so they must provide for their own security through military capability, alliances, and diplomacy. This structural condition generates what realist theorists call the **security dilemma**: when one state arms to increase its own security, neighboring states perceive an increase in threat and arm in response — leaving everyone less secure than before, despite each acting rationally from its own perspective. The arms races preceding World War I, and the mutual build-up of nuclear arsenals during the Cold War, are paradigm cases. **Balance of power** is the classical response to this dilemma: states form coalitions against threatening powers to prevent any single state from achieving hegemony, not because they love cooperation but because hegemony would leave all others at that state's mercy.

From your nationalism prerequisite, you know that states are not merely strategic calculators — they are also identity communities whose foreign policy is shaped by collective self-understanding. **Nationalism** gives states their internal cohesion and frames the interests they pursue internationally. A state whose identity is built around ethnic grievance or revanchist nationalism will behave differently than one whose identity is organized around commercial liberalism or democratic solidarity. International institutions (the UN, NATO, the WTO, the International Criminal Court) complicate the pure anarchy picture: they create forums for coordination, generate norms that constrain state behavior, and sometimes make international cooperation more reliable than raw self-help logic predicts. The central debate between **realism** (anarchy forces states into competitive self-help regardless of institutions) and **liberalism** (institutions, interdependence, and norms substantially modify anarchic competition) is a debate about how much these institutions actually matter — and is one of the most productive ongoing arguments in the discipline.
