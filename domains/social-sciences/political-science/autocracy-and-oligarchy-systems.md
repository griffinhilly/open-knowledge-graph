# Autocracy and Oligarchy as Forms of Rule

## Core Idea
Autocracy is rule by a single absolute authority; oligarchy is rule by a small group of elites. Both concentrate power, typically lack democratic accountability mechanisms, and offer limited protection of individual rights. They differ in whether power is held by one person or a coordinating elite.

## Explainer

From your study of authoritarian governance, you know that authoritarian regimes share a rejection of democratic accountability and competitive elections, but they are not all the same. The distinction between **autocracy** and **oligarchy** captures a fundamental difference in where power is concentrated. In an autocracy, a single ruler — a monarch, dictator, or party general secretary — holds supreme authority. Their word is law; institutions exist to implement their will rather than constrain it. In an oligarchy, power is held by a small coordinating elite — a military junta, a ruling family network, a wealthy business class — who must govern collectively and manage internal competition for position and resources.

This structural difference has predictable consequences. **Autocrats** face the problem of succession: when power is personalized, there is no institutional mechanism for transferring it peacefully. The death or removal of an autocrat regularly triggers instability, civil war, or coup attempts, because no legitimate successor exists. To stabilize their position, autocrats typically cultivate a personal following, purge potential rivals, and use patronage to bind supporters to their survival. The paradox of autocracy is that the more successful an autocrat is at concentrating power, the more isolated they become from accurate information — subordinates tell them what they want to hear, and dissent disappears precisely when it might be most useful.

**Oligarchies** face a different problem: **elite coordination**. A ruling group must manage internal competition so that the group as a whole does not fracture into factions that undermine collective rule. This typically requires institutions — even non-democratic ones — to distribute rents, resolve disputes, and signal commitment. A military junta allocates ministerial portfolios. A party politburo manages promotions and ideological line. A ruling family carves up business monopolies among cousins. These institutions are not democratic but they are real mechanisms of governance. When elite coordination breaks down — when one faction decides it can gain more by defecting than by staying within the group — oligarchies collapse into either more concentrated autocracy (one faction wins) or chaos.

Both forms share the absence of **downward accountability**: neither the autocrat nor the oligarchic elite faces meaningful electoral removal or judicial independence. Stability instead depends on **co-optation** (buying the loyalty of key supporters with patronage, positions, or rents) and **coercion** (suppressing dissent through surveillance, imprisonment, or violence). The **selectorate theory** developed by Bueno de Mesquita and colleagues formalizes this logic: rulers maintain power by distributing private goods to a coalition of "essentials" — those whose support is critical — rather than providing public goods to the population at large. Autocrats have smaller essential coalitions and can therefore sustain loyalty with cheaper private payoffs; oligarchs have larger ones and must distribute more broadly. This framework predicts observable differences in policy: autocracies tend to provide fewer public goods (roads, schools, hospitals) relative to private transfers to elites, while oligarchies — with larger coalitions to satisfy — provide somewhat more.
