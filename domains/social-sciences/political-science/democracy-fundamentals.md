# Democracy: Fundamentals and Varieties

## Core Idea
Democracy is a system of governance in which power ultimately derives from and is accountable to the people through competitive elections, rule of law, and protection of individual rights. Democracy exists on a spectrum—perfect democracy is theoretical, while real democracies vary in how fully they embody democratic principles like equal voice, free elections, and meaningful accountability.

## Explainer

From your study of state power and sovereignty, you know that states hold a monopoly on legitimate violence within a territory. From political authority and legitimacy, you know that raw power alone does not produce stable governance — rulers require the acceptance or consent of the governed. **Democracy** is the specific institutional answer to the legitimacy problem that has become globally dominant: political authority is legitimate when it derives from and remains accountable to the people. The operative word is *accountable* — not merely that government claims to represent the people, but that mechanisms exist to hold rulers genuinely answerable and replaceable when they fail.

The minimal definition of democracy, associated with Joseph Schumpeter, is procedural: democracy is a system in which leaders are selected through competitive elections in which citizens choose among alternatives. This definition is deliberately thin — it focuses on the *mechanism* of selection, not on what governments do once in power. **Electoral democracy** requires free and fair elections, universal suffrage, and peaceful transfer of power when incumbents lose. By this standard, many countries qualify as democratic that fail to protect civil liberties or constrain executive power in more robust ways. The advantage of the thin definition is that it is measurable and avoids importing ideological preferences into the concept; the disadvantage is that it misses much of what makes democracy valuable.

**Liberal democracy** adds substantive requirements beyond elections: protection of individual rights and civil liberties (speech, press, assembly, religion), an independent judiciary that constrains both the government and majorities, rule of law applying equally to rulers and ruled, and protection of minority rights against majoritarian override. The tension between majority rule and minority rights is built into liberal democracy's architecture: majorities cannot vote to deprive minorities of fundamental rights, because doing so would undermine the conditions that make future competitive elections meaningful. This is why constitutional courts, independent media, and civil society organizations are not incidental features of liberal democracy but structural necessities — they are the mechanisms that prevent **illiberal democracy**, in which rulers are elected by genuine majorities but then dismantle the constraints that keep future elections competitive.

Democracy exists on a spectrum rather than as a binary, and the concept has evolved considerably from its origins in small Athenian city-states to the **representative democracy** that modern mass politics requires. No contemporary democracy is directly democratic in the Athenian sense; citizens do not vote on most decisions. Instead, they elect representatives and hold them accountable through periodic elections. This creates its own accountability problems: voters have limited information about what representatives actually do between elections; interest groups with concentrated resources can disproportionately shape policy; and principal-agent problems are pervasive throughout government bureaucracy. Understanding democracy as a live set of institutional arrangements — with characteristic failure modes as well as strengths — is more analytically useful than treating it as a settled achievement.
