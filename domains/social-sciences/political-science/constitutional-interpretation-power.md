# Judicial Review and Constitutional Interpretation

## Core Idea
Judicial review is the power of courts to invalidate laws or executive actions that violate the constitution. This power, absent from most legal systems historically, became prominent in the United States and has spread globally. Courts interpreting constitutions adopt different methodologies: originalist (literal historical meaning), living constitutionalist (evolving interpretation), purposivist (underlying intent), or textualist (plain language). The scope and exercise of judicial review profoundly affects the distribution of power among branches and the protection of individual rights.

## How It's Best Learned
Analyze landmark constitutional court cases and the reasoning courts employ. Compare different countries' approaches to constitutional review: who can bring cases, what standing do plaintiffs have, and how do courts justify overturning legislation?

## Common Misconceptions
Judicial review is not inherent to all democracies; many parliamentary systems explicitly reject it. Constitutional interpretation is not purely technical but involves value choices; claiming to be 'neutral' on interpretation does not eliminate those choices.

## Explainer

From your study of constitutionalism, you know that constitutions are higher law — foundational rules that constrain what ordinary legislation can do. From your study of the judicial branch, you know that courts interpret and apply law. Judicial review is where these two concepts collide: it is the power of courts to say that a law enacted by a democratic legislature violates the constitution and is therefore void. Understanding this power requires understanding both its logic and its deep political stakes.

The logic of judicial review is elegantly simple. If the constitution is supreme law, and ordinary legislation contradicts it, then one of the two must yield. As Chief Justice John Marshall argued in *Marbury v. Madison* (1803): "It is emphatically the province and duty of the judicial department to say what the law is." If courts cannot enforce the constitution against the legislature, the constitution becomes a suggestion, not a constraint. The alternative — allowing the legislature to determine the constitutionality of its own acts — creates a conflict of interest that would render constitutional limits unenforceable. Judicial review is the institutional solution to this problem: an independent body with lifetime tenure (in the US model) reviews laws against constitutional standards without depending on electoral outcomes for its authority.

But this clean logic immediately encounters a problem: **how do you interpret the constitution?** The document was written in different historical contexts, often in deliberately general language, and must be applied to situations its drafters never imagined. **Originalism** holds that the constitution means what its framers understood it to mean at ratification — this constrains interpretation but requires difficult historical inquiry and often produces answers that seem wildly disconnected from contemporary needs. **Living constitutionalism** holds that constitutional meaning evolves with social understandings — this allows the constitution to remain relevant but is accused of allowing judges to substitute their preferences for the democratic will. **Textualism** focuses strictly on the words as written, without reference to framers' intent. **Purposivism** focuses on the underlying goals the provision was meant to serve. Each methodology produces different outcomes on contested questions (abortion, gun rights, affirmative action), and no methodology is politically neutral in its effects.

The **counter-majoritarian difficulty** is the deepest tension in constitutional theory. Judicial review allows unelected, life-tenured judges to invalidate laws passed by elected majorities. In a democracy, this requires justification. The standard defenses are: (1) some rights should be insulated from majoritarian pressures precisely because majorities can oppress minorities; (2) courts are interpreting pre-committed constitutional choices, not making new policy; (3) the constitution itself was democratically enacted. Critics respond that courts do make policy, that constitutional text underdetermines results, and that when judges call themselves "neutral interpreters" they are disguising political choices. The selection of judges — through presidential appointment and Senate confirmation in the US, through varying processes elsewhere — is intensely political precisely because everyone understands that **interpretive methodology is not value-free**. The power to say what the constitution means is a profound form of political power, and the choice of who holds that power is itself a constitutional question.
