# Constitutionalism and Limited Government

## Core Idea
Constitutionalism is the principle that governmental power is limited by a supreme law (constitution) that is difficult to change. Constitutional governance establishes procedures for decision-making, distributes authority among institutions, and typically protects fundamental rights against governmental infringement.

## How It's Best Learned
Compare constitutions across democracies and autocracies. Analyze how constitutional courts interpret constitutions and limit government. Study constitutional changes and amendments to understand how constitutions evolve and constrain change.

## Explainer

Your prerequisites in authority/legitimacy and social contract theory establish the foundational problem constitutionalism is designed to solve. From social contract theory, you know that governments derive their authority from the consent of the governed — people accept political authority in exchange for the protection and coordination benefits the state provides. From authority and legitimacy, you know that power backed only by force is fragile; durable authority requires that people believe it is rightfully exercised. **Constitutionalism** is the institutional design response to this: if government derives its authority from the people, then there must be a law *above* the government — a **constitution** — that defines the scope of legitimate authority and places its exercise under enforceable constraints. Government without constitutional limits is just organized coercion; constitutional government is coercion made legitimate by operating within publicly agreed rules.

The core problem constitutionalism must solve is: who enforces the supreme law on those who hold power? If the ruler interprets and enforces their own constitutional limits, those limits are no limits at all. The most influential answer in modern constitutional design is **judicial review** — the authority of courts to strike down governmental acts that violate the constitution. This places a non-elected institution (the judiciary) in a position to constrain elected legislatures and executives, which is both the design's strength (independence from political pressure) and its perennial democratic tension (the counter-majoritarian problem: who elected the judges?). Different constitutional systems balance this differently. The American model grants the Supreme Court expansive review authority; the British model traditionally held that Parliament is supreme; European systems have developed specialized constitutional courts with more precisely defined jurisdiction.

The mechanisms constitutions use to limit government go beyond judicial review. **Separation of powers** divides authority across branches — legislative, executive, judicial — so that no single actor controls all the instruments of government. **Checks and balances** are the interlocking arrangements that give each branch tools to constrain the others: the executive can veto legislation, the legislature can override vetoes, the judiciary can invalidate laws, the legislature confirms judicial appointments. **Federalism** distributes authority across levels of government — national and subnational — adding a spatial dimension to the division of power. **Enumerated rights** carve out domains — speech, religion, due process — where majoritarian legislation cannot reach, protecting individuals and minorities from popular will.

What makes a constitution more than a document is the political culture and institutional practice that give it bite. Many countries have constitutions that enumerate rights and constraints but lack independent judiciaries, rule of law norms, or civil society capable of enforcing those limits. Conversely, some constitutional arrangements without a single written document — the UK's uncodified constitution — function as effective constraints because of entrenched norms and institutional practices. **Constitutional entrenchment** — the requirement of supermajorities or special procedures to amend — is designed to make the constitution resistant to short-term political majorities. But it also creates rigidity: a constitution that cannot adapt to changed circumstances may become an obstacle to legitimate governance. All constitutional systems face the tension between stability and adaptability, between protecting foundational commitments against transient majorities and allowing democratic majorities to govern themselves over time.
