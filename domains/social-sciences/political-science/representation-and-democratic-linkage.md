# Representation and Democratic Linkage

## Core Idea
Representation is the relationship between citizens and elected officials; representatives may adopt a delegate role (voting constituents' preferences), a trustee role (exercising independent judgment in constituent interest), or a partisan role (following party leadership). Democratic linkage—the mechanisms connecting citizen preferences to policy—operates through elections, political parties, and interest groups. Democracies vary in how directly preferences translate to outcomes: some systems prioritize mandates from elections, others emphasize deliberative judgment by representatives. The concept of representation itself is contested between those seeing it as mechanism for expressing constituent preferences and those seeing it as locus of deliberation and judgment.

## Explainer

The core tension in democratic representation is straightforward to state but difficult to resolve: when your elected representative disagrees with the majority of their constituents, what should they do? The **delegate model** says follow constituents — the representative's job is to be a faithful messenger. The **trustee model**, articulated classically by Edmund Burke, says use independent judgment — voters elect representatives because they trust their competence and character, not to rubber-stamp public opinion. In practice, most legislators oscillate between these roles depending on the issue: deferring to constituents on locally visible questions (a new highway, a factory closure), but exercising independent judgment on technical policy or foreign affairs where constituents lack information.

The **partisan model** adds a third dimension: many legislators primarily follow their party's positions, treating the party as the unit of representation. You can understand this from your prerequisite work on party systems — in a parliamentary system especially, the party is the vehicle through which voters actually influence government. A voter who chooses Labour or the Conservatives is effectively delegating to the party platform, not to an individual representative. This is why disciplined party voting in parliament can actually be *more* democratic in one sense: it translates election results directly into policy.

**Democratic linkage** names the broader set of mechanisms through which citizen preferences influence government decisions. Elections are the most obvious — they create accountability because representatives who ignore constituents can be voted out. But elections are blunt instruments: they happen infrequently, cover many issues simultaneously, and often hinge on non-policy factors. Political parties strengthen linkage by aggregating and clarifying policy positions across elections. Interest groups and advocacy organizations provide continuous pressure between elections, linking organized constituencies to specific policy decisions. The strength of democratic linkage varies enormously across institutional designs — proportional systems tend to produce closer alignment between vote shares and seat shares; majoritarian systems can produce large parliamentary majorities from slim vote pluralities.

The deeper conceptual dispute is whether representation is fundamentally about *mirroring* (representatives should demographically and attitudinally resemble constituents) or *acting for* (representatives must exercise judgment to serve constituent interests, which may differ from constituent preferences). A legislator who votes against her district's short-term economic interests to prevent an environmental catastrophe might be a poor delegate but an excellent trustee. Understanding which model you're invoking clarifies what counts as failure: a delegate fails by voting against stated constituent preferences; a trustee fails by making poor judgments regardless of what constituents wanted.
