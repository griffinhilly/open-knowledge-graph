# Intersectionality in Sociological Analysis

## Core Idea
Intersectionality examines how multiple systems of oppression (gender, race, class, sexuality, disability) interact in ways that can't be reduced to sum of individual oppressions. An individual's position results from simultaneous location in multiple hierarchies. Different intersectional positions produce distinct experiences and constraints even within the same demographic category.

## How It's Best Learned
Analyze a social phenomenon (poverty, education, incarceration) examining how it's shaped by intersecting systems. How does outcome vary by intersectional position?

## Common Misconceptions
Intersectionality isn't just adding up identities—it's analyzing how systems of domination mutually constitute each other.

## Explainer

From feminist sociology, you've learned to analyze gender as a system — not just individual prejudice or unequal outcomes, but a structured set of relations that shapes opportunities, expectations, identities, and power. Intersectionality, developed most influentially by legal scholar Kimberlé Crenshaw in the late 1980s and elaborated by sociologist Patricia Hill Collins, extends this insight: gender does not operate in isolation. It always intersects with race, class, sexuality, disability, nationality, and other axes of social organization. The framework argues that these intersections produce qualitatively distinct social positions that cannot be derived by simply "adding up" the effects of each axis separately.

The foundational insight came from a practical legal problem. In cases of workplace discrimination, Black women were often told they had no claim: either they couldn't prove gender discrimination (because the company hired plenty of white women) or they couldn't prove race discrimination (because the company hired plenty of Black men). Their specific experience — being simultaneously Black and women — fell through the cracks of single-axis analysis. Crenshaw called this **intersectional invisibility**: frameworks that treat each axis of oppression as separable systematically misrepresent the experiences of people who occupy multiple subordinated positions simultaneously.

The sociological mechanism is that systems of domination — **patriarchy**, **white supremacy**, capitalism, heteronormativity — are not parallel, independent structures stacked on top of each other. They are mutually constitutive: each shapes the form the others take. Racism is not experienced the same way for Black women as for Black men; sexism is not experienced the same way for white women as for women of color. To say that a Black woman experiences "racism plus sexism" implies that you can separate them — but there is no moment when she is only experiencing one. Her entire social location is shaped by their simultaneous operation. Collins uses the term **matrix of domination** to describe this structure: a grid of intersecting systems rather than a list of separate disadvantages.

A practical implication is that **within-group diversity** must always be attended to. Policies and programs designed to help "women" may primarily benefit white, middle-class, cisgender women if they treat gender as the only relevant axis. Research that analyzes "the Black experience" without attention to class or gender may aggregate across radically different social positions. Intersectionality demands that we disaggregate — that we ask whose experiences are captured by our categories, whose are erased, and what gets missed when we treat social groups as internally homogeneous.

The framework has also been extended beyond individual experience to analyze **institutions** and **social structures**. An intersectional analysis of the criminal justice system, for example, would examine not just racial disparities in sentencing, but how those disparities interact with gender (women's experiences differ markedly), class (quality of legal representation), immigration status, and disability — asking how the system simultaneously produces and reproduces multiple hierarchies. This structural application moves intersectionality from a framework for understanding individual lives to one for analyzing how systems of power operate together.
