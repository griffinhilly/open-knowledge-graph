# Erving Goffman and Dramaturgy

## Core Idea
Goffman analyzed social interaction as theatrical performance: individuals manage impressions ('front stage' behavior vs. 'back stage' authenticity), perform roles, and collaborate with others to maintain shared definitions of situations. All social life involves strategic presentation of self.

## Explainer

From symbolic interactionism you already understand that meaning is not inherent in objects or actions — it is constructed through interaction, through the shared symbols and interpretations participants bring to social situations. Goffman accepts this premise and asks: how, exactly, does that construction happen moment-to-moment in face-to-face encounters? His answer borrows from the theater.

The core metaphor is **performance**. Whenever we interact with others we are performing — not in a fake or consciously deceitful sense, but in the structural sense that we are presenting a version of ourselves calibrated to our audience and setting. The **front stage** is any social setting where others are watching: the classroom, the office, the dinner party. In front-stage contexts we exercise **impression management** — selecting clothing, words, posture, and demeanor to project the identity we want recognized. The **back stage** is where the performance relaxes: the kitchen before guests arrive, the break room, the private message thread. Back-stage behavior is not more "real" in some philosophical sense, but it is less regulated by the audience's gaze.

The concept connecting performance to social structure is the **definition of the situation**. When people interact, they work collaboratively to establish a shared frame — what kind of encounter is this, who are the participants, what rules apply? All parties cooperate to sustain a definition even when private signals might contradict it. A doctor and patient both perform their roles to maintain the clinical frame even when the doctor is uncertain and the patient is frightened. Breaking the frame — laughing at the wrong moment, openly acknowledging the performance — is experienced as a serious social violation, which reveals how much work the frame is doing and how much is at stake in maintaining it.

What elevates Goffman beyond theatrical metaphor is his analysis of **stigma** and **spoiled identity**. Some individuals carry attributes that disrupt the smooth performance of an accepted social identity — a physical disability, a criminal record, a mental illness, a stigmatized race or sexuality. Goffman showed how stigmatized individuals develop elaborate **passing** strategies to control information about their stigmatized attribute, and how **mixed contacts** (situations where stigmatized and "normal" individuals interact) generate mutual discomfort and interactional labor on both sides. This connects the micro-level analysis of face-to-face interaction to macro-level structures of inequality: which identities can be performed without effort, and which require constant management, reveals who the social order was built to accommodate.
