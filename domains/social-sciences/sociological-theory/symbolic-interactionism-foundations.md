# Symbolic Interactionism: Foundations

## Core Idea
Symbolic interactionism posits that humans act based on meanings they assign to things, and these meanings arise from social interaction. People continuously interpret and re-interpret the world, creating and modifying meanings through social processes. Society is built through countless interactions using symbols like language and gestures.

## Explainer

From the sociological imagination, you know to look for the connection between individual experience and social structure — to ask how biography and history intersect. Symbolic interactionism offers a specific answer to how that connection operates at the micro level: through meaning. Unlike structural approaches that explain behavior by pointing to institutions, norms, or material conditions, symbolic interactionism says that what matters is not the objective condition but the **meaning** actors attach to it. People act toward things based on what those things mean to them, and meanings are not fixed in objects — they are created, negotiated, and modified through ongoing social interaction.

The framework rests on three core premises, articulated by Herbert Blumer who coined the term "symbolic interactionism" in 1937, drawing on the social psychology of George Herbert Mead. First: humans act toward things based on the meanings those things have for them. A doctor's coat means authority; a police siren means threat or safety; a handshake means agreement. None of these meanings is inherent in the physical object — they are socially assigned. Second: the meaning of such things arises from social interaction. You learned what a doctor's coat means by being socialized into a culture that uses it consistently. Third: meanings are handled through an active **interpretive process**. People do not simply apply learned meanings automatically — they actively interpret situations, assess what is happening, and decide how to respond.

The concept of **symbols** is central. A symbol is anything that stands for something else and carries shared meaning: language is the master symbol system, but gestures, objects, rituals, clothing, and spatial arrangements all function symbolically. When you wave goodbye, both parties understand the gesture as farewell — not because of anything intrinsic to the hand motion, but because the meaning is shared through socialization and convention. Language in particular is extraordinarily powerful because it allows people to communicate about things that are absent, future, hypothetical, or abstract — enabling the elaborate coordination that constitutes social life.

Society, from this view, is not a fixed structure sitting above individuals and pressing down on them. It is continuously produced and reproduced through interaction. **Social order** is an ongoing accomplishment, not a given. Every time you greet a colleague, follow a conversational turn-taking norm, or adjust your behavior when you realize someone is watching, you are not just following a rule — you are actively maintaining the shared reality that makes coordinated life possible. When Harold Garfinkel's "breaching experiments" had students violate mundane norms (refusing to explain jokes, bargaining at fixed-price stores), they revealed just how much taken-for-granted interpretive work goes into sustaining ordinary interaction.

The power of symbolic interactionism lies in its attention to the **active, meaning-making subject**. It resists mechanistic accounts of behavior and insists on interpretation as a central social process. Its limitation, which critics raise, is that by focusing on micro-level interaction it may undertheorize how broader structural forces — inequality, power, institutional constraints — shape what meanings are available, which interpretations are possible, and whose definitions of reality come to prevail. Later developments in the tradition, including labeling theory and Goffman's dramaturgical analysis, push toward these structural questions while retaining the interactionist focus on situated meaning-making.
