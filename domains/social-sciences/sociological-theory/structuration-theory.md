# Giddens: Structuration Theory

## Core Idea
Structuration theory overcomes the agency/structure divide: structures enable and constrain action; actions reproduce and transform structures. Structures exist as rules and resources that agents recursively draw on, and through this recursive process, structures persist or change. Society is neither determined by structure nor freely created by agents but emerges through this duality.

## How It's Best Learned
Choose an institution and analyze how actors both reproduce and potentially transform it. What would it take to change structures?

## Common Misconceptions
Structuration doesn't claim perfect symmetry between agency and structure—power differentials mean some agents can transform structures more easily.

## Explainer

From your encounters with conflict theory and structural functionalism, you've seen two very different answers to a fundamental question: what determines social life — the choices of individual actors, or the structural forces that shape and constrain them? Structural functionalism tended to explain behavior by the requirements of the social system; conflict theory emphasized how structures reproduce power and inequality. Anthony Giddens argued that both traditions made the same mistake: they treated structure and agency as two separate things, with one ultimately explaining the other. **Structuration theory** dissolves this dualism entirely.

Giddens's key move is the concept of the **duality of structure**: structures are not external cages that constrain actors from the outside. Instead, structures are *constituted by* the very actions they enable and constrain. When you speak grammatically, you are drawing on the rules of language (a structure) — but in so doing, you are also reproducing those rules, keeping the language alive and in use. If everyone stopped speaking a language, the language would cease to exist as a structure. Structures exist only insofar as they are instantiated in recurrent social practices. They are not things "out there" but **rules and resources** that actors draw on in their ongoing activity.

**Rules** in Giddens's sense have two dimensions: they are constitutive (they define what counts as meaningful action — like chess rules that define what a "move" is) and regulative (they govern conduct normatively). **Resources** are either allocative (control over material things — money, property) or authoritative (control over people — authority, organizational position). When actors draw on rules and resources, they reproduce the structures those rules and resources embody. But they can also, in drawing on them strategically or creatively, transform them — use the rules in new ways, leverage resources in unexpected directions, build new coalitions. Structure both enables and constrains; it makes social life possible while also limiting it.

The concept of **practical consciousness** is central to how structuration explains social reproduction. Most of the time, actors don't articulate why they do what they do — they just do it. You know how to navigate a social interaction, follow office protocols, or observe gender norms without consciously consulting a rulebook. This tacit, practical knowledge is what keeps structures running smoothly without constant deliberate reproduction. **Discursive consciousness** — the ability to put reasons into words — is also available, but it's not the normal mode. Change becomes possible when routines are disrupted, when contradiction between practices becomes visible, or when agents gain reflexive awareness of the structures they reproduce. This is why Giddens sees structural change as neither impossible (as structural determinists might imply) nor easy (as voluntarists imagine): it requires agents to act differently in ways that gradually shift the patterns that constitute structure.
