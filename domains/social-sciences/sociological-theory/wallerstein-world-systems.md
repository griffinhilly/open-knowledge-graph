# Wallerstein and World-Systems Theory

## Core Idea
World-systems theory views capitalism as a global system with hierarchical division: core (wealthy, industrialized), periphery (colonized, extractive), semi-periphery (intermediate). This structure, not national culture, determines development paths. The system emerged in 16th century and perpetuates inequality; development in core requires underdevelopment in periphery.

## How It's Best Learned
Examine how a specific nation/region occupies its position: What historical processes placed it there? What mechanisms maintain its position?

## Common Misconceptions
World-systems theory isn't geographic determinism—positions can change, but structural inequalities persist across the system.

## Explainer

Conflict theory — your prerequisite — taught you to analyze society in terms of competing groups with unequal power, where dominant groups structure institutions in ways that serve their interests at the expense of others. Wallerstein's world-systems theory applies this logic at a scale far beyond the nation-state. Capitalism is analyzed as a single world-economy that operates across state boundaries and has done so since the sixteenth century. The relevant unit of analysis is not any individual society or nation but the **capitalist world-system** as a whole.

The system is organized around a three-tiered **core-periphery hierarchy**. **Core** zones — originally Northwestern Europe, now including North America and Japan — concentrate high-skill, capital-intensive production, extract surplus from the rest of the system, and exercise political and military dominance. **Peripheral** zones — largely former colonies in Africa, Asia, and Latin America — provide raw materials, agricultural commodities, and cheap labor; their surplus flows upward to the core rather than being reinvested locally. The **semi-periphery** is Wallerstein's crucial third zone: intermediate economies like Brazil, India, China, or historical Spain that are simultaneously exploited by the core and exploiting the periphery. The semi-periphery provides political stability to the overall system by giving ambitious rising economies somewhere to go without requiring them to directly displace core powers.

The key theoretical claim is that **underdevelopment is not a starting point but a product**. Peripheral regions are not simply behind on a development ladder that core countries have already climbed — they have been actively incorporated into the world-system in ways that prevent independent capitalist development. Colonial extraction dismantled pre-existing economies, created export-oriented monocultures, and transferred wealth systematically upward. Dependency is not accidental but structural: it is required by the logic of capital accumulation in the core. This is conflict theory's central insight — inequality is produced and maintained by structural arrangements — extended to the global level.

Positions within the system are not permanent, but movement is the exception rather than the rule. The United States moved from semi-periphery to core between 1865 and 1913; Japan and South Korea made similar transitions. But such movement typically requires specific political conditions (deliberate industrialization strategies, favorable geopolitical positioning) that the world-system constrains even as it allows. The key corrective to geographic determinism is understanding that the mechanism is economic and political, not cultural or natural. The structure persists not because periphery nations are incapable of development but because core powers have strong material interests in maintaining the arrangements that benefit them.
