# Class Struggle and Class Consciousness

## Core Idea
Marx argued that society is fundamentally divided between classes defined by their relationship to the means of production. Class struggle between bourgeoisie and proletariat is the engine of historical change. Class consciousness refers to workers' awareness of their collective interests and common position.

## Explainer

From your study of historical materialism, you know that Marx grounded his analysis in material conditions: who owns the means of production shapes everything else — ideology, law, politics, culture. With that foundation in place, class struggle and class consciousness explain how material conditions become historical forces. The economy doesn't just produce goods; it produces classes, and those classes stand in a relationship of fundamental antagonism.

Under capitalism, the basic class division is between the **bourgeoisie** — those who own factories, land, and capital — and the **proletariat** — those who own only their labor power and must sell it to survive. This is not merely an economic relationship but a relationship of domination and exploitation. Workers produce more value than they receive as wages; the surplus is appropriated by owners as profit. This **exploitation** is structural, not personal: even a well-meaning capitalist must extract surplus value or go bankrupt. The conflict of interests isn't a matter of bad attitudes — it is built into the system.

But recognizing this conflict requires **class consciousness**: workers must understand their situation, identify with their class rather than with their employer or nation, and see their collective interests as distinct from and opposed to those of capital. Marx distinguished between a **class in itself** — people who share the same objective position in the relations of production — and a **class for itself** — a class that has become conscious of its situation and acts collectively on that basis. The proletariat begins as a class in itself; revolutionary transformation requires it to become a class for itself. This gap explains why exploitation persists: workers may accept their situation as natural, identify with their employer, or see other workers as competitors rather than allies.

**False consciousness** — a concept later Marxists developed — refers to ideological beliefs that lead workers to misunderstand or accept their own subordination. Religion, nationalism, consumerism, and meritocratic beliefs all function, in this analysis, to obscure the class basis of social life. The ruling class controls not just the economy but the dominant ideas of an era: "the ruling ideas of every epoch are the ruling ideas of the ruling class." Class struggle does not only happen in workplaces and strikes — it happens in schools, media, and politics wherever the definition of what is "natural" or "inevitable" is contested. This is why consciousness, not just material conditions, matters for whether historical change occurs.
