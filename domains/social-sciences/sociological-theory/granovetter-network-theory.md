# Granovetter and Network Theory

## Core Idea
Network theory analyzes society as patterns of connections between actors. Granovetter showed that weak ties (distant acquaintances) often transmit novel information better than strong ties (close friends), solving the embeddedness problem. Social networks shape opportunities (jobs, ideas, resources) and constraints; position in networks determines access and influence.

## How It's Best Learned
Map your own network: who are your strong ties? Weak ties? Through which ties do novel opportunities come? How does your position enable/constrain action?

## Common Misconceptions
Network theory isn't reducing society to connections—it's showing how structure of connections shapes material outcomes and social possibilities.

## Explainer

The sociological imagination trained you to see individual lives as shaped by larger social structures. Network theory gives that insight a precise analytical form: the structure that matters most immediately is the structure of your relationships. Who you are connected to, and how those connections are themselves connected, shapes what information you receive, what opportunities come your way, and what constraints you face. Mark Granovetter's contribution was to make a counterintuitive argument about which connections matter most — and it turned out not to be the obvious ones.

The key insight is the **strength of weak ties**. Your **strong ties** are your close friends, family, and regular collaborators — people you interact with frequently, trust deeply, and share much in common with. Intuitively, these seem like your most valuable social resources. But Granovetter pointed out a structural problem: your close friends are likely connected to each other and to many of the same people you know. Their information networks largely overlap with yours. Your **weak ties** — distant acquaintances, former colleagues, friends-of-friends — are different: they move in different social circles, hear different things, and access different opportunities. When Granovetter studied how people found jobs, he found that most found them through acquaintances they saw rarely, not through close friends. The counterintuitive conclusion: weak ties are often *better* conduits for novel information than strong ties, precisely because they bridge otherwise disconnected social clusters.

The mechanism here is **bridging**: weak ties typically span different social **cliques** or clusters, functioning as bridges between groups that would otherwise remain informationally isolated. Granovetter called this the **embeddedness** problem — economic and social action is embedded in networks of relationships, and the structure of those networks determines what is possible. An actor who sits between two otherwise unconnected clusters — what structural sociologist Ron Burt later called occupying a **structural hole** — can broker information, control access, and extract strategic advantages unavailable to actors embedded within a single dense cluster.

This framework reframes what it means to be well-positioned in society. Network theory doesn't say talent, effort, or class position are irrelevant — it says those things interact with network position. Two equally talented people with equal economic resources but different network positions will have very different outcomes, because one has access to the diverse information flows and brokerage opportunities that come from bridging weak ties, while the other, deeply embedded in a single cluster, may have strong support but little access to new possibilities. This is why network analysis is not merely a metaphor but a research methodology: you can map networks, measure centrality, identify bridges, and trace how information and resources actually flow — making visible the structural advantages and disadvantages that individual-level analysis misses.
