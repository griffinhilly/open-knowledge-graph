# Feminist Sociology and Intersectionality

## Core Idea
Feminist sociology critiques how sociology traditionally ignored or marginalized women and gender relations. Intersectionality, developed by Black feminist scholars, argues that race, class, gender, sexuality, and other dimensions of identity intersect to produce unique forms of inequality and oppression, not reducible to any single dimension.

## Explainer

When you use the sociological imagination — your prerequisite — you shift from individual troubles to public issues by locating personal experience within historical and social structures. Feminist sociology applies exactly this move to gender: what looks like individual women's "choices" or "failures" in careers, families, or public life often reflects systematic structural arrangements that advantage men and disadvantage women. But early feminist sociology made a parallel error: in critiquing gender inequality, it often treated "women" as a unified category, as if all women faced identical barriers. This is where intersectionality corrects and deepens feminist analysis.

**Intersectionality** was developed primarily by Black feminist scholars, most famously articulated by Kimberlé Crenshaw in her analysis of Black women in anti-discrimination law. Crenshaw observed that employment discrimination suits required plaintiffs to identify *either* race discrimination *or* sex discrimination — but Black women's experiences didn't fit neatly into either category alone. Black women weren't harassed in the same ways as white women; they weren't excluded in the same ways as Black men. Their position at the intersection of race and gender created a *unique* disadvantage that wasn't simply race plus gender added together. The intersection itself generates a qualitatively different form of oppression that single-axis analysis erases entirely.

The theoretical claim of intersectionality is that systems of domination — race, gender, class, sexuality, disability, nationality, and others — are **mutually constitutive**: they don't operate as separate tracks running in parallel, they are woven together to produce a single, complex social fabric. A Black lesbian working-class woman doesn't experience racism "plus" sexism "plus" homophobia "plus" class disadvantage as separate burdens that add up. She occupies a *position* within a system where all these dimensions are simultaneously operative. This means researchers and advocates cannot address any one dimension adequately without understanding its articulation with the others. Patricia Hill Collins called this system of interlocking oppressions the **matrix of domination**.

Methodologically, intersectionality is both a theoretical framework and a research orientation. Intersectional research deliberately attends to who is made invisible by single-axis analysis and actively centers the experiences of those at multiply-marginalized positions. This has transformed large sections of sociology: from stratification research (examining compound disadvantage, not just race or class separately) to organizational sociology (examining who faces what kinds of discrimination in workplaces) to health sociology (explaining why health disparities follow the intersections of race and class, not just one alone). The core lesson is that heterogeneity *within* social categories — often ignored in aggregate statistics — is theoretically and empirically meaningful, not noise to be averaged away.
