# Émile Durkheim and Social Facts

## Core Idea
Durkheim defined social facts as collective phenomena (norms, institutions, beliefs, laws) that exist independently of any individual and exert coercive force on behavior. Social facts must be studied empirically as things, not derived from individual psychology.

## Explainer

One of sociology's founding arguments is that society is not simply the sum of its individual members — it has its own emergent properties that cannot be explained by studying individuals alone. Émile Durkheim formalized this argument through the concept of the **social fact**: a way of acting, thinking, or feeling that is external to the individual and exercises a constraining force over them.

The externality claim is counterintuitive. When you speak French, feel shame at dishonesty, or obey traffic laws, it may feel like these are your own choices. But Durkheim's point is that you did not invent French, you did not create the moral sense that makes dishonesty feel wrong, and you did not design traffic law. These things existed before you, will exist after you, and would exist even if you as an individual had never been born. They are not your properties — they are properties of the social world that you inhabit. This makes them external to you in a specific sense: they have an existence independent of any particular individual's mind.

The constraint claim follows: social facts shape behavior not merely by providing options, but by exerting pressure. Break a social norm — wear formal business attire to a beach party, or skip a funeral to watch television — and you will feel the constraint in the form of social disapproval, exclusion, or censure. Some constraints are legal (try refusing to pay taxes). Others are informal but powerful. Durkheim's methodological instruction was to treat these constraints as **things** — observable phenomena subject to empirical investigation — rather than as mere ideas to be derived from first principles about human nature or individual psychology.

This was a polemical claim aimed at two rivals. Against the psychologists, Durkheim insisted that you cannot explain social patterns by studying individual minds. Suicide rates, for instance, vary systematically across religious groups, occupations, and marital statuses — but these are all social-level variables. The individual psychology of any particular suicide victim cannot explain why Protestants consistently have higher rates than Catholics in the same city. Against the philosophers, Durkheim insisted that sociology should be an empirical science, not a branch of moral philosophy or armchair theorizing about human nature.

The concept of social facts is the foundation for everything that follows in Durkheim's work. Mechanical and organic solidarity are social facts about different types of societies. Anomie is a social fact — a state of normlessness at the collective level. The sacred and the profane are social facts. Understanding social facts as a category helps you see why Durkheim's sociology looks so different from, say, Weber's: where Weber focuses on the meanings individuals attach to their actions, Durkheim focuses on the collective structures that constrain and produce those meanings in the first place.
