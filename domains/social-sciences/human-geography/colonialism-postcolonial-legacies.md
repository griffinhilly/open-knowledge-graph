# Colonialism and Postcolonial Geographies

## Core Idea
Colonialism remapped the world, drawing borders, extracting resources, imposing institutions, and subordinating peoples in service of European imperialism. Colonial legacies persist in current global inequalities, unequal trade relationships, geopolitical tensions, and political instability in formerly colonized regions. Postcolonial geographies examine how former colonies navigate, resist, and rebuild from colonial legacies.

## How It's Best Learned
Trace how colonialism created current territorial boundaries—which cut across ethnic groups and combine incompatible peoples. Examine how colonial extraction of resources shaped current poverty. Study postcolonial movements and independence struggles. Analyze how colonial institutions persist in law, education, and governance.

## Common Misconceptions
Colonialism is not merely historical; colonial relationships persist in current structures of inequality. Postcolonial states are not independent if they remain economically dependent on former colonizers. Blaming colonialism does not excuse postcolonial states' own oppression and exploitation of their populations.

## Explainer

You've already studied the geographic legacy of colonialism — the broad pattern of how European empires carved up Africa, Asia, the Americas, and Oceania. This topic builds from that foundation by asking a sharper question: not just what happened, but how the structures created during colonial rule continue to shape the geography of the present. The central insight is that colonialism was not a historical episode with a defined end point. It was a process of building infrastructure — administrative, economic, cultural, and psychological — designed to extract value from peripheries for metropolitan benefit. That infrastructure did not disappear when flags were lowered at independence.

Consider the **arbitrary border problem** as a geographic starting point. Colonial powers drew administrative boundaries according to their own logics — watersheds, spheres of influence, treaty lines — with little regard for the ethnic, linguistic, and ecological boundaries that organized actual human communities. The result was a map that grouped hostile peoples into the same state and split cohesive communities across multiple states. The borders of modern Nigeria, Sudan, India, and dozens of other postcolonial states reflect this legacy directly. Post-independence conflicts in these regions often trace back to the pressure these artificial boundaries placed on communities that had never shared political institutions. What looks like "tribal conflict" or "ethnic violence" frequently has colonial border-drawing as a proximate structural cause.

**Economic dependency** is the second major geographic legacy. Colonies were developed not as integrated economies but as extraction systems: mines, plantations, and ports were built to move raw materials outward and manufactured goods inward. Railway networks in colonial Africa, for instance, were built not to connect African cities to each other but to connect mining and agricultural zones to coastal ports. This radial infrastructure pattern — everything pointed outward toward the metropole — is still visible in the transport geography of many postcolonial states and continues to inhibit intra-regional trade. After independence, many states remained locked into producing commodities whose prices are set on global markets dominated by former colonizers, a condition theorists of **dependency** and **world-systems** call **neocolonialism** — formal political independence combined with continued economic subordination.

**Institutional persistence** is subtler but equally consequential. Colonial administrations left behind legal codes, property regimes, languages of governance, educational systems, and bureaucratic structures. Many postcolonial states inherited institutions designed for authoritarian administration rather than democratic governance — designed, specifically, to manage populations rather than serve them. The concentration of administrative power in capitals (often coastal port cities selected for extraction logistics), the weakness of rural infrastructure, and the disconnect between formal legal systems and customary practice all reflect colonial design choices that postcolonial governments have struggled to dismantle. Changing laws is far easier than changing the geographic and institutional patterns embedded in cities, roads, property records, and educational curricula over decades of colonial rule.

Postcolonial geography, as a field, does not argue that colonialism explains everything wrong in formerly colonized societies — postcolonial governments have made their own decisions, often catastrophically bad ones. Rather, it insists that the starting conditions for those decisions were not neutral. When analyzing global inequality — why some regions are wealthy and others are not, why capital flows the way it does, why certain borders are unstable — the colonial period is not ancient history but the recent past whose structures remain materially present in the landscape, the economy, and the institutional framework of the world today.
