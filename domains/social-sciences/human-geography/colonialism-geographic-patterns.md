# Colonialism and Geographic Patterns of Inequality

## Core Idea
Colonialism created geographic patterns of inequality that persist in postcolonial states. Colonial powers extracted resources, established boundaries, and imposed economic systems that advantaged colonizers. Postcolonial geographies inherit these inequalities, visible in borders, cities, economies, and social hierarchies.

## Explainer

You have already studied how **political territory** and **economic production** are organized through power — how control of land, resources, and labor gets enforced and institutionalized. Colonialism is the most consequential application of that territorial logic in modern history. Between the 15th and 20th centuries, European powers seized control of the vast majority of the world's land surface, and in doing so, created geographic structures that continue to shape inequality today. Understanding colonial geography is not merely historical — it is the prerequisite for understanding why the map of global poverty looks the way it does.

The most visible legacy is **borders**. Colonial administrators drew the boundaries of today's African, Asian, and Middle Eastern states primarily for administrative convenience and resource extraction, with little regard for pre-existing ethnic, linguistic, or political communities. The Berlin Conference of 1884–85 famously partitioned Africa among European powers without a single African representative present. The result was states that lumped together traditional enemies and split apart coherent communities — producing the conditions for ethnic conflict, civil war, and contested sovereignty that characterize many postcolonial states. These borders are not accidents; they are spatial expressions of colonial power, frozen in place by independence movements that inherited them.

Colonial **economic geography** was organized around extraction. Transportation infrastructure — roads, railways, ports — was built not to connect interior communities to each other but to move raw materials from resource zones to coastal export ports. This spatial logic, sometimes called the **colonial trade structure**, created economies deeply integrated into European markets but weakly integrated with their own neighbors. When independence came, these infrastructure patterns did not disappear; they shaped where investment flowed, which cities grew, and which regions remained peripheral. The classic example is Africa's railway network: lines run perpendicular to the coasts into resource-extraction zones, with almost no lateral connections between countries — a geography of export, not regional integration.

**Primate cities** — cities vastly larger and more economically dominant than any other in their country — are another colonial spatial artifact. Colonizers typically concentrated administrative and commercial functions in a single port city (Lagos, Nairobi, Accra, Bombay), which became the node connecting the colony to the metropole. This concentration of infrastructure, institutions, and economic opportunity persisted after independence, producing hyperurbanization in a single city while interior regions remained underdeveloped. Meanwhile, colonial land tenure systems — plantation agriculture, settler land grabs, communal land dismantlement — restructured rural property relations in ways that still determine who can access land and on what terms.

The persistence of colonial geographic patterns challenges **modernization theory's** assumption that development is simply a matter of time and investment following a universal path. Postcolonial geographers argue that underdevelopment is not a natural starting condition but a produced outcome — the geographic infrastructure of inequality was deliberately built. This does not mean colonial geographies are immutable. But it does mean that development interventions that treat geography as a neutral container, rather than as a politically produced landscape with a colonial history, will consistently misdiagnose why some places remain poor while others develop.
