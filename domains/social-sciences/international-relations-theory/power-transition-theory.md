# Power Transition Theory and Great Power War

## Core Idea
Power transition theory argues that wars occur when a rising power challenges a declining hegemon. As long as the hegemon maintains clear superiority, peace prevails. But when a rising power approaches parity, war becomes likely because the rising power expects future decline if the current order persists, and the hegemon fears losing its privileged position. The transition period—when capabilities are roughly equal and intentions are unclear—is most dangerous. Historically, power transitions preceded many great power wars.

## How It's Best Learned
Analyze the rising US challenging Britain's naval dominance (peaceful transition), Germany's rise challenging France and Britain (led to WWI), and Japan's rise challenging Western dominance (led to WWII).

## Common Misconceptions
Power transition theory predicts war as likely but not inevitable—peaceful accommodation of rising powers is possible if hegemon and challenger negotiate new rules.

## Explainer

From your study of balance-of-power theory and power capabilities distribution, you know the basic intuition of realist IR: states monitor the distribution of power carefully and form alliances to prevent any single state from achieving dominance. Balance-of-power theory predicts that states balance against the most powerful actor — and that the system is most stable when no one is dominant. **Power transition theory**, developed by A.F.K. Organski in the 1950s and later extended by Jacek Kugler, reaches a strikingly different conclusion: war is most likely not when power is unequal, but when it is becoming equal.

The core logic runs as follows. The international system is hierarchical: a dominant **hegemon** at the top sets the rules of the order — the norms, institutions, and arrangements that govern trade, security, and diplomacy. Smaller states position themselves as satisfied or dissatisfied with this order. As long as the hegemon is clearly dominant, the system is stable: the cost of challenging the order is too high, and even dissatisfied states wait. But as a rising challenger grows toward parity, the calculus shifts. The challenger, whose capabilities are still increasing, calculates that if the current order persists, it will eventually be locked out of the benefits it believes it deserves. The hegemon, whose relative position is declining, calculates that acting now — before the challenger becomes too strong — may be its last window of advantage. This is the **danger zone**: when capabilities are near-equal and both sides have incentives to prefer the risks of war to the trajectory they project into the future.

The contrast with balance-of-power theory is instructive. Balance-of-power predicts that dangerous periods occur when one state achieves preponderance — so the prescription is to balance early and prevent dominance. Power transition theory inverts this: preponderance is stable; *equality* is dangerous. The theory also adds the dimension of satisfaction/dissatisfaction with the existing order, which pure capability-based theories ignore. A rising power that is satisfied with the international order — like the United States rising to challenge Britain's naval supremacy — can be accommodated without war. A rising power that is dissatisfied — like Germany's rising ambitions before WWI, or Japan's pre-WWII imperial project — is much more dangerous, because the transition also involves a demand for a *different* international order.

The historical test cases shape the theory's contours. The US-UK transition is the canonical peaceful case: the US surpassed Britain in industrial power around 1870–1880, but both shared cultural ties, a broadly compatible vision of international order, and Britain made strategic concessions (the Monroe Doctrine, naval withdrawal from the Western Hemisphere) that acknowledged US primacy. The Germany-Britain-France case is the canonical conflict case: Germany's rapid industrialization generated nationalist ambition for a new world order, dissatisfied with a status quo that excluded it from colonial prestige. The theory predicts not just that Germany rose, but that a dissatisfied challenger at rough parity with the status quo hegemon creates the conditions for systemic war.

The contemporary relevance centers on China's rise and its relationship with US hegemony — sometimes called the **Thucydides Trap** in popular discourse (after Athens and Sparta). Power transition theory would identify the danger zone not as China's current position (still significantly behind the US in overall capability) but as the period when capabilities converge, if dissatisfaction with the US-led order remains high. The prescription is clear but difficult: peaceful transitions require the rising power to be incorporated into the order in ways that address its legitimate grievances, and the hegemon to accommodate rather than retrench. Neither is easy when domestic political pressures in both countries reward confrontation.
