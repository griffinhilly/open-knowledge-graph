# Classical Realism and the Pursuit of Power

## Core Idea
Morgenthau's classical realism grounds IR in timeless human nature—the perpetual drive for power—rather than in systemic structure. States pursue power as an end in itself and as a means to security. Power-seeking is constant across cultures and history, making conflict a recurring feature of IR.

## How It's Best Learned
Read Morgenthau's *Politics Among Nations* (especially the "Six Principles of Political Realism") to understand how he distinguishes political realism from idealism and moralism. Compare his argument to Thucydides' account of the Melian Dialogue in the *History of the Peloponnesian War*, which Morgenthau treats as the foundational text of realist thought.

## Common Misconceptions
- Classical realism is not pure cynicism; Morgenthau believed diplomacy, international law, and moral constraints could moderate the struggle for power, but could not eliminate it.
- Realism does not predict constant war; it predicts the recurrence of power politics and conflict as structural features of international life, with varying intensity.

## Explainer

Your study of realism-in-ir established the core realist premises: states are the primary actors in international politics, the international system is anarchic (no overarching authority above states), and states must therefore rely on self-help for security. Classical realism accepts these premises but locates their *source* in a distinctive place. Hans Morgenthau, writing in the wake of the Second World War, argued that what drives international conflict is not the structure of the system but something deeper and more intractable — **human nature**.

Morgenthau's central claim is that human beings are driven by an irreducible **animus dominandi** — a lust for power, a desire to dominate others. This drive is not a reaction to circumstances; it is a permanent feature of the human condition, as consistent across history as other basic drives. States, being composed of and led by human beings, reflect this drive at the collective level. Statesmen pursue power not merely as a means to security (though they do that too) but as an end in itself — because power is intrinsically gratifying to those who possess it. This is the move that distinguishes classical realism from its structural successor: Morgenthau's realism is ultimately a theory of political psychology, rooted in a pessimistic reading of human moral psychology, while structural realism (which you will study next) replaces that psychological claim with a systemic argument.

The concept of **national interest defined in terms of power** is Morgenthau's methodological core. He argues that the statesman's job is to think in terms of power — to ask not "what is morally right in the abstract?" but "what serves the national interest, defined as the accumulation and maintenance of power sufficient for security and influence?" This is not a claim that morality is irrelevant; Morgenthau was himself deeply moral and believed that prudent statecraft required restraint. But he insisted that moral principles cannot be applied to international politics without being mediated through the logic of power. A state that ignores power considerations in favor of moral purity will be destroyed, and with it whatever values it sought to promote. The "realism" in classical realism is precisely this insistence on starting from the world as it is rather than the world as it ought to be.

The concept of the **balance of power** follows naturally. If all states seek power, the only mechanism that prevents any single state from dominating all others is a rough equilibrium among competing states. Statesmen should manage this balance — building coalitions against rising powers, avoiding unnecessary conflicts that exhaust national strength, and preserving a system of multiple centers of power rather than allowing hegemonic domination. This was the logic of European diplomacy from the 17th through 19th centuries, which Morgenthau admired: skilled diplomats like Metternich and Bismarck managed the balance without moralistic crusades, and the result was relative stability. The catastrophe of the 20th century, in Morgenthau's diagnosis, resulted partly from the displacement of pragmatic balance-of-power thinking by nationalist ideology and liberal internationalism — both of which he regarded as forms of dangerous utopianism that obscured the permanent reality of power politics.

Classical realism, then, is a fundamentally tragic worldview: conflict is inherent in human nature, not contingent on reformable institutions. You cannot end power politics by designing better international organizations (the liberal view) or by restructuring the international system (the structural realist view). What you can do is manage it with prudence, restraint, and unsentimental attention to the balance of power. As you move into structural realism, notice the precise point where Waltz departs from Morgenthau: he keeps the anarchic structure and the self-help logic, but jettisons the human nature premise entirely, grounding conflict in systemic structure rather than psychology. That pivot is the central theoretical fault line in the realist tradition.

Foreign policy decisions should be evaluated by whether they serve this interest, not by whether they conform to universal moral principles. This differs from a morality-based approach in that moral principles cannot be directly applied to international relations: what is virtuous for an individual (turning the other cheek, self-sacrifice) can be fatal for a state. Morgenthau did not believe morality was irrelevant — he argued for prudence and restraint as guides — but he insisted that moral reasoning must be mediated through the logic of power rather than applied naively."
  explanation: "The key distinction is between domestic ethics (which can be applied relatively directly to individuals) and international statecraft (which operates under anarchy and requires a different logic). Morgenthau is not saying statesmen should be immoral; he is saying that applying domestic moral reasoning to international politics produces catastrophic results because the international system has no enforcer and no shared moral community equivalent to what exists within states."

- question: "What is the 'animus dominandi' in Morgenthau's theory, and why does he ground realism in this concept rather than in the structure of the international system?"
  type: short-answer
  answer: "The animus dominandi is the drive to dominate — the pervasive human desire for power over others that Morgenthau treats as a permanent feature of human nature. He grounds realism in this concept rather than in systemic structure because he believes the source of international conflict is not contingent on any particular configuration of states or institutions. Even if the international system were redesigned — with stronger international law, more international organizations, better-designed institutions — the underlying human drive for power would find expression through whatever channels are available. This makes the classical realist analysis more pessimistic than structural realism: because the source of conflict is human nature, not an alterable system, it cannot be engineered away. Structural realism (Waltz) rejects this premise, arguing that states may be internally pacific but are forced into power competition by anarchy."
  explanation: "This question tests the precise location of the classical/structural divide within realism. Morgenthau: problem is human nature → permanent. Waltz: problem is systemic structure → can be analyzed and potentially modified. Both are pessimistic about liberal projects, but for different reasons."
