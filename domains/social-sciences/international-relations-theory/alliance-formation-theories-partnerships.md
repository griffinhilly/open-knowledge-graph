# Alliance Formation and Security Partnerships

## Core Idea
States form alliances to balance threats or to side with rising powers (bandwagoning). The choice between balancing and bandwagoning depends on threat level, domestic politics, and geographic proximity. Alliance credibility—making others believe commitments—is crucial but difficult to establish, creating alliance dilemmas.

## Explainer

From your study of balance of power, you know that states in an anarchic international system pay close attention to the distribution of capabilities — who has power, who is gaining it, and what coalitions might form to check a rising state. Alliance theory extends this logic into a specific question: when a threatening or powerful state emerges, how do other states respond? The dominant realist answer is **balancing**: states threatened by a rising power will align against it to prevent domination, because no state wants to risk being subordinated. The opposite response is **bandwagoning**: siding with the threatening power rather than opposing it, hoping to profit from association with the likely winner or to appease an aggressor before it turns hostile. The balance-of-power logic predicts balancing as the dominant behavior, but real-world alliance choices frequently look more like bandwagoning — small states routinely align with great powers rather than against them.

Stephen Walt's **balance of threat theory** resolves this puzzle by arguing that states balance against *threats*, not merely against raw capabilities. A state may be powerful but non-threatening if it is geographically remote, has a clearly defensive military posture, or has demonstrated benign intentions through prior behavior. Conversely, a state with moderate capabilities but aggressive intentions and geographic proximity poses a higher threat level. This modification explains why many states aligned with the United States rather than against it during the Cold War: they perceived the Soviet Union — closer, more aggressive, and ideologically hostile — as the primary threat, making the more distant and powerful US a natural alliance partner rather than a target for balancing. Geography, offense-defense balance, and perceived intent all modulate the threat calculus.

**Alliance credibility** is the central management problem. An alliance is a commitment: a promise that if you are attacked, your ally will help. But in an anarchic system with no enforcement mechanism, commitments are only as reliable as the incentives to honor them at the moment of crisis. Your ally must believe you will actually bear costs on their behalf. **Extended deterrence** — the promise to defend a third party — is especially fragile: the defending state must convince both the ally and the potential aggressor that it will go to war for someone else's territory. This is why alliance credibility requires visible signals — forward deployment of troops on ally soil, joint exercises, public declarations, and a track record of past commitment-keeping. US troops stationed in Western Europe and East Asia are partly about military capability but primarily about credibility: they make abandonment costly and therefore harder to credibly threaten.

The core tension in alliance management is the dilemma between **abandonment** and **entrapment**. Abandonment occurs when an ally defects from its commitment when its partner actually needs help — a risk that makes junior partners anxious and leads them to demand ever-more-credible signals. Entrapment occurs when a state is dragged into a conflict it would not have chosen independently because its alliance commitment requires it. July 1914 is the canonical entrapment case: Austria-Hungary's local dispute with Serbia triggered cascading alliance commitments (Germany, Russia, France, Britain) that transformed a regional crisis into a world war neither side had intended. Managing the abandonment-entrapment tradeoff — making commitments credible enough to deter attack without creating automatic escalation escalators — is what makes alliance design an enduring problem in international security.
