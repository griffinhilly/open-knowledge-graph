# English School: Pluralism and Solidarism

## Core Idea
The English School debate between pluralists and solidarists concerns whether international society is merely a system of independent states respecting sovereignty (pluralism) or whether shared norms create deeper obligations toward justice (solidarism). This debate underpins disagreements about humanitarian intervention and universal values.

## Explainer

From your study of the English School's concept of international society, you know that this tradition occupies a distinctive middle position in IR theory — rejecting both realism's anarchic billiard-ball world and liberal institutionalism's vision of progressive convergence toward a world government. International society, for English School thinkers, is real and substantive: states share common interests, recognize each other's legitimacy, and abide by common rules and institutions. But within this shared framework, a fundamental dispute divides the tradition. **Pluralists** and **solidarists** agree that international society exists — they disagree sharply about what kind of society it is and what it demands.

The **pluralist** position, associated most closely with Hedley Bull, holds that international society rests on a thin but durable foundation: mutual recognition of sovereignty, non-interference, and respect for existing borders. This minimalist consensus is valuable precisely because it accommodates radical diversity — states with vastly different domestic political systems, religious traditions, economic arrangements, and cultural values can all participate in international society without being required to share a common substantive vision of justice. For pluralists, this tolerance for difference is not a bug but a feature. The alternative — using international society to enforce particular moral standards — risks imposing the values of powerful states on weaker ones under the guise of universal principles. Sovereignty, on this view, is a protective norm that enables diversity to coexist under shared rules.

The **solidarist** position, developed by thinkers like Nicholas Wheeler and, in qualified form, Andrew Linklater, holds that this minimalist consensus is morally inadequate and empirically incomplete. If international society is constituted by shared norms, why should those norms stop at mutual recognition? Solidarists observe that the norms governing international society have in fact expanded over time — from prohibitions on certain weapons, to human rights treaties, to the emerging norm of the **Responsibility to Protect** (R2P). This evolution suggests that states themselves have recognized obligations beyond mere coexistence: an obligation not to allow mass atrocities within sovereign borders to proceed unchallenged. For solidarists, when a government massacres its own population, the protective logic of sovereignty inverts — the state has forfeited its claim to non-interference.

The practical stakes of this debate become concrete in cases like Kosovo (1999), Libya (2011), and Syria (2011–present). In Kosovo, NATO intervened without UN Security Council authorization to stop ethnic cleansing — a solidarist intervention that pluralists argued violated the non-interference norm and set a dangerous precedent for powerful states to use humanitarian rhetoric to pursue strategic interests. In Libya, the Security Council authorized intervention under R2P; the subsequent regime change led Russia and China to conclude they had been manipulated, and they blocked similar authorization for Syria. These episodes reveal that the pluralist-solidarist divide is not merely academic — it tracks real disagreements among great powers about what international society permits and requires.

What makes the debate productive rather than simply intractable is that both positions capture genuine values. Pluralism is right that sovereignty protects political diversity and restrains powerful states from imposing their preferences on others; the history of colonialism demonstrates how readily "civilizing missions" become instruments of domination. Solidarism is right that a society of states indifferent to mass atrocities within borders is morally deficient and practically unstable. Most contemporary English School scholarship recognizes this tension rather than resolving it, asking instead how **international society can be thickened** — developing shared norms on human rights and intervention — without simply becoming a vehicle for the preferences of the powerful. The debate remains live precisely because neither side has a conclusive answer.
