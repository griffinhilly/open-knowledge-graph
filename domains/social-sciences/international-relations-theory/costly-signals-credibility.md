# Costly Signals and Building Credibility

## Core Idea
Costly signals are actions that a state would only take if it truly intends to follow through—they raise the cost of backing down. Deploying troops to a region, imposing sanctions on oneself as punishment for breaking an agreement, or making public commitments are costly signals. Credibility comes from making your intentions and commitment costly enough that bluffing becomes irrational. This explains why states sometimes deliberately make decisions hard to reverse.

## How It's Best Learned
Compare cheap talk (verbal threats) with costly signals (troop deployments). Why do leaders make public commitments even when keeping them becomes inconvenient?

## Common Misconceptions
Costliness alone does not guarantee credibility—if a state can afford the cost, it might still be bluffing. Credibility requires that the cost of bluffing is higher than the benefit.

## Explainer

From your study of signaling and resolve, you know that international politics is full of communication problems: states cannot directly observe each other's intentions, and states that are bluffing have every incentive to look like states that are not. The result is a classic credibility puzzle — words alone cannot reliably signal resolve, because words are cheap. The theory of costly signals is the central framework for understanding how states can make their threats and commitments believable despite this problem.

A **costly signal** is an action whose cost is significantly higher for a bluffing state than for a genuinely committed one. The logic is simple: if backing down after a signal would be worse than not having signaled at all, a rational actor would only send the signal if it genuinely intended to follow through. Consider a military deployment. Moving troops to a border region costs money, creates domestic political exposure, and — critically — raises the political and reputational cost of a retreat. A government that deploys troops and then backs down looks weak, unreliable, and domestically vulnerable. A government that genuinely intends to fight can absorb those costs; a government that is bluffing faces a much worse expected outcome. The deployment therefore separates the resolved from the irresolute in a way that a speech cannot.

The concept of **tying hands** — making retreat costly in ways visible to the adversary — is a central mechanism. Public ultimatums work partly this way: a leader who announces publicly that "X will not be tolerated" has staked domestic political credibility on the claim. If X then occurs without consequences, the leader suffers at home. The public commitment imports domestic audience costs into the international calculation. Another mechanism is **burning bridges**: physically removing the option to retreat (the historical practice of burning one's own ships or bridges forces an army to fight rather than flee, making commitment observable). Modern equivalents include irrevocable treaty commitments, the stationing of tripwire forces too small to fight but whose deaths would trigger escalation, or the automatic economic sanctions that activate if an agreement is broken.

It is crucial to understand the precise condition that makes a signal credible. The signal must impose a cost that is asymmetric between the resolved and the unresolved. A state with enormous resources can afford a deployment, a sanction, or a public statement even if it has no intention of following through — if the cost is proportionally tiny. What matters is the relationship between the cost of the signal and the state's **reservation value** — what it actually gains from bluffing successfully compared to what it loses by being caught. Rich, powerful states face a harder credibility problem in some ways, precisely because they can more easily absorb the costs of the signals that smaller states cannot fake.

This framework illuminates a number of puzzles in international politics. Why do states sometimes make dramatic, irreversible public commitments that appear to box them in? Because that boxed-in quality is the source of credibility. Why do leaders pursue escalatory military buildups even when they believe war would be catastrophic? Partly because the buildup itself signals resolve in ways that would otherwise require actual war to demonstrate. And why does deterrence sometimes fail despite expensive military demonstrations? Because clever adversaries can find ways to interpret signals as insufficient — and because domestic pressures sometimes force leaders to signal even when they lack the resolve to follow through, producing the cheap-talk problem in a new form.
