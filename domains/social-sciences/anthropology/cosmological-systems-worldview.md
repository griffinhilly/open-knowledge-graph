# Cosmological Systems and Worldviews

## Core Idea
A cosmology is a culture's comprehensive system of understanding the nature of reality, the universe, deities or spirits, and humanity's place within it. Studying cosmologies reveals how different cultures organize fundamental categories of being, time, space, causality, and morality, demonstrating that scientific worldviews are themselves culturally particular cosmologies.

## Explainer

From your work on myth and symbolism, you learned that myths are not simply false stories but structured narratives that do real cultural work — organizing values, explaining origins, and mapping the relationship between humans and the sacred. A **cosmological system** is the broader framework within which myths operate. Where a myth tells a story, a cosmology provides the entire stage on which that story unfolds: it specifies what kinds of things exist, how time flows, what forces govern the universe, and what role humans are meant to play. Every culture, including modern scientific ones, operates within such a framework.

A useful way into this concept is to compare how cultures organize **time**. Many Indigenous traditions hold a cyclical cosmology in which time spirals through seasons, generations, and cosmic epochs — the past is not gone but continuously present, and the future recapitulates what has come before. The Western scientific cosmology holds a linear time arrow moving from the Big Bang forward. Judeo-Christian cosmology holds a narrative arc from creation through fall, redemption, and apocalypse. These are not merely different stories; they organize different practical realities. In a cyclical cosmology, ancestors remain relevant agents. In a linear scientific cosmology, historical progress is the structuring value. The temporal assumption shapes what counts as meaningful action.

Cosmologies also organize **causality** — why things happen. A naturalistic cosmology attributes events to physical causes, statistical regularities, and emergent properties. An animistic cosmology attributes events to the intentions of spirits or ancestors. Many cosmologies layer both: illness may be explained simultaneously by a physical mechanism (bacteria) and a social cause (a broken relationship with ancestors or a breach of taboo). **Anthropologists call this the "why me?" problem**: when bad things happen, people everywhere want not just the proximate cause but the moral or cosmic reason it happened to them specifically. Different cosmologies provide different answers, and the answers have different practical implications for healing, justice, and social order.

Understanding cosmologies comparatively requires the discipline your prerequisite study began: **cultural relativism** in method, not in ethics. The anthropologist's task is to understand how a cosmological system makes sense from within — what it explains, what problems it solves, what it makes thinkable — before evaluating it externally. This is where the study of cosmology connects to epistemology: every cosmology defines what counts as valid evidence, who can interpret signs from the cosmos, and what explanations close rather than open further inquiry. The history of science itself can be read as a series of cosmological revisions — Copernican, Newtonian, Einsteinian — each reorganizing the fundamental categories of space, time, matter, and causality.

## Questions

```yaml
- question: "Why do anthropologists say that modern scientific worldviews are 'themselves culturally particular cosmologies'?"
  type: short-answer
  answer: "Because science also depends on culturally specific assumptions about what exists, how causality works, what counts as evidence, and what questions are worth asking. These assumptions are not universal but historically situated and shaped by cultural context."
  explanation: "This does not mean science is arbitrary or equivalent to magic — it means that the framework within which science operates (linear time, materialism, empirical evidence) is a cosmological commitment, not a neutral default. Recognizing this is what allows genuinely comparative study of knowledge systems."

- question: "Two explanations are offered for a child's illness: 'a bacterial infection' and 'the family violated a ritual prohibition.' Are these necessarily in conflict?"
  type: short-answer
  answer: "Not necessarily. In many cosmological systems, both explanations operate at different levels — the bacteria explain the proximate mechanism, but the cosmological explanation addresses why this child got sick, why now, and what must be done to restore social and spiritual order."
  explanation: "The 'why me?' problem is a different question from 'what caused this?' Anthropologists call this the distinction between proximate and ultimate explanation. Many people worldwide hold both simultaneously without contradiction, since they operate at different levels of the causal hierarchy."
```
