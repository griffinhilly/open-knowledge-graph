# Cross-Cultural Comparison and Typologies

## Core Idea
Systematic cross-cultural comparison identifies patterns, variations, and possible universals across societies by developing comparable analytical categories and examining multiple cases. This method moves beyond single ethnographies to test hypotheses and build generalizations about human social organization. Typologies—systematic classifications of cultural patterns like descent systems or marriage rules—are essential tools for comparison.

## How It's Best Learned
Use comparative databases or classic typologies (descent systems: bilateral, patrilineal, matrilineal) to examine variation and identify correlations with other features (residence patterns, inheritance, kinship terminology).

## Common Misconceptions
- Assuming that shared typological categories create equivalent cultural meanings.
- Treating comparison as value-neutral when categories are culturally constructed.
- Losing the specificity and context of individual cases in pursuit of comparison.

## Explainer

A single ethnography tells you what one society looks like from the inside — rich, contextual, specific. But it cannot tell you whether what you observed is universal, regionally common, or unique. Cross-cultural comparison is the method that moves anthropology from description to explanation by examining variation across many cases. The logic is similar to controlled comparison in natural science: if you want to know whether a feature of social life (say, matrilineal descent) tends to co-occur with another feature (say, particular residence patterns), you need more than one case to test the hypothesis.

**Typologies** are the classification tools that make comparison possible. A typology groups societies or practices into categories based on shared structural features, ignoring surface differences. Descent systems, for example, can be classified as **patrilineal** (descent traced through fathers), **matrilineal** (through mothers), or **bilateral** (through both equally). Marriage rules can be classified as **exogamous** (must marry outside one's group) or **endogamous** (must marry within). Once societies are categorized, you can ask: do patrilineal descent societies tend to have particular residence patterns? Do exogamy rules correlate with particular kinship terminologies? Large databases like the Human Relations Area Files (HRAF) make these comparisons possible across hundreds of societies.

The methodological challenge is that typological categories are always constructed by researchers working from their own cultural vantage point. When nineteenth-century anthropologists classified kinship systems, they built from European categories and often misrepresented what they found. A **typological category** groups cases by a feature that looks similar from outside, but that similarity may mask very different local meanings. Calling two societies "patrilineal" tells you something about their formal rule of descent but nothing about whether women have power, what rights children have, or how people emotionally experience kinship. This is the core tension: comparison requires abstraction, but abstraction strips context.

The **Galton problem** — named after Francis Galton, who raised it at an 1889 meeting — is a specific methodological threat in cross-cultural comparison. If societies are not independent because they share common ancestry or have borrowed from each other through diffusion, then finding the same feature in many societies does not provide independent confirmation of a hypothesis. A hundred societies that all practice the same rain ritual might be doing so because they all descended from the same ancestral population or had extensive trade contact, not because there's a universal pressure that produces that ritual. Comparative anthropologists use techniques like geographic controls and phylogenetic methods to address this.

Contemporary cross-cultural comparison has moved beyond seeking universal laws toward more modest goals: identifying patterns of co-variation, testing specific causal claims, and mapping the range of human variation. The value is not finding what all humans share (though that matters) but charting what different structural configurations are actually possible and how common each is. Typologies, for all their limitations, remain indispensable — they are the vocabulary that allows comparison to happen at all, provided the analyst remembers that every category is an analytical tool, not a natural kind found in the world.
