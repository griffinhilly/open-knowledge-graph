# Newton's Laws of Motion and Universal Gravitation

## Core Idea
Isaac Newton synthesized Galilean mechanics and Keplerian astronomy into a unified mathematical system with three laws of motion and a law of universal gravitation. Newtonian physics explained both terrestrial and celestial phenomena through mathematical laws, becoming the model for scientific explanation and establishing the authority of mathematics in natural philosophy.

## Explainer

You already know Kepler's three laws: planets move in ellipses with the sun at one focus, sweep equal areas in equal times, and have orbital periods proportional to the 3/2 power of their semi-major axes. Kepler's laws described the planetary orbits with impressive precision — but he could not explain *why* orbits took those shapes. That was Newton's achievement. Newton's *Principia Mathematica* (1687) showed that Kepler's laws were mathematical consequences of two more fundamental claims: his three laws of motion and the law of **universal gravitation**.

Newton's three laws build on each other. The **first law** (inertia) states that a body continues in its state of rest or uniform motion unless acted upon by a net force. This was genuinely radical: Aristotelian physics held that motion required a continuous cause. Newton inverted this — rest and uniform motion are the natural state; only *changes* in motion require explanation. The **second law** (F = ma) quantifies how forces change motion: force equals mass times acceleration. The **third law** (action-reaction) states that every force has an equal and opposite counterpart — the Earth pulls you down, and you pull the Earth up, with exactly equal force, just negligible effect given the mass ratio.

The law of **universal gravitation** states that every mass attracts every other mass with a force proportional to the product of their masses and inversely proportional to the square of the distance between them. The crucial word is "universal": the same force that makes an apple fall governs the Moon's orbit. Newton demonstrated that a Moon falling toward Earth at the rate predicted by the inverse-square law would trace exactly the elliptical path Kepler had observed. Terrestrial and celestial mechanics, previously treated as entirely different domains, were unified under a single mathematical relationship.

The historical significance goes beyond the physics. Newton's system gave natural philosophers a template: express natural laws as mathematical equations, derive predictions, test them. This **mechanistic-mathematical program** became the model for scientific explanation across subsequent centuries — it is what people mean when they invoke "Newtonian science" as a cultural ideal. For Enlightenment thinkers, the Newtonian achievement proved that the universe operated by discoverable rational laws, that human reason could penetrate nature's workings, and that the same methods might be applied to society, economics, and morality. Newton's physics was not just an achievement in astronomy; it was the intellectual cornerstone of a worldview.
