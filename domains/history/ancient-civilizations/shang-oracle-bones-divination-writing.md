# Shang Oracle Bones, Divination, and Early Chinese Writing

## Core Idea
Shang rulers used oracle bones (ox scapulae and turtle shells) for divination, inscribing questions and results in proto-Chinese script. These artifacts provide the earliest evidence of Chinese writing and reveal how divination guided decisions about warfare, harvests, and governance, demonstrating the integration of religious practice and state administration.

## How It's Best Learned
Study photographs and rubbings of oracle bones to observe inscriptions and understand the divination process. Compare oracle-bone script with later Chinese writing to trace linguistic evolution.

## Common Misconceptions
Oracle bones were not random divination—they followed systematic procedures (heating bones until they cracked, reading the patterns) and recorded specific outcomes, creating a historical record.

## Explainer

If you have studied Shang bronze vessels and ritual objects, you already know that the Shang state organized itself around elaborate ceremonial life — that bronze was reserved for ritual purposes, and that access to correct ritual was itself a form of political authority. Oracle bones extend this logic into the realm of writing. The king did not simply command; he consulted. Before a military campaign or a harvest ceremony, court diviners applied heat to the shoulder blade of an ox or the plastron of a turtle until the bone cracked. The pattern of cracks was then interpreted as a divine response. What makes oracle bones extraordinary is that someone then **inscribed** the question asked, the crack-reading, and often the actual outcome — turning an ephemeral act of divination into a written record.

Those inscriptions are the earliest confirmed examples of **Chinese writing**, and the script is recognizably ancestral to modern Chinese characters. Many oracle-bone graphs are pictographic: the graph for "sun" looks like a circle with a dot, "moon" like a crescent. But even at this early stage the script was phonetic as well as pictographic — some signs represented sounds rather than images, which is a feature of mature writing systems. The fact that a sophisticated phonographic-pictographic hybrid existed by the late Shang period (roughly 1250–1046 BCE) tells us this writing system had already undergone centuries of development before the surviving inscriptions.

The content of the inscriptions reveals how thoroughly divination was integrated into **state administration**. The questions recorded fall into predictable categories: Will this military expedition succeed? Will the next ten days be without disaster? Will the harvest be good? Will the queen give birth safely? This is not private mysticism — it is institutional decision-making. Every significant action required supernatural validation, and the king was the pivotal intermediary between the human and divine realms. By monopolizing access to divination, the Shang court concentrated political and religious authority in the same hands, a pattern visible in many early states.

For historians, oracle bones are an unusually direct window into Shang royal concerns because the inscriptions were never intended as public records — they were ritual objects, buried or stored. This distinguishes them from the propaganda of monumental inscriptions in other civilizations. When a divination entry notes that the campaign succeeded "as predicted," or that the harvest "was not good," those are candid administrative notes, not celebratory proclamations. Combined with your knowledge of Shang bronze ritual — which shows elite control over sacred objects — oracle bones complete the picture of a state in which religious authority and political power were not separate spheres but a single system.
