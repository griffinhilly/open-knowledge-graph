# Ancient Northeast Africa: The Kingdoms of Kush and Aksum

## Core Idea
Kush, in Nubia along the middle Nile in today's Sudan, was a powerful African kingdom that traded and fought with Egypt for centuries. In the 700s BCE its kings conquered Egypt and ruled it as the Twenty-Fifth Dynasty, and later, from its capital at Meroë, Kush became a center of iron working and built its own pyramids. Aksum, in today's northern Ethiopia and Eritrea, rose in the first centuries CE and grew rich from Red Sea trade between the Mediterranean, Arabia and India. Under King Ezana in the 300s CE, Aksum became one of the first states to adopt Christianity.

## How It's Best Learned
Place both kingdoms on a map of the Nile and the Red Sea, and trace the goods that moved through them, such as gold, ivory and incense. Compare a Kushite pyramid with an Egyptian one to discuss borrowing and adaptation. Use Aksumite coins as primary sources to show how a ruler announced his power and his faith.

## Common Misconceptions
- Kush was simply an outpost of Egyptian civilization. (Kush borrowed from Egypt, but it had its own rulers, gods, Meroitic script and traditions, and for about a century its kings ruled Egypt itself.)
- Christianity reached Africa only through European missionaries in modern times. (Aksum's royal court adopted Christianity in the 300s CE, and Ethiopia has had a Christian church ever since.)
- Ancient African kingdoms were cut off from the wider world. (Aksum minted its own coins and sat on trade routes that linked the Roman Empire, Arabia and India.)

## Explainer

When you studied ancient Egypt, Nubia appeared mostly as a land to the south that supplied gold and that Egyptian armies conquered during the New Kingdom. Seen from Nubia, the story looks different. The kingdom of Kush, centered on the Nile in what is now Sudan, was a strong state with its own rulers and gods.

As Egyptian power weakened, Kush grew. In the 700s BCE the Kushite king Piye marched north and took control of Egypt. He and his successors, including Taharqa, ruled as pharaohs of the Twenty-Fifth Dynasty and restored old temples. In the 600s BCE the Assyrians invaded Egypt and pushed the Kushites back south. Kush did not collapse. Its royal center later moved to Meroë, farther up the Nile. The region had iron ore and wood, and Meroë became a major center of iron working. Its rulers, including powerful queens, were buried under steep, narrow pyramids. The Kushites also wrote their own language in a Meroitic script. Scholars can read the sounds of the script, but much of the language is still not understood. Kush declined in the 300s CE.

To the southeast, on the highlands of today's northern Ethiopia and Eritrea, Aksum rose in the first centuries CE. Its strength came from trade. Through the Red Sea port of Adulis, Aksum sent ivory, gold and other goods to Roman Egypt, Arabia and India, and it imported cloth, metal goods and wine. Aksum minted its own coins in gold, silver and bronze, many with Greek inscriptions, which shows how closely it was tied to Mediterranean trade. Its rulers raised tall stone stelae over royal tombs. A third-century text attributed to the Persian prophet Mani ranked Aksum among the four great kingdoms of the world.

In the 300s CE King Ezana adopted Christianity, guided by Frumentius, a Christian from the Roman world. His later coins replace older symbols with the cross. Christianity remained the faith of the Ethiopian state for many centuries, and its church used the Ge'ez language and script. In the early 600s, Aksum's king sheltered early followers of Muhammad who fled persecution in Mecca. Aksum declined in the following centuries as Red Sea trade routes changed.
