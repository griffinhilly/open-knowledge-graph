# Magna Carta and Constitutional Limits on Power

## Core Idea
Magna Carta (1215) was a feudal charter extracted from King John of England by rebellious barons, establishing the principle that even the king was subject to the law and could not deprive free men of rights without lawful judgment. Though originally a narrow document protecting baronial privileges, it became over centuries a foundational symbol of constitutional government and rule of law. Magna Carta exemplifies how documents acquire meaning far beyond their original intent through subsequent reinterpretation and invocation.

## How It's Best Learned
Comparing the original 1215 text (with its largely feudal, aristocratic concerns) to later invocations in the English Civil War, American Revolution, and modern human rights discourse illustrates how historical documents are reinterpreted to serve new purposes. This is an excellent case study in the history of ideas.

## Common Misconceptions
- Magna Carta did not guarantee rights to all English people — it initially protected the liberties of barons and the Church, with limited application to ordinary subjects.
- Its celebrated status as the foundation of constitutional government is largely a post-hoc construction, especially by 17th-century English lawyers and later American revolutionaries.
