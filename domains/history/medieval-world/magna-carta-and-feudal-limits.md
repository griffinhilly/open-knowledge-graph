# Magna Carta and Constitutional Limits on Power

## Core Idea
Magna Carta (1215) was a feudal charter extracted from King John of England by rebellious barons, establishing the principle that even the king was subject to the law and could not deprive free men of rights without lawful judgment. Though originally a narrow document protecting baronial privileges, it became over centuries a foundational symbol of constitutional government and rule of law. Magna Carta exemplifies how documents acquire meaning far beyond their original intent through subsequent reinterpretation and invocation.

## How It's Best Learned
Comparing the original 1215 text (with its largely feudal, aristocratic concerns) to later invocations in the English Civil War, American Revolution, and modern human rights discourse illustrates how historical documents are reinterpreted to serve new purposes. This is an excellent case study in the history of ideas.

## Common Misconceptions
- Magna Carta did not guarantee rights to all English people — it initially protected the liberties of barons and the Church, with limited application to ordinary subjects.
- Its celebrated status as the foundation of constitutional government is largely a post-hoc construction, especially by 17th-century English lawyers and later American revolutionaries.

## Explainer

To understand Magna Carta, start from what you already know about **feudalism**: the entire medieval political order rested on chains of personal loyalty and obligation, with land granted in exchange for military service and obedience. The king sat at the top of this pyramid, but he was not truly above it — he had made his own feudal promises to his barons, and those barons believed those promises were binding. Magna Carta was born from this logic. When King John repeatedly violated feudal custom — seizing lands, imposing irregular taxes, and ignoring customary legal processes — his barons did not frame their rebellion as revolution. They framed it as the king living up to his feudal obligations.

The 1215 document itself is strikingly narrow. Most of its 63 clauses protect specific baronial privileges: inheritance rights, limits on arbitrary taxation, restrictions on the king's misuse of wardship and marriage rights over noble heirs. The famous Clause 39 — "no free man shall be seized, imprisoned, or stripped of his rights except by the lawful judgment of his equals and the law of the land" — sounds universal but in context meant only free men, a small minority of England's largely unfree peasant population. There was no intent to create popular democracy. The barons were protecting themselves, not serfs.

What transformed Magna Carta from a feudal contract into a constitutional icon was **reinterpretation across centuries**. In the 17th century, common-law lawyers like Edward Coke invoked it against the Stuart kings, arguing it demonstrated that English law had always constrained royal power. Parliamentarians used it to justify resistance to Charles I. American colonists cited it to argue that "taxation without representation" violated ancient liberties. By this point, the document's actual 1215 meaning was almost irrelevant — what mattered was the idea it had come to represent: that sovereign power is not absolute, and that even rulers must answer to law.

This pattern — a document acquiring meaning far beyond its original intent — is crucial for understanding constitutional history. The U.S. Constitution's own history works similarly: later courts, legislators, and social movements have invoked its clauses to establish rights that the 18th-century framers never contemplated. The lesson from Magna Carta is not that 1215 barons were proto-democrats. It is that **foundational documents are living political tools**, continually reinterpreted by later actors to serve new purposes. Their power lies not in what they originally meant but in what they are made to mean over time.
