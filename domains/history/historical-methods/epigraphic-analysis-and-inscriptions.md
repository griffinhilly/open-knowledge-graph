# Epigraphic Analysis and Inscriptions

## Core Idea
Inscriptions carved in stone, metal, or clay preserve official records, commemorations, and everyday writing. Epigraphic analysis examines letter forms, abbreviations, language, grammar, and context to extract information about politics, religion, commerce, and literacy rates that manuscripts alone cannot provide.

Inscriptions capture commercial transactions, everyday language, legal arrangements, funerary customs, and the names and lives of people who were invisible to literary tradition. Pompeian graffiti recording election endorsements, bar tabs, and gladiatorial scores is historical evidence that no amount of literary reading can replicate. The absence of 'narrative context' is precisely what makes inscriptions valuable — they were written for specific functional purposes, not curated for posterity."

## Explainer

Inscriptions survive when manuscripts don't. Stone is nearly indestructible; papyrus and parchment rot, burn, and get discarded. The result is a paradox: for many ancient and medieval societies, the most abundant written evidence is not literary texts or administrative records but inscriptions—carved dedications, boundary markers, funerary epitaphs, legal decrees, and graffiti scratched into walls. **Epigraphy** is the discipline that reads and interprets this material, and it draws on your prerequisite skills in artifact examination and paleography to do what manuscript historians cannot: recover writing that was never copied, never curated, and never filtered through a scribal tradition.

The most fundamental skill in epigraphic analysis is identifying **letter forms**—the shapes, strokes, and proportions of individual characters. Letter forms changed over time and varied by region, so a trained epigraphist can date an undated inscription to within a generation based on how its letters look, using the same logic a paleographer applies to handwriting. A Roman inscription with tall, thin letters and serifs characteristic of the second century CE can be distinguished from one with the rounder, heavier forms common in late antiquity, even without any internal date. Combine letter-form dating with **archaeological context**—where the inscription was found, what it was built into, what other materials were associated with it—and you can often narrow a date considerably.

**Abbreviations** are the next challenge. Ancient inscriptions, especially official Latin and Greek texts, used abbreviated formulaic language that was instantly recognizable to contemporary readers but opaque without a working knowledge of the conventions. A Roman funerary inscription reading *D.M. / L. VALERIO FELICI / ANN. XXXV* expands to *Dis Manibus / Lucio Valerio Felici / Annorum XXXV*—"To the Divine Shades, to Lucius Valerius Felix, aged 35." Knowing the formulas lets you extract the content; the abbreviations themselves tell you something about literacy and shared convention among the people who produced and read such inscriptions.

What makes epigraphy particularly valuable is the kinds of information it preserves that manuscripts rarely contain. Funerary epitaphs record the ages, occupations, family structures, and freedman status of ordinary people whose names appear nowhere in literary sources. **Graffiti** from Pompeii record election endorsements, gladiatorial scores, love declarations, and bar tabs—the texture of everyday life. Commercial inscriptions on pottery, amphorae, and weights record producers, trade routes, and price standards. Boundary stones and property records preserve legal and economic arrangements. **Honorific inscriptions**—the plaques thanking benefactors for funding public buildings or games—reveal patterns of civic patronage and the social expectations surrounding wealth. None of this appears in the literary tradition because it was never literary. The inscriptions were written for contemporaries in specific functional contexts, which is precisely why they are so revealing about the social realities that elite literature took for granted and did not bother to explain.
