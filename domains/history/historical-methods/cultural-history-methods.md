# Cultural History and Meaning-Making Approaches

## Core Idea
Cultural history examines how people made meaning through beliefs, practices, rituals, arts, and representations rather than focusing primarily on structures or political events. This approach treats culture as the realm where meaning is created and contested. Understanding cultural practices—from religious rituals to material consumption to artistic production—reveals historical consciousness and social organization.

    - "Using multiple layers of archival sources to triangulate the accuracy of historical accounts"
  answer: 1
  explanation: "Thick description is Geertz's term for interpretive analysis that goes beyond recording the bare behavioral fact ('thin description': the man winked) to explaining what it means within its cultural context (he gave a conspiratorial wink to mock a friend's performance). The best cultural history treats meaning-making as always social, always partial, and always contested rather than presenting a single coherent 'mentalité' of a period."

  type: short-answer

A cultural historian asks how people made sense of their world — what they believed mattered, how they understood suffering and hierarchy, what rituals and representations organized their experience. Both might study the same period, but one measures and structures it while the other reads and interprets it. ## Explainer

Cultural history emerged as a distinctive approach partly in reaction to social and economic history's focus on structures, statistics, and material conditions. Social history asked: what were the demographic patterns, labor conditions, and class formations of a given society? Cultural history asks a different question: how did people in that society understand their world — what did they believe mattered, what rituals structured their experience, how did they make sense of suffering, hierarchy, and change? You can think of the difference as the gap between measuring a society and reading it.

The core methodological move in cultural history is treating **cultural practices as texts** to be interpreted. A religious procession, a carnival, a pattern of courtship, an act of popular violence — none of these are simply events to record. They are performances through which people expressed, negotiated, and challenged meanings. This approach draws heavily on anthropology, particularly the work of Clifford Geertz, whose concept of **thick description** — interpreting actions by placing them within their entire web of meanings — became a touchstone for cultural historians. Natalie Zemon Davis, whose work you may know, exemplified this by studying the meanings embedded in popular rituals like charivari (community shaming ceremonies) and in extraordinary events like the case of Martin Guerre to illuminate broader cultural logics.

**Material culture analysis** — your soft prerequisite — feeds directly into this method. Objects are not inert; they carry meaning, indicate status, and encode beliefs. A medieval reliquary is not just a container for bones; it is a material node in a system of beliefs about sanctity, intercession, and the power of holy remains. A Victorian parlor is not just furniture; it is a staged performance of respectability and gender norms. Cultural historians learn to read objects with the same attention they give texts: asking who made it, who used it, what it communicated, and what it assumed about the world.

The approach has productive tensions. One is the risk of **over-reading**: cultural history requires interpretation, and interpretation can drift from the evidence toward the historian's own frameworks. A second tension is between **elite and popular culture**: whose meanings are we recovering? The literate leave more traces, but cultural historians have developed techniques for accessing the meanings of non-literate people through records of court proceedings, sermons, images, and material remains. A third tension is between **coherence and contestation**: cultural history sometimes risks presenting a culture as a unified whole when in reality cultures are sites of conflict, where different groups fight over what things mean. The best cultural history holds these tensions consciously, treating meaning-making as always social, always partial, and always contested.
