# Understanding Author Perspective and Historical Position

## Core Idea
Every source was created by someone situated in a particular time, place, and social position. Understanding who wrote something, when, where, and why—and what they stood to gain or lose by telling their version—is essential to using sources as evidence. Author position shapes not just what they chose to include, but what they took for granted or could not see.

## How It's Best Learned
Take a single source and build a profile of its author: their social class, region, gender, political allegiances, financial interests, education, and beliefs. Identify how each aspect shaped what they wrote. Compare your analysis with a source from someone in a very different position discussing similar events.

## Questions

```yaml
- question: "A colonial administrator's report describes an indigenous rebellion as a 'minor disturbance caused by irrational resistance to progress.' How should a historian best use this source?"
  type: multiple-choice
  options:
    - "Discard it — the author's bias makes it unreliable as evidence"
    - "Accept the description literally, since officials had the best access to information"
    - "Use it to understand the administrator's perspective and interests, while recognizing what it cannot tell you about indigenous motivations and experience"
    - "Extract only quantitative data (troop numbers, dates) and ignore the narrative framing"
  answer: 2
  explanation: "A biased source is not a useless source — it is a partial source. The administrator's report is invaluable evidence of how colonial authorities framed indigenous resistance, what anxieties it provoked, and what information the administration had access to. But it cannot substitute for sources closer to indigenous experience. The historian's job is to understand both what the source can show and what its author's position prevents it from showing. Discarding biased sources would eliminate most of the historical record."

- question: "A factory owner and a factory worker both write accounts of the same labor dispute. Their accounts differ significantly. What does the concept of 'author position' PRIMARILY explain about this difference?"
  type: multiple-choice
  options:
    - "One of them is lying and the other is telling the truth"
    - "The difference in their formal education produced different writing abilities"
    - "Each author had different access to events and different interests shaping what they emphasized — both accounts are partial in different ways"
    - "The more detailed account is always more reliable"
  answer: 2
  explanation: "Author position explains why two honest observers of the same events can produce radically different accounts. The factory owner had access to financial records, management deliberations, and legal communications the worker never saw — and had interests in portraying the dispute as orderly, manageable, and economically unjustified. The worker had access to shop-floor conditions, worker sentiment, and daily experience the owner never witnessed — and had interests in emphasizing grievances and solidarity. Neither account is simply 'lying'; both are partial in structured, explicable ways."

- question: "An author's silence about something — what they choose not to mention or take for granted — can be as historically revealing as what they explicitly state."
  type: true-false
  answer: true
  explanation: "What an author doesn't say is often the most valuable historical evidence. A medieval chronicler who never describes what peasants ate wasn't being evasive — that information was so obvious to contemporary readers that it required no comment. These silences reveal shared assumptions, taken-for-granted realities, and the invisible background of a society. A soldier's letter that doesn't mention fear, a merchant's account that ignores the enslaved workers in the supply chain, a political speech that doesn't acknowledge women as citizens — each silence is a window into the author's social position and the limits of what could be said or imagined."

- question: "A source written by someone with a clear personal interest in the outcome — such as a general writing a military memoir — is automatically unreliable and should be excluded from historical analysis."
  type: true-false
  answer: false
  explanation: "Interested sources are partial, not worthless. The general's memoir may be self-serving, but it is invaluable evidence of how decisions were made, what information commanders had, how they understood their own choices, and how they wanted posterity to remember them. Excluding all interested sources would eliminate most of the historical record, since almost every document was produced by someone with a stake in events. The historian's task is not to find 'unbiased' sources (they don't exist) but to read partial sources critically, triangulate across sources with different positions, and understand what each position reveals and conceals."

- question: "Explain the difference between an author's perspective and their bias. Why does the distinction matter for historical analysis?"
  type: short-answer
  answer: "Perspective refers to what an author could see or access based on their social position — a sailor knew Atlantic wind patterns a landlocked merchant did not; a palace official witnessed royal deliberations a peasant could not. Bias refers to the direction their interests or beliefs pushed their account — what they chose to emphasize, downplay, or frame in self-serving ways. The distinction matters because perspective shapes what evidence a source contains (and cannot contain), while bias shapes how that evidence is presented. A source can have valuable perspective on events while also being biased in its interpretation — a historian must analyze both to use it correctly."
  explanation: "Conflating perspective and bias leads to two errors: dismissing sources as biased when they contain irreplaceable perspective, or trusting sources because they seem 'objective' without examining what their position prevented them from seeing. An indigenous leader's account of colonization has perspective the colonizer lacked, and may also have bias toward emphasizing agency and resistance. A colonial administrator's report has perspective on administrative decisions the colonized couldn't access, and bias toward minimizing resistance and justifying policy. Analyzing both dimensions of each source, then triangulating across sources with different positions, is the core practice of historical method."
```

## Explainer

Your work on source credibility has already taught you to ask: is this source accurate? Author perspective and position asks a deeper question: *why* was it written the way it was, by this person, at this moment? Every source is simultaneously a window (showing you events or conditions) and a mirror (reflecting the author's position in the world). Becoming a skilled historian means learning to use both views at once.

**Positionality** — where an author stands in social space — shapes historical records in at least three ways. First, it determines *access*: a palace official had access to royal deliberations that a peasant did not; a sailor knew the Atlantic wind patterns that a landlocked merchant only heard second-hand. Second, position shapes *interests*: a colonial administrator writing about indigenous resistance had reasons to minimize it or frame it as irrational; an indigenous leader recounting the same events had reasons to emphasize injustice and agency. Third — and most subtle — position shapes what an author could not see or thought too obvious to mention. A medieval chronicler writing about famine rarely describes what people ate, because everyone already knew. What is taken for granted is often invisible to the writer, and therefore hardest to recover.

The key skill is distinguishing **perspective** (what the author could see or experience) from **bias** (the direction their interests or beliefs pushed their account). These are related but not identical. A working-class diarist has access to shop floor conditions a factory owner lacks — that is perspective. But the same diarist may overestimate how widely their local conditions applied — that is a limitation of perspective shading into distortion. A government census official has access to systematic data no individual could assemble — perspective — but may classify people into categories that serve administrative needs rather than social reality — bias. Neither makes the source useless; both make it partial and require compensatory reading.

In practice, interrogating author position means asking: What did this author stand to gain or lose by telling this version? Who was their intended audience, and what did that audience expect or want to hear? What would have been dangerous, embarrassing, or simply unthinkable for them to say? What are they conspicuously silent about? These questions do not lead to the conclusion that all sources are equally unreliable — they lead to a richer understanding of what each source can and cannot tell you, and they prepare you to triangulate across sources with different positions to reconstruct events more fully than any single account could provide.
