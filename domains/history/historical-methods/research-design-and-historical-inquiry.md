# Designing and Planning Historical Research Projects

## Core Idea
Good historical research begins with a clear research question, not a vague topic. The question shapes which sources you seek, how you interpret them, and what counts as evidence. Research design involves identifying what you need to know, where you're likely to find it, potential obstacles, and how you'll handle contradictory evidence.

## Questions

```yaml
- question: "A history student says: 'I'm going to research the Black Death in medieval Europe.' A historian says that is a topic, not a research question. Which of the following is the clearest example of a genuine research question?"
  type: multiple-choice
  options:
    - "I will examine how the Black Death affected European populations between 1347 and 1353"
    - "Was the collapse of manorial labor systems in England more a consequence of demographic loss from the Black Death or of pre-existing peasant organizing in the 1340s?"
    - "I will focus my research on England, France, and Italy to keep the scope manageable"
    - "What caused the Black Death, and where did it originate?"
  answer: 1
  explanation: "A genuine research question is answerable, falsifiable, significant, and scoped — you can imagine what evidence would support or undermine it. Option B specifies a comparison, identifies the relevant historical actors, and names what would count as an answer. Options A and C are scoping decisions, not questions. Option D gestures at a question but remains too broad ('what caused X') and the two parts ('caused' and 'where') are different questions. The specific question is what determines which sources matter and what a satisfying answer looks like."

- question: "A historian discovers two contemporary chronicles that directly contradict each other about the sequence of events at a key battle. The most rigorous response is to:"
  type: multiple-choice
  options:
    - "Use whichever chronicle better supports the historian's initial thesis, since one source must be wrong"
    - "Dismiss both chronicles and rely exclusively on later secondary sources written with more distance"
    - "Evaluate each chronicle's reliability independently, look for patterns in who generated the conflicting accounts and why, and be willing to revise the research question in light of the contradiction"
    - "Average the two accounts to arrive at a probable middle-ground version of events"
  answer: 2
  explanation: "Contradictory evidence is an opportunity, not an obstacle. The rigorous response is to treat the contradiction as data: Who wrote each chronicle? Under what circumstances? With what interests? Do they disagree on facts or on interpretation? What other sources might break the tie? This approach tests the question rather than proving a predetermined answer. Option A confirms bias. Option B discards the best-positioned eyewitness evidence. Option D literally averages narratives — a category error, since conflicting accounts don't split differences like physical measurements."

- question: "A good historical research question must be falsifiable — you should be able to imagine what evidence would lead you to a different conclusion than the one you currently expect."
  type: true-false
  answer: true
  explanation: "Falsifiability is essential because it is what separates a genuine historical question from an unfalsifiable narrative. If no possible evidence could change your conclusion, you are not doing research — you are illustrating a belief. Falsifiability also tells you what sources to look for: those that might challenge the hypothesis, not just those that support it. Research designed to find confirming evidence only will always find it, producing confident conclusions unsupported by any real test."

- question: "Acknowledging that key sources are lost or inaccessible is a weakness in a historical research project because it exposes gaps that undermine the conclusion."
  type: true-false
  answer: false
  explanation: "Explicit gap analysis is a mark of rigor, not weakness. It shows the historian knows what evidence would be needed to strengthen or challenge the argument and is being honest about what the existing evidence can and cannot establish. Historians who pretend their evidence is complete, when it is not, make stronger-sounding claims that are actually less trustworthy. Acknowledging that certain records were destroyed, are in restricted archives, or were never created forces the conclusions to match what the surviving evidence actually supports."

- question: "Why is the research question — rather than the choice of archives or sources — the most important design decision in a historical research project?"
  type: short-answer
  answer: "The research question determines everything else: which sources are relevant, what counts as evidence for or against the answer, how to weight contradictory accounts, and what a satisfying conclusion looks like. Without a specific, answerable, falsifiable question, there is no principle for deciding which sources matter, which archives to visit, or when the research is done. A historian who starts by accumulating sources without a question risks writing whatever story those sources happen to tell — finding what is in the archive rather than testing a real historical claim."
  explanation: "The contrast with topic-based research is key. A student 'researching the French Revolution' has no criteria for relevance — everything could be relevant. A historian asking whether artisan economic grievances or political ideology drove popular mobilization in 1789 has a specific test: what would we expect to find in artisan records, guild documents, wage data, and political pamphlets if grievances dominated vs. if ideology dominated? The question makes the design tractable and the conclusion falsifiable."
```

## Explainer

You have already learned to classify sources as primary, secondary, or tertiary and to think about causal explanation in history. Research design is where those skills get organized into a systematic plan. The difference between a student who "researches the French Revolution" and a historian who "investigates whether economic grievances in the Parisian artisan class were more important than political ideology in motivating popular participation in 1789" is not a difference in subject matter — it is a difference in question structure. The specific question defines the project: it tells you which sources are relevant, what you need to compare, and what a satisfying answer looks like.

A good **research question** has several properties. It is answerable — there exists, at least in principle, evidence that could bear on it. It is falsifiable — you should be able to imagine what evidence would lead you to conclude the opposite. It is significant — a yes or no answer would matter for how we understand something historically. And it is appropriately scoped — it can be addressed within the practical constraints of the project. "Why did Rome fall?" is a topic; "Did the debasement of the third-century currency contribute to provincial loyalty collapse in the Danubian frontier by creating conditions of military pay crisis?" is closer to a question (though still very large). Learning to distinguish the two, and to sharpen vague topics into genuine questions, is the core design skill.

**Source planning** follows from the question. Different questions require different source types: a question about elite decision-making may be addressed through state documents and correspondence; a question about popular experience may require parish records, legal depositions, and material culture. Your source classification skills are now inputs to a strategic problem: given my question, what would I need to find, in what archives or repositories, subject to what access constraints? This planning stage also requires honest **gap analysis** — what sources would ideally exist but don't? Which questions are currently unanswerable because the evidence is lost or inaccessible? Acknowledging these limits is not a weakness; it is what separates rigorous scholarship from confident assertion.

The most important design decision is how you will handle **contradictory evidence**. You will always find sources that point in different directions. The naive approach is to report only what confirms your initial hypothesis; the more sophisticated approach is to design your project from the beginning to test the question rather than prove an answer. When evidence conflicts, you have several tools: evaluate the reliability of each source independently, look for patterns in who is generating conflicting accounts and why, distinguish between genuine disagreement about facts and disagreement about interpretation, and be willing to revise your question in light of what you find. Good research design is not a rigid plan executed mechanically — it is a framework flexible enough to accommodate surprise while disciplined enough to keep you from simply writing the story you wanted to write before you looked at the evidence.
