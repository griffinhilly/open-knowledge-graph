# Using Archaeological Evidence

## Core Idea
Historical inquiry often draws on archaeological evidence to supplement or challenge the documentary record, especially for periods and populations underrepresented in written sources. Archaeology recovers material remains through controlled excavation, recording the precise spatial and stratigraphic context of finds — context that, once destroyed, cannot be recovered. Historians who use archaeological evidence must understand basic concepts of stratigraphy (reading layers of soil as chronological sequence), typology (classifying objects to establish relative dates), and the distinction between what can be inferred from material remains and what cannot. The relationship between archaeology and history is collaborative but not always harmonious: material and documentary evidence sometimes contradict each other.

## How It's Best Learned
Study the archaeology of a specific historical site alongside its documentary record (e.g., Pompeii, colonial Jamestown, medieval Winchester) and identify three claims that each type of evidence supports but the other does not. Reflect on what the silences in each record type reveal.

## Common Misconceptions
- Archaeological evidence does not simply 'confirm' what documents say; it often complicates, challenges, or reveals dimensions of the past entirely absent from written records.
- Excavation is irreversible — archaeological context, once destroyed, is gone forever, which is why recording methodology is as important as what is found.

## Explainer

Your work with material culture analysis has prepared you to treat objects as historical evidence — artifacts that carry information about the people who made and used them. Archaeology extends that analysis by adding a crucial dimension: **context**. Where an object is found is as significant as what the object is. A Roman coin found in a Viking burial tells a fundamentally different story than the same coin found in a Roman market town. Stratigraphy is the conceptual framework that makes this possible.

**Stratigraphy** reads soil and deposit layers as a chronological sequence. The basic principle is simple: things on top are generally later than things below. When archaeologists excavate, they meticulously record which layer — which **stratum** — each find comes from, along with its precise horizontal and vertical position. This transforms the excavation from a treasure hunt into a forensic exercise: the physical relationship between objects, features, and layers is the evidence. The irreversibility that the Common Misconceptions section flags is a direct consequence — once you dig through a layer to reach what's below, the stratigraphic relationship is gone. You cannot re-excavate. This is why meticulous recording, including drawings, photographs, and written descriptions of each context, is not a bureaucratic formality but the core methodological commitment of archaeology.

**Typology** provides a second dating tool. Objects tend to change in predictable ways over time — pottery shapes, tool forms, architectural styles all evolve through recognizable sequences. By classifying objects against established typological sequences, archaeologists can assign relative dates even without written records. When stratigraphy and typology agree, confidence rises; when they conflict, the contradiction itself is evidence that demands explanation (perhaps objects were moved, deposited as heirlooms, or deliberately buried with older material).

The relationship between archaeological and documentary evidence is best understood as **corroboration with tension**. Sometimes they align: excavations of medieval English towns generally confirm documentary accounts of their layouts and industries. But archaeology regularly reveals what documents conceal. Excavations of colonial Virginia plantations found enslaved quarters, food remains, and material culture that documentary sources — written almost entirely by enslavers — largely ignored. Archaeology of medieval peasant villages reveals life expectancy, diet, and household organization that literate elites never recorded. The principle you learned in corroboration and triangulation applies here: convergence across independent evidence types strengthens claims; divergence signals that at least one source is incomplete, biased, or misread. Material and documentary evidence are not rivals — they answer different questions, and the most powerful historical arguments typically use both.
