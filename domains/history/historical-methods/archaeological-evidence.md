# Using Archaeological Evidence

## Core Idea
Historical inquiry often draws on archaeological evidence to supplement or challenge the documentary record, especially for periods and populations underrepresented in written sources. Archaeology recovers material remains through controlled excavation, recording the precise spatial and stratigraphic context of finds — context that, once destroyed, cannot be recovered. Historians who use archaeological evidence must understand basic concepts of stratigraphy (reading layers of soil as chronological sequence), typology (classifying objects to establish relative dates), and the distinction between what can be inferred from material remains and what cannot. The relationship between archaeology and history is collaborative but not always harmonious: material and documentary evidence sometimes contradict each other.

## How It's Best Learned
Study the archaeology of a specific historical site alongside its documentary record (e.g., Pompeii, colonial Jamestown, medieval Winchester) and identify three claims that each type of evidence supports but the other does not. Reflect on what the silences in each record type reveal.

## Common Misconceptions
- Archaeological evidence does not simply 'confirm' what documents say; it often complicates, challenges, or reveals dimensions of the past entirely absent from written records.
- Excavation is irreversible — archaeological context, once destroyed, is gone forever, which is why recording methodology is as important as what is found.
