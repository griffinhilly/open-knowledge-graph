# The Factory System and Working-Class Conditions

## Core Idea
Factories concentrated production and workers in ways that were qualitatively different from craft production. Factory workers faced long hours (often 12-16 hours per day), dangerous conditions (machinery without guards), and low wages. They worked for factory owners, not on their own schedule, and faced harsh discipline for mistakes or insubordination. Factories employed women and children, who could be paid less than men, creating a gender-segmented labor force. Factory conditions were horrific by modern standards: injuries were common; diseases spread easily in crowded housing; few protections existed. Yet factories also concentrated workers, enabling collective organization: labor unions emerged to bargain for better conditions. The factory system created a clear class division between capital (owners) and labor (workers), and between management and workers. This clarity enabled political organization around class interests in ways that were less possible in craft or agricultural economies. The history of factory labor reveals both the costs of industrialization and how those costs generated resistance and eventually reform. Over the 19th and 20th centuries, labor movements succeeded in reducing hours, increasing wages, improving safety, and restricting child labor. Yet the tension between capital's interest in profit (achieved through low wages and minimal safety investment) and labor's interest in good conditions and decent pay remains fundamental. It also shows how globalization of production has shifted manufacturing to regions with weaker labor regulations.

Craft production: skilled artisans (weavers, cobblers, smiths) worked in their own workshops, owned their tools, controlled their pace and schedule, and produced complete goods from start to finish. - question: "What was the Luddite movement, and what were its members actually protesting?"
  type: short-answer
  answer: "The Luddites (1811-1816) were skilled textile workers -- primarily framework knitters and weavers in the English Midlands -- who destroyed machinery that was being used to undercut their wages and employment. The term is now used to mean anti-technology, but this is historically inaccurate: Luddites were not opposed to machinery as such; they were opposed to its use to displace skilled workers and reduce wages without compensation. They made fine distinctions -- destroying frames used to produce inferior goods that undermined quality craft standards while leaving other frames intact. They were protesting a specific use of technology under specific market conditions, not technology itself. The British government responded with military force and capital punishment (execution for machine-breaking made felony in 1812). Luddism was suppressed militarily but its underlying concerns -- who bears the costs of technological change -- remain live."
  explanation: "The misuse of 'Luddite' to mean anti-technology obscures the actual political content of the movement. E.P. Thompson's 'The Making of the English Working Class' (1963) rehabilitated Luddism as rational political action by workers defending their livelihoods."

- question: "Child labor in factories was primarily a result of poverty -- poor families needed children's income to survive."
  type: true-false
  answer: false
  explanation: "Poverty did create pressure for children to work; this is true. But factory child labor was also actively structured by employers: children were preferred for certain tasks (small hands in textile machinery; small bodies in coal mine shafts); children could be paid much less than adults; and children were easier to discipline than adult workers with community ties. Orphans and pauper children were bound as apprentices to factory masters with essentially no choice. The Factory Act of 1833 (restricting children under 9 from factory work and limiting hours for older children) passed over employer opposition -- if child labor were simply poverty relief, employers would have welcomed restrictions on their costs. Child labor was profitable for employers, not merely necessary for families."

## Explainer

The factory system -- concentrated production in large buildings with mechanized equipment, employing wage workers under direct supervision -- emerged in 18th-century Britain and spread globally in the 19th and 20th centuries. It transformed not only how goods were produced but how work was organized, how time was structured, and how social class was defined.

Before factories, production was organized in two main ways. In craft production, skilled artisans (weavers, shoemakers, clockmakers, printers) worked in their own workshops, owned their tools, controlled their pace, and produced complete goods from raw material to finished product. Their skill was their capital. In the putting-out system, merchant capitalists supplied raw materials to rural households, who processed them -- spinning, weaving, assembling components -- and returned finished goods for piece-rate payment. Capital owned materials; workers owned tools and controlled their time.

The factory centralized all of this under single ownership and direct supervision. Richard Arkwright's water-powered spinning mills (from 1771) required workers to come to the mill, work at the pace set by machinery, during hours set by the factory bell, doing a single repetitive task. Workers owned nothing -- not tools, not materials, not the product. They sold labor time to the factory owner. This was conceptually new: a clean separation between those who owned capital (machinery, raw materials, buildings) and those who owned only their labor.

Early factory conditions were severe. Parliamentary investigations (Sadler Committee, 1832; Ashley Commission, 1842) documented what workers experienced: 12-16 hour days, six days a week; children as young as five employed in coal mines and textile mills; machinery running without guards, causing frequent amputations and deaths; cotton dust filling the air of spinning mills, causing "brown lung." The 1842 Children's Employment Commission report, with engravings of children pulling coal tubs through low mine shafts, shocked middle-class public opinion and drove the Mines Act of 1842 banning women and children from underground work.

These conditions were not the result of individual cruelty but of structural forces. Competition in textile markets required minimizing costs; labor was the largest variable cost; unorganized workers could not resist wage cuts or speed-up; and the law offered no minimum conditions. Employers who voluntarily improved conditions faced competitive disadvantage. Change required collective action -- trade unions, which grew despite the Combination Acts (1799-1800) that criminalized labor organizing -- and legislation: the Factory Acts of 1833, 1844, and 1847 progressively restricted child labor and working hours.

The factory system also transformed urban geography. Workers needed to live within walking distance of factories; landlords responded by subdividing buildings and constructing back-to-back houses packed into courts, producing the industrial slum. Manchester's Ancoats, Leeds's East End, London's Whitechapel combined factory smoke, inadequate water, no sewerage, and dense housing in conditions that drove epidemic cholera and tuberculosis. Friedrich Engels's "The Condition of the Working Class in England" (1845) documented Manchester's conditions from firsthand observation -- one of the founding documents of industrial sociology.

The factory system concentrated workers, and concentration enabled collective organization. Trade unions, mutual aid societies, cooperative stores, and eventually political parties emerged from the factory's social geography. The labor movement that eventually improved factory conditions and reduced working hours was itself a product of the concentration the factory system created.
