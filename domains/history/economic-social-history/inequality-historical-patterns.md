# Historical Patterns of Inequality: Wealth, Power, and Opportunity

## Core Idea
Inequality — unequal distribution of wealth, power, and opportunity — varies across societies and time. Ancient and medieval societies had extreme inequality: elites controlled most wealth; commoners had few political rights; mobility between classes was limited. Industrial societies developed different forms of inequality: class (ownership of capital versus sale of labor) rather than status (legal ranks). Wealth inequality decreased in wealthy democracies during the mid-20th century (rising wages, progressive taxation, welfare states) but has increased since the 1980s (deindustrialization, declining unions, tax cuts for wealthy, globalization). Global inequality has not clearly trended: some countries developed rapidly (reducing inequality with wealthy countries) while most poor countries remained poor (increasing global inequality). Inequality within countries correlates with various harms: higher inequality societies have higher crime, worse health, lower social trust, lower social mobility. Yet inequality also varies as a choice: countries with similar levels of development have very different inequality depending on policies (taxes, spending, labor regulation, education). Understanding inequality history requires recognizing that inequality is neither natural nor inevitable, but results from institutions and policies. It also shows that some inequality reduction happened not automatically but through political struggle (progressive taxation, labor rights, welfare states). This suggests that reducing inequality requires political will and collective action.

## Explainer

Inequality has been a persistent feature of human societies, but its forms and magnitudes have varied. Ancient societies—Rome, Egypt, China—had extreme inequality based on legal status. The wealthy owned land, slaves, and political power; the poor were enslaved, indentured, or peasants with few rights and no legal protection. Mobility between status groups was rare; your status was largely determined by birth. Medieval European society formalized this: feudal hierarchy placed nobles at top, clergy with special privileges, commoners below, and serfs (nearly enslaved peasants) at bottom. Legally enforced inequality meant that a noble had different rights and obligations than a peasant. This legal inequality gradually eroded with commercialization. As markets developed, merchants and wealthy commoners challenged noble privilege. Industrialization accelerated this: the new basis of inequality became class—ownership of capital versus sale of labor—rather than legal status. A poor person could theoretically accumulate wealth and become wealthy; legal equality (all citizens equal before law) replaced legal privilege. Yet the practical effect was still extreme inequality: factory owners controlled vast wealth; workers had little property and depended on wages. Industrial inequality was perhaps more extreme than feudal inequality because it was less constrained by tradition and more driven by market forces.

In the 20th century, wealthy democracies experienced a remarkable compression of inequality during roughly 1945-1980. This resulted from specific political choices: strong labor unions negotiated high wages; progressive taxation imposed high top tax rates (90%+ in the US during the 1950s); welfare states provided universal public services (education, healthcare, pensions) and cash transfers. These policies redistributed income downward. The result was rising living standards, reduced poverty, and lower inequality. The postwar period appeared to show that inequality could be reduced. Yet this compression was not inevitable or permanent. Since the 1980s, inequality has risen sharply. Union membership declined; top tax rates fell; welfare spending stagnated; globalization enabled firms to shift production to low-wage countries; technology favored high-skilled workers. Now the wealthy have accumulated wealth more easily and retained more of it, while workers have faced stagnant wages and reduced public services. The comparison reveals that inequality reflects policy choices, not economic inevitability.

Global inequality—inequality between countries—is even more dramatic. Early industrializers (Britain, Western Europe, North America, Japan) accumulated vast wealth. Poor countries were colonized and extracted for resources; post-colonial development was hindered by debt and unfavorable trade arrangements. Today, the richest countries are 100+ times wealthier than the poorest. Within countries, inequality harms populations widely: unequal societies have higher crime, worse health outcomes, lower social trust, and lower social mobility. These correlations suggest that extreme inequality is socially destructive. Yet countries with similar development levels have very different inequality levels based on policy. This proves inequality is not determined by development but by institutions. Nordic countries with strong unions, progressive taxation, and universal public services have low inequality and high welfare. The US, similarly wealthy, has high inequality and less universal services. These differences are policy choices. Understanding inequality history reveals three key insights: inequality takes different forms historically (status-based vs. class-based vs. global), it can be reduced through political action (the postwar compression proves this), and reducing it requires sustained effort against those benefiting from inequality (evident in the 1980s reversal of egalitarian policies).
