# Mercantilism and Colonialism: Economic Imperialism

## Core Idea
Mercantilism was an early modern economic doctrine: states should maximize precious metal reserves and trade surpluses, pursue colonies for raw materials and captive markets, and regulate trade to benefit the state. Under mercantilism, trade was understood as zero-sum competition — one nation's gain was another's loss. European states competed to establish colonies in the Americas, Africa, and Asia, viewing them as sources of wealth. Spain extracted precious metals from the Americas; Britain, France, and the Netherlands competed for trading posts and territorial control. Colonial economic systems were designed to extract value for the metropole (the colonizing power): colonies provided raw materials and markets for manufactured goods, enriching merchants and manufacturers at home. Mercantilist logic justified colonialism: colonies were investments; the metropole had a right to profit from them. Yet mercantilism rested on a misunderstanding — modern economics recognizes that free trade is mutually beneficial; zero-sum competition is not economically productive. Britain, under Adam Smith's influence, began moving away from mercantilism toward free trade in the late 18th century. Yet mercantilism's legacy persisted: colonial economies remained extractive; colonial elites remained subordinate to metropolitan interests. Understanding mercantilism reveals how economic theory shapes policy; it also shows that state-directed economic policy, once challenged, gave way to ideological free-market arguments that also served powerful interests.

## Explainer

Mercantilism emerged in the 16th century as European kingdoms competed for global trade and treasure. The foundational mercantilist assumption was simple: national wealth equaled bullion (gold and silver). A nation with more bullion was stronger, wealthier, and more powerful. This assumption drove policy. Spain's acquisition of vast American silver (especially from Potosi in Bolivia) seemed to prove mercantilist logic — Spanish wealth soared in the 16th century. But Spain's later decline (despite continued silver flows) puzzled observers; mercantilists failed to recognize that mere bullion accumulation doesn't ensure long-term economic development. Mercantilism also embodied competition logic: trade was zero-sum. If England exported cloth to gain silver, France lost that silver. Nations therefore pursued self-sufficiency (autarky) and tried to maximize exports while restricting imports. Every nation wanted surpluses (exports > imports, measured in bullion); not all could simultaneously achieve surpluses. This logic justified colonial conquest: colonies were sources of raw materials and captive markets, enabling the metropole to run surpluses.

Colonial mercantilism took different forms. Spain focused on precious metal extraction, using indigenous and enslaved African labor in mines (particularly silver in Mexico and Peru). This created immense short-term wealth for the crown but little industrial development in Spain or productive capacity in colonies. Portugal pursued trade monopolies in spices and luxuries via trading posts in Asia, Africa, and Brazil. Britain and the Netherlands developed settlement colonies in North America and the Caribbean that combined agricultural production (sugar, tobacco, cotton) with settler occupation. The British Navigation Acts (1651–1663) were archetypical mercantilist policy: colonial products could only be sold to Britain or other British colonies; colonials could only buy British manufactures. This ensured Britain captured profits from colonial production. Colonial planters and merchants resented the system—they couldn't trade directly with other nations or buy cheaper foreign goods—but lacked political power to resist. The system enriched British merchants and manufacturers.

Mercantilist policy instruments included monopolies (granting exclusive trading rights to chartered companies like the East India Company), tariffs (taxes on imports), and navigation acts (requiring goods to be transported in national ships). These policies protected some domestic industries from competition, but they created inefficiencies. Tariffs raised consumer prices; monopolies restricted supply; navigation acts increased shipping costs. Yet these policies worked for the groups controlling them: merchants with monopolies profited enormously (the East India Company became the world's richest company); tariff-protected industries lobbied for higher protection. State power backed these policies; competing in the mercantilist system required powerful navies to protect trade and enforce monopolies.

The system began cracking in the late 18th century. Adam Smith's "The Wealth of Nations" (1776) provided intellectual ammunition against mercantilism: Smith showed that absolute advantages in production (and later comparative advantage) meant both trading partners could benefit from free exchange. Britain's industrial revolution generated confidence in British competitiveness; by the 1840s, Britain had repealed the Navigation Acts and moved toward free trade. But this shift wasn't purely intellectual — it reflected Britain's dominant position. Free trade benefited Britain because British industry was most competitive; other nations that couldn't compete (like India, colonized partly to protect British manufacturing) suffered. Mercantilism's demise didn't mean colonialism ended; it evolved. Instead of trade monopolies, colonial powers used tariffs and colonial preference (imperial preference) to maintain control. Colonial economies remained extractive: Algeria exported wine and grain to France; India exported cotton, sugar, and indigo to Britain. The colonial-metropole hierarchy persisted through mercantilism's successor systems, a reminder that economic doctrines serve power, not universal principles.
