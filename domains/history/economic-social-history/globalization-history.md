# Globalization: Expanding Networks, Trade, and Interdependence

## Core Idea
Globalization — the increasing integration of economies, communications, and cultures across borders — accelerated dramatically in the late 20th century. Trade as a share of world GDP grew; foreign direct investment increased; multinational corporations became dominant; supply chains spanned continents. Reduced transportation costs, telecommunications, and trade liberalization (GATT, WTO) enabled this integration. Globalization offered benefits: consumers access cheaper goods; developing countries gain export markets and investment; global problems (climate change, pandemics) require global solutions. Yet it also created costs and anxieties: manufacturing jobs shifted from wealthy countries to lower-wage countries; local industries could not compete with imports; corporate power increased relative to national governments; profits flowed to investors while workers' wages stagnated; environmental costs (pollution, resource depletion) were often externalized to poor countries. Globalization is not inevitable or neutral — it reflects decisions by governments, corporations, and international institutions. Different forms of globalization are possible. Understanding globalization's history requires recognizing both its efficiency benefits (trade does reduce prices and increase choice) and its distributional consequences (winners and losers). It also requires attention to how globalization's architecture — trade agreements, investment rules, IP protection, labor mobility restrictions — shapes outcomes. The current backlash against globalization reflects legitimate grievances about its unequal distribution of benefits, even if the proposed solutions are often problematic.

## Explainer

Globalization—the increasing integration of economies, communications, and cultures across borders—accelerated dramatically in the late 20th century due to convergent technological and policy changes. Containerization and air freight made shipping vastly cheaper; satellite communications and eventually the internet enabled real-time coordination of global supply chains; trade agreements (GATT, WTO) reduced tariffs and removed investment barriers. These changes transformed what was economically possible. Companies could now produce goods in multiple countries and assemble them elsewhere, coordinating operations across oceans in ways that were impossible before.

The scope of integration was unprecedented. Trade's share of global GDP roughly doubled from the 1960s to 2000s; foreign direct investment (money flowing across borders to build factories and businesses abroad) accelerated; multinational corporations became the dominant form of economic organization. Supply chains became genuinely global—a car might have components made in five countries; a smartphone assembled in China from parts sourced across Asia using designs from the US. This enabled remarkable efficiency: companies could access the cheapest labor and materials globally; consumers benefited from dramatically cheaper goods.

Yet this distribution of benefits was profoundly unequal. Manufacturing workers in wealthy countries—particularly the US, Britain, and parts of Europe—faced devastating job losses. Factories closed; communities that depended on manufacturing employment declined; wages stagnated for workers without college education. For these workers, globalization meant economic insecurity even as global GDP grew. Meanwhile, corporations and investors benefited enormously—profits increased, stock prices rose, compensation for executives soared. Consumers benefited from cheaper goods, but this benefit was distributed across the population while costs were concentrated on displaced workers.

Trade agreements shaped which benefits globalized. These agreements reduced tariffs on manufactured goods (benefiting corporations and consumers of those goods) but maintained high protections in agriculture and services where wealthy countries had advantages. Intellectual property rules strengthened monopolies on patents and copyrights, benefiting pharmaceutical, software, and entertainment companies. Investment rules allowed corporations to sue governments for regulations that reduced corporate profits—a remarkable transfer of power from public to private actors. Labor mobility restrictions prevented workers from migrating in search of jobs, while capital could move freely. These rule architectures reflected power imbalances: wealthy countries negotiated to protect their interests while opening others' markets.

Environmental and social costs were systematically externalized. Manufacturing relocated to countries with weaker environmental regulations and labor protections, allowing corporations to avoid paying for pollution, worker safety, and resource depletion. These costs were borne by communities in developing regions. A factory worker in Bangladesh might earn $3 per day making clothes for Western markets while bearing the health costs of factory pollution. This represented a massive transfer of environmental and health burdens from wealthy to poor regions, effectively subsidizing wealthy consumers' cheap goods.

Some developing regions captured benefits through export-driven growth (South Korea, Taiwan, Vietnam in recent decades) by building manufacturing capacity. But many others (particularly sub-Saharan Africa, parts of Latin America) saw minimal gains while facing increased competition that harmed local industries. Global inequality—measured as differences in average income between countries—actually increased during the globalization era despite the theory predicting convergence. Globalization created highly divergent outcomes depending on initial conditions, policy choices, and geographic advantages. Understanding contemporary inequality, geopolitical tensions, and political backlash against trade requires recognizing these distributional consequences of globalization, not just its aggregate efficiency benefits.
