# The History of Banking: Credit, Institutions, and Crisis

## Core Idea
Banking — the practice of accepting deposits and making loans — emerged in medieval Europe from money-changing and merchant operations. Early banks (like the Medici bank in Florence) financed trade and government; bankers became wealthy and influential. Banking depends on trust: depositors must believe their money is safe; borrowers must be able to repay. This introduces risk: banks do not actually hold all deposits as cash (fractional reserve banking); if many depositors withdraw simultaneously, banks fail. Bank crises have occurred repeatedly: panics when confidence collapses; runs as depositors rush to withdraw; failures as banks cannot pay. Modern central banks evolved partly to manage these risks: they act as lenders of last resort, providing liquidity during crises; they regulate banks to ensure safety. Yet banking also creates opportunities for speculation and excessive risk-taking: if profits are private but losses are socialized (governments bail out failing banks), banks have incentive to take excessive risks. The 2008 financial crisis resulted from excessive risk-taking in mortgage lending and derivatives; governments bailouts saved banks while homeowners and workers suffered. Understanding banking's history reveals both its necessity (credit is essential for economic activity) and its dangers (fractional reserve banking and profit incentives create instability). It also shows how government regulation and central banking evolved to manage instability, yet remain imperfect. The political economy of banking — how financial interests shape regulation — is crucial to understanding modern economies.

## Explainer

Banking is the practice of accepting deposits from those who have excess money and making loans to those who need credit. It emerged gradually in medieval Europe as merchants and money-changers evolved into organized banking. The Medici family of Florence developed one of history's first banks in the 14th century, financing trade, providing services to rulers, and managing international transactions. Medici bankers didn't just handle transactions; they made loans and investments, building wealth and political influence. This established the pattern: banking could be enormously profitable and politically powerful.

Banking depends fundamentally on trust. Depositors must believe their money is safe—that they can withdraw it when needed. Borrowers must be creditworthy—likely to repay loans. Bankers earn profits by accepting low-interest deposits and making higher-interest loans, pocketing the spread. However, this creates an inherent structural risk: banks don't hold all deposits as cash. Instead, they practice fractional reserve banking—accepting deposits worth $100 million while holding only, say, $10 million in cash reserves, and lending the other $90 million. This works because historically not all depositors withdraw simultaneously. But it creates vulnerability: if many depositors try to withdraw at once (a "bank run"), the bank cannot meet withdrawals and fails.

Bank crises have recurred throughout history. Panics occur when depositors lose confidence and rush to withdraw deposits before a bank fails. These panics can become self-fulfilling: if depositors believe a bank will fail and withdraw money, the withdrawals cause the failure they feared. Panics spread—if one bank faces a run, depositors at other banks may panic, spreading the crisis. The Great Depression (1929-1939) involved massive banking collapse: in the US alone, roughly 9,000 banks failed. Depositors lost all their savings. This catastrophe created pressure for banking reform.

Modern central banking evolved partly to prevent banking crises. Central banks act as "lenders of last resort"—they provide emergency liquidity (cash) to banks facing runs, allowing them to meet withdrawal demands without failing. The US Federal Reserve, created in 1913, played this role during the Great Depression insufficiently, but the principle was established. Deposit insurance—government guaranteeing that depositors will be repaid even if their bank fails—provided another safeguard. In the US, the Federal Deposit Insurance Corporation (FDIC) insures deposits up to $250,000. This eliminated the rationale for panic: if your deposits are guaranteed by government, you have no reason to rush to withdraw.

These innovations greatly reduced banking crises in the postwar period (1945-1980). Regulation also increased: the Glass-Steagall Act (1933) separated commercial banking (deposit-taking) from investment banking (speculative trading), reducing risk-taking incentives. Bank capital requirements forced banks to maintain minimum equity reserves. These regulations worked reasonably well for decades.

However, the political economy of banking created pressure against regulation. Financial interests—banks, investment firms, wealthy individuals benefiting from financial deregulation—lobbied continuously for deregulation, arguing that restrictions reduced efficiency and innovation. Starting in the 1980s and accelerating through the 1990s, regulations were systematically dismantled. Glass-Steagall was repealed (1999), removing the separation between commercial and investment banking. Capital requirements were weakened. Financial innovation created new instruments (derivatives, mortgage-backed securities) that weren't regulated. Mortgage lending became increasingly risky: banks made loans to borrowers with no income verification, securitized the mortgages into complex derivatives, and sold them off, removing the lender's incentive to ensure loans would be repaid.

The 2008 financial crisis revealed the consequences. Banks had taken enormous risks in mortgage lending and derivatives, assuming housing prices would perpetually rise. When housing prices fell, mortgage defaults soared, mortgage-backed securities became worthless, and major financial institutions faced failure. The government bailed them out—spending roughly $700 billion to rescue banks. Meanwhile, homeowners foreclosed, unemployment skyrocketed, and millions lost their savings. The bailout revealed a fundamental inequity: banks' losses were socialized (paid by taxpayers), while their profits had been privatized.

This pattern reveals the core problem in banking: profit incentives create risk-taking, but the risks are borne by depositors and taxpayers through bailouts. This moral hazard problem—where those taking risks don't bear the consequences—is never fully solved. Regulations help, but political power determines their strength. After 2008, some re-regulation occurred, but financial interests lobbied successfully against stronger measures. The system remains vulnerable to future crises because the underlying incentives—profits are private, losses are socialized—remain unchanged. Understanding banking's history reveals both the necessity of credit for economic activity and the dangers of unregulated financial systems in which profit-seeking drives excessive risk-taking.
