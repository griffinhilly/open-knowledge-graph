# Urbanization: The Growth of Cities and Urban Societies

## Core Idea
Urbanization — the migration of people from countryside to cities — accelerated during the Industrial Revolution. Cities grew because factories were located in or near existing cities; workers migrated to seek factory work; cities generated services (trade, banking, government) that provided employment. Between 1800 and 1900, the proportion of people living in cities in Britain rose from 25% to 75%. Rapid urbanization created challenges: housing was inadequate, crowded, and unsanitary; water supplies became contaminated; disease spread easily. London's cholera epidemics and other cities' public health crises were consequences of unplanned rapid urbanization. Cities were filthy, dangerous, and squalid for workers while amenities existed only for the wealthy. Yet cities also offered opportunity — wages in cities were higher than rural areas; cities had schools, theaters, and public spaces offering life beyond farming. Urbanization generated social problems that motivated reform: public health movements pushed for clean water, sewers, and sanitation; housing reformers fought for tenement regulation; sanitation engineers built the infrastructure needed for large cities. Understanding urbanization reveals how industrial economic organization drove massive social transformation: the movement of billions of people from countryside to cities reshaped geography, politics, culture, and social relationships. It also shows how cities were built environments that reflected power — wealth determined access to good housing, clean air, and safety, while workers lived in crowded, dangerous conditions.

## Explainer

Urbanization was not new in the 1800s, but its scale and rapidity were unprecedented. Pre-industrial cities were small (London had 500,000 people in 1700; preindustrial cities rarely exceeded 100,000). Urbanization during industrialization was massive: London exceeded 1 million in 1800, 3 million by 1860. Manchester grew from 25,000 (1772) to 300,000 (1850). This growth was driven by factories: mills required water power (found in specific locations), coal access, and labor. Factory owners chose locations; workers migrated to those locations. Urban services (banking, government, trading houses) concentrated in cities, generating additional employment. By 1850, Britain (the industrial leader) had 50% urban population; by 1900, 75%. This transformation was staggering: in a century, Britain shifted from rural agricultural to urban industrial society.

The human costs of rapid urbanization were severe. Workers crowded into tenement housing: a single room housed 5–10 people in cities like Manchester, Liverpool, and Glasgow. Tenements rose 5–7 stories, packed densely; toilets were shared privies shared by dozens; water came from pumps contaminated by sewage. Disease thrived: cholera (water-borne), typhus (lice), tuberculosis (crowding), dysentery (contamination). Deaths in the 1830s–1850s were horrifying: cholera epidemics killed thousands in days. London's cholera outbreak of 1854 (the Broad Street pump outbreak) killed 500 in a week in a single neighborhood. Infant mortality in industrial cities reached 30–50% (compared to 15–20% in rural areas). Living conditions were worse than rural poverty partly because urban poverty was crowded; rural poor scattered across countryside could at least maintain distance from contagion.

Why did workers accept these conditions? Urban wages were substantially higher: factory workers earned 6–8 shillings per week; rural agricultural laborers earned 3–4 shillings. Higher wages meant survival, food, possibility of accumulation. Rural areas faced their own crises: enclosure of common lands (1750–1850) displaced peasants; mechanized agriculture reduced labor demand; Corn Laws (1815–1846) kept grain prices artificially high, deepening rural poverty. Migration to cities was rational economic choice, even knowing conditions were terrible. Some workers saved money and bought small property; others fell into chronic poverty. Either way, the wage differential pulled workers urban.

Urbanization generated social problems that demanded solutions. Cholera epidemics, which struck repeatedly, mobilized public health response: sewerage systems, clean water supplies, sanitation regulations. The Great Stink of 1858 (London's sewage and industrial waste overflowing the Thames) forced Parliament to action; by 1865, London had modern sewers (engineered by Joseph Bazalgette). Public health officials emerged as new profession: inspecting food, water, housing. Housing reform movements fought for tenement regulation, restricting occupancy and requiring sanitation. Public education expanded partly to improve urban children's productivity. Recreation facilities (parks, libraries, museums) were built partly for social control—reformers believed parks provided healthy outlets for workers' leisure time, reducing radicalism and crime.

Urban reform was hampered by political economy. Tenement landlords opposed rent control or occupancy limits (which reduced profits). Property owners resisted taxation for sewers and water (public goods they had to help finance). Factory owners initially resisted safety regulations and child labor laws. Yet reform succeeded partially because epidemics threatened wealthy neighborhoods too and because labor movement pressure (strikes, unionization, political agitation) forced concessions. Reform was thus political struggle: workers fought for livable conditions; capital fought to minimize costs; governments mediated, often favoring capital. The result: substantial improvements (sewers, water, public health) in cities, but persistent inequality (wealthy neighborhoods got better services first; poor neighborhoods remained substandard).

Urbanization's cultural and social effects were profound. Urban crowding created new forms of social connection: workingmates, political organizations, labor unions. Cities enabled mass culture: newspapers, theaters, music halls. Cities generated new middle class (managers, professionals, merchants) distinct from both workers and landed gentry. Urban middle-class culture (moral respectability, temperance, female domesticity) spread through literature and became aspirational. Yet cities also generated radical movements: socialists, anarchists, and communists found urban audiences; the 1871 Paris Commune was an urban insurrection; trade unionism grew from urban workplaces. Urbanization thus reshaped politics and culture, not just economy and society. It created the modern working class as a self-conscious political force and modern radical political movements as reactions to industrial capitalism.
