# The History of Taxation: Revenue, Redistribution, and Equity

## Core Idea
Taxation — government collection of revenue from individuals and businesses — is fundamental to modern states. Early taxation was often arbitrary and unpopular; the American Revolution was partly triggered by British taxation; the French Revolution partly by financial crisis linked to unequal taxation. Modern tax systems include: income tax (taxation of wages and profit); sales tax (taxation of consumption); property tax (taxation of wealth); corporate tax (taxation of business profit). Tax incidence — who actually bears the tax burden — varies: progressive taxes (higher rates for higher income) shift burden to wealthy; regressive taxes (same rate for all) place heavier burden on poor. Tax systems also serve redistributive purposes: governments use taxes to fund public goods (roads, education, defense) and transfer payments (welfare, healthcare, pensions). Progressive taxation increased during the 20th century: top tax rates reached 90% in the US during WWII. Yet since the 1980s, taxation has become less progressive: top tax rates have fallen; corporate taxes have fallen; wealth taxation has been reduced. Outcomes of tax policy vary: countries with progressive taxation and strong public spending have lower inequality and poverty; countries with regressive taxation have higher inequality. Understanding taxation history reveals that tax systems are choices, not natural features. They reflect political power: wealthy groups lobby for low taxes; workers lobby for public spending and progressive taxation. Tax policy is thus a key site of political contestation about distribution of resources.

## Explainer

Taxation emerged as states developed the bureaucratic capacity to assess property, income, and transactions. Early taxation was typically arbitrary: rulers extracted what they could from conquered territories. Peasants owed taxes in crops or labor; merchants faced arbitrary import duties. This arbitrary taxation created deep resentment and contributed to revolutions. The American Revolution (1775) was partly triggered by British efforts to tax colonists ("taxation without representation"). The French Revolution (1789) erupted partly because of a fiscal crisis: the government needed revenue but relied heavily on taxing commoners while exempting the wealthy and clergy. This unequal burden created resentment that fueled revolutionary sentiment. As modern nation-states developed, tax systems became more systematic and rationalized. Modern tax systems typically include multiple components: income tax (taxation of wages and business profits), sales tax (taxation of consumption), property tax (taxation of wealth), and corporate tax (taxation of business profit). Each tax type has different effects on behavior and distribution.

A crucial concept is "tax incidence": who actually bears the burden of a tax. A sales tax nominally paid by the buyer might have incidence on producers if it reduces demand. An income tax nominally paid by the employee might have incidence on the employer if workers demand higher wages to compensate. Progressive taxation—where higher incomes face higher tax rates—shifts burden toward wealthy people. Regressive taxation—where everyone pays the same rate—places heavier burden on poor people because a fixed percentage consumes more of their income. Progressive taxation increased dramatically during the 20th century. In the US, the federal income tax started in 1913 at low rates but reached 90% on top earners during WWII and remained high (70%+) through the 1970s. This reflected political commitment to equality during the postwar period. Yet since the 1980s, taxation has become less progressive: top tax rates fell to 50%, then 37%; corporate taxes declined; wealth taxation was reduced or eliminated. These changes reflected shifting political power—wealthy groups successfully lobbied for lower taxes and reduced government programs.

Tax systems also serve redistributive purposes beyond just raising revenue. Governments use taxes to fund public goods (roads, education, defense) and transfer payments (welfare, healthcare, pensions, unemployment insurance). These functions reflect political choices about the state's role. The evidence shows that countries vary dramatically in inequality based on their tax and spending choices. Countries with progressive taxation and strong public spending—Scandinavia, Germany, much of Western Europe—have lower inequality and poverty than countries with regressive taxation and weak public spending—many developing countries, and increasingly the US and UK as progressivity declined. This demonstrates that inequality is not a natural outcome but results from policy choices. Understanding taxation history reveals that tax policy is fundamentally political. Wealthy groups lobby for low taxes and weak public spending. Workers and the poor lobby for progressive taxation and strong public spending. The balance between these forces determines actual tax systems. The postwar period saw labor unions at peak strength and egalitarian values dominant, producing progressive taxation. Since 1980, union decline and pro-business ideology produced regressive taxation. This politics of taxation shapes distribution of resources and inequality.
