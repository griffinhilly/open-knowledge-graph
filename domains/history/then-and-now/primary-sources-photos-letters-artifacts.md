# Primary Sources — Photos, Letters, and Artifacts

## Core Idea
Primary sources are objects and documents from the past that give us direct evidence of what life was like. Old photographs show us how people and places looked. Letters tell us what people were thinking and feeling. Artifacts — objects people used, like tools, toys, or clothing — show us how people lived day to day. Primary sources are the building blocks of history because they come directly from the time period being studied.

## How It's Best Learned
Bring in (or show images of) real primary sources: old family photos, postcards, coins, tools, or newspaper clippings. Have children examine them like detectives — what can you learn from this object? Who might have used it? When was it from? Practice comparing a primary source (an old photo) with a secondary source (a textbook description of the same event). Visit a local museum or archive to see primary sources in person.

## Common Misconceptions
- Children may think primary sources are only very old things from hundreds of years ago (a photo from 20 years ago is also a primary source).
- Some children confuse primary sources with secondary sources (a primary source is from the time period itself; a secondary source is someone writing about it later, like a textbook).
- Children may think primary sources always tell the complete truth (a single source gives one perspective, and it may be incomplete or biased).

## Explainer

Imagine you find an old box in your attic. Inside, there is a faded **photograph** of a woman in old-fashioned clothes, a **letter** with handwriting you can barely read, and a small **tool** you have never seen before. These items are what historians call **primary sources** — real objects from the past that give us direct clues about how people lived.

A **primary source** is anything that comes directly from the time period being studied. Old photos show us what people, buildings, and streets actually looked like. Letters and diaries tell us what people were thinking and feeling in their own words. **Artifacts** — physical objects like tools, toys, coins, clothing, and dishes — show us how people went about their daily lives. Even old newspapers and maps are primary sources.

What makes primary sources so special is that they are not someone's retelling of history — they ARE pieces of history. When you hold an old tool and wonder who used it, or read a letter from someone who lived 100 years ago, you are touching the **actual past**. That is different from reading about the past in a textbook, which is a **secondary source** — someone's description of events they did not personally experience.

But primary sources have limitations too. A single photograph shows only one moment from one angle. A letter captures only one person's thoughts and feelings. The person writing might have been exaggerating, mistaken, or only telling part of the story. That is why historians try to look at **many different primary sources** and piece them together, like assembling a puzzle.

Learning to examine primary sources is one of the most exciting skills in history. When you look at an old photo and ask "Who are these people? Where are they? When was this taken? What was their life like?" — you are doing exactly what real historians do every day. You are reading the clues the past left behind.
