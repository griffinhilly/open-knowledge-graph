# Figurative Language and Poetic Devices

## Core Idea
Figurative language—metaphor, simile, personification, hyperbole, and others—creates meaning through comparison, image, and compression. These devices are not mere decoration; they shape how readers perceive and understand. Analyzing figurative language requires attention to specific word choices and patterns of imagery.

## Explainer

You already know the basic categories: a **metaphor** says one thing is another, a **simile** says it is like another, **personification** gives human qualities to non-human things, **hyperbole** exaggerates for effect. You can identify these. The move this topic is asking you to make is different: from identification to analysis. Identification answers "what is this?" Analysis answers "what does it do?" — what does this specific figure create that literal language could not, and why does it matter?

Start with a single metaphor and work it through. Sylvia Plath writes: "Dying / Is an art, like everything else. / I do it exceptionally well." The comparison frames death as craft — something skilled, practiced, self-conscious. That is already surprising. But then "I do it exceptionally well" adds pride, even competitive pride. A literal statement — "I have nearly died" — communicates fact. The metaphor communicates a psychology: the speaker's relationship to her own suffering is not victim-passive but performance-oriented, which is disturbing and darkly funny at once. The metaphor is doing interpretive work that literal language cannot do without many more words, and it is doing it while also generating tone and emotional ambivalence. That is what figurative language analysis looks for: the **surplus meaning** a figure produces over a literal equivalent.

**Patterns of imagery** are a further level. Individual figures are meaningful; repeated figures across a text are structural. If a poem or novel consistently uses water imagery — rivers, floods, drowning, thirst — those images accumulate into a pattern that the reader begins to experience as a register of something the text is arguing. The pattern might reinforce a theme, create an emotional texture, or contradict the surface meaning of the plot. When you identify an image pattern, ask: what does this pattern add up to? What does the text seem to be saying through images that it is not quite saying in its explicit statements?

**Metonymy** and **synecdoche** work differently from metaphor and simile and repay separate attention. A metaphor substitutes based on resemblance ("the world is a stage"). A metonymy substitutes based on association ("the crown decided to raise taxes" — the crown stands for the monarchy because it is associated with it, not because it resembles it). These are not mere rhetorical variants; they represent different cognitive operations, and recognizing them opens different analytical questions. Close reading of figurative language is ultimately an exercise in precision: what exactly is being compared to what, on what basis, and with what effect? The more precisely you can answer those three questions, the richer the analysis becomes.
