# World Literature Debates: Damrosch, Casanova, Moretti, and Beyond

## Core Idea
Scholars like David Damrosch, Pascale Casanova, and Franco Moretti have developed different theoretical frameworks for understanding world literature. Damrosch emphasizes circulation and reception; Casanova theorizes literary world-systems and prestige economies; Moretti proposes distant reading and quantitative methods. These debates raise fundamental questions: What counts as world literature? How do we compare vastly different traditions? Can we study world literature responsibly without exoticizing or homogenizing difference? Understanding these debates is essential for engaging thoughtfully with comparative literature.

## Explainer

You've already encountered the concept of world literature and the methodological foundations of comparative study. These critical debates sharpen the underlying tensions that comparative method only partly resolves: *who gets to define the canon, through what mechanisms does a text become "world literature," and what are we actually doing when we compare texts across vastly different traditions?* The three major theorists each offer a different answer.

**David Damrosch** proposes that world literature is defined by circulation and reception rather than origin or intrinsic quality. A work becomes world literature when it travels — when it is read, translated, and interpreted outside its culture of origin. Crucially, Damrosch argues that texts change as they circulate: a text read in translation, in a different historical moment, in a different cultural context, is not the same text the original readers encountered. World literature is always literature *in translation*, both literally and figuratively. This is a generous, pluralistic definition — it opens the canon to works that become influential far from home — but it also raises the question of whether circulation favors texts that are already legible to Western readers, in effect rewarding what is already translatable rather than what is most significant within its own tradition.

**Pascale Casanova's** framework is more adversarial. Drawing on Pierre Bourdieu's sociology of cultural fields, she theorizes a **world republic of letters** structured around unequal distributions of literary capital. Some languages and traditions — French literature above all, in her account — accumulate prestige and function as consecrating institutions: a novel recognized in Paris gains international legitimacy that a novel only recognized in its home country lacks. This model explains why some national literatures seem perpetually "emerging" while others set the terms of literary value. The implication is political: world literature is not a neutral gathering of humanity's best writing; it is a field structured by power relations that privilege certain centers over peripheries. Postcolonial scholars extended this analysis, arguing that the dominant frameworks for reading world literature import Western aesthetic assumptions that distort or simply fail to read non-Western texts on their own terms.

**Franco Moretti** takes a radically different approach, proposing **distant reading** — the analysis of large archives using quantitative and computational methods rather than close attention to individual texts. His argument is pragmatic: no scholar can read all of world literature, so the pretense of depth through close reading is a selection bias masquerading as universality. By studying patterns across thousands of novels — plot structures, character networks, the spread of the novel form globally — Moretti claims to reveal systemic regularities invisible to the close reader. Critics object that statistical regularities miss what is most important about individual works, and that Moretti's models privilege the texts that are already digitized and translated (disproportionately Western). The debate between close and distant reading is also a debate about what literary study is *for*.

What unites all three frameworks — and the debates they have generated — is a shared recognition that "world literature" is not a neutral descriptive category but a contested construction. When you engage with comparative literature, you are inevitably making choices about canon, method, and values that these debates make explicit. The most rigorous comparatists approach these questions self-consciously: not pretending their method is transparent, but being clear about what they are prioritizing, what they cannot see from their position, and whose literature risks being misread or excluded.
