# Time Travel: Causality, Paradox, and Timeline Logic

## Core Idea
Time travel narratives grapple with causality, paradoxes, and multiple timeline theories. Fixed timeline theories prevent changing the past (whatever happened, happened); branching timeline theories allow divergence from a split point; ontological paradoxes create logical loops where information or objects arrive with no origin. Different time-travel frameworks enable different story structures and raise different philosophical questions about free will and determinism.

## How It's Best Learned
Chart three time-travel stories using different causality frameworks (e.g., Primer [fixed], Back to the Future [branching], All You Need Is Kill [ontological]). Notice how each framework constrains and enables different plot developments.

## Common Misconceptions
Not all time-travel stories require paradoxes; some use consistent timelines where the past cannot be changed. The philosophical framework matters as much as the mechanics.

## Explainer

Time-travel fiction requires writers to make explicit what other narratives can leave implicit: what rules govern causality in this fictional universe? These rules, once established, profoundly constrain what kinds of stories can be told. A writer must decide: Can the past be changed? If so, what happens to the original timeline? Does changing the past erase the time traveler? These decisions aren't arbitrary; they determine the entire structure of possibility for the story.

Fixed timeline theory establishes that the past cannot be changed. Whatever happened, happened. This creates a deterministic narrative framework where the time traveler's actions are revealed to be part of history, not disruptions to it. A character traveling back to prevent an assassination discovers they were the assassin all along. A character trying to warn the past discovers they already did warn them. This framework enables stories about discovering the true nature of history and one's role within it. It raises philosophical questions about free will and predetermination. If the past cannot be changed, then the time traveler never actually had the choice to change it, even if they believed they did.

Branching timeline theory allows the past to remain unchanged while creating a new timeline from the point of divergence. When a time traveler changes the past, they create an alternate reality that diverges from the original. This framework enables stories about consequence and responsibility. The traveler's actions matter in their new timeline but cannot erase the original timeline. This creates poignant narrative possibilities: the traveler saves someone in the new timeline but the original version of that person is still dead. They prevent a disaster in one timeline but the original disaster still occurred. Branching timelines create a sense of irreversible multiplication—every change creates another world, none of which can be undone.

Ontological paradoxes create logical loops where information or physical objects exist without ever being created. A time traveler retrieves a musical composition, travels back in time, and presents it to a composer as original work. The composer publishes it, and eventually it becomes the same composition the time traveler once retrieved. The composition exists but was never created by anyone. It's a causality loop with no origin point. Ontological paradoxes create a kind of metaphysical weirdness distinct from the logical problems of changing history. They raise questions about information and identity: if something exists in a causal loop, does it really exist? What's the difference between a genuine creation and a loop-created object?

Different frameworks shape narrative stakes and emotional resonance. Fixed timeline theory creates a deterministic feel where free will is revealed as illusion. Branching timelines create tragedy and ethical complexity—the traveler must live with knowing they left their original timeline (and the people they love) unhelped. Ontological paradoxes create metaphysical discomfort. Understanding which framework a time-travel story uses is crucial to understanding what questions the story explores and what kinds of consequences matter.

Understanding time-travel narrative requires recognizing that the philosophical framework is as important as the mechanics. A time machine is just a tool; what matters is the causality rules governing its use. The framework determines whether the story is about discovering one's role in predetermined history, about the tragedy of creating alternate timelines where you cannot follow, or about the metaphysical strangeness of causality loops. Great time-travel fiction uses its chosen framework not as a constraint to minimize but as the primary vehicle for exploring ideas about fate, free will, and the nature of causality itself.
