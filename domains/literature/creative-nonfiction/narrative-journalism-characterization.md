# Characterization in Narrative Journalism

## Core Idea
Characterization in literary journalism builds from reported observation, interviews, and research rather than invention. Writers develop complex portraits of real people through revealing detail, dialogue, behavior, and interior access (drawn from interviews or observation) while maintaining factual fidelity and avoiding caricature or stereotype.

## How It's Best Learned
Profile someone you can observe in two different settings, such as a coach at practice and at a team dinner. Gather material in three columns: what they say, what they do, and what others say about them. Build a paragraph around the three details that most complicate a first impression, attributing each to its source, then check whether any detail leans on a stereotype with nothing to complicate it.

## Common Misconceptions
- To give a real person psychological depth, a journalist has to imagine their inner life. (Depth comes from reporting: interviews about how the person understands themselves, observation across settings, and documents in their own voice.)
- Any vivid detail is fair to use as long as it is true. (A true detail chosen without context can still create a false impression or a stereotype, so selection carries responsibility.)
- Describing a subject reveals more than quoting them. (Accurately reported dialogue often reveals character more than description does.)

## Explainer

Characterization is a literary technique often associated with fiction, but narrative journalists use similar techniques to create complex portraits of real people. The difference is that in journalism, you're working with actual people, so you must build character from reported material—interviews, observation, documents, research.

Good characterization in journalism starts with thorough reporting. You need to know the person well enough to recognize what's significant about them, what's revealing, what's contradictory. Through interviews, you learn how they understand themselves. Through observation, you see how they behave in different contexts. Through documents, you access their own voice and perspective.

You then select revealing details. How someone speaks, dresses, moves—these small things reveal character. But you must be careful that details are fairly chosen and won't create false impressions. A detail about someone's appearance might confirm a stereotype unless you also show complexity that complicates that stereotype.

Dialogue is crucial in journalism characterization. Real dialogue, accurately reported, reveals character more than description. What someone says, how they say it, what they notice and ignore—this shows who they are.

Interior access—showing what someone thought or felt—requires reporting justification. You might quote an interview where they describe their thoughts. You might observe behavior and describe the emotion it suggests. But you're not inventing; you're reporting what you learned.

The best characterization in journalism creates dimension without needing invention. Through careful reporting and selection, real people emerge as complex, contradictory, interesting—because that's what people actually are. The journalist's job is not to invent depth but to discover and faithfully represent it.
