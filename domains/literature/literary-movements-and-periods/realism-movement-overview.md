# Realism: Objective Representation and Social Truth

## Core Idea
Realism (mid-19th century) rejected Romanticism's subjectivity in favor of objective representation of contemporary life in mundane particularity. Realist writers claimed fidelity to observed reality, selecting ordinary subjects while employing controlled narrative perspective and precise descriptive detail. The movement shaped prose style and narrative technique, establishing the novel as an instrument for understanding society.

## Explainer

Realism represents nineteenth-century literature's fundamental reorientation. Romanticism had celebrated subjectivity, emotion, and imagination. Realism inverted priorities: objectivity, careful observation, and fidelity to reality became primary values. Rather than transcending ordinary experience, realists celebrated it. A peasant's life, a merchant's day, a shopkeeper's struggles—these ordinary subjects were worthy of serious literature.

This demanded new techniques. Precise detail, careful observation, controlled narrative perspective—these served truth-telling. Realists adopted what they understood as scientific method: observe carefully, record precisely, avoid subjective distortion. The novel became an instrument for understanding society, as systematic and reliable as any scientific investigation.

Realism democratized literature. By selecting ordinary subjects and ordinary people, realism claimed their experiences mattered. This challenged assumptions that literature should address exceptional figures or elevated themes. It asserted that understanding society required representing all classes and conditions.

The movement shaped prose style itself. Realists rejected ornamental language, seeking precision and clarity. This economy of style served truth: language should convey reality accurately without decoration. Narrative technique similarly served representation: controlling perspective allowed authors to shape how readers understood reality.

Realism's legacy is the establishment of the novel as legitimate vehicle for social investigation. It proved literature could address serious social questions, could represent reality faithfully, and could contribute to understanding contemporary life. This conviction persists: literature's capacity to investigate society remains fundamental to how we understand the form.
