# The Native American Renaissance

## Core Idea
"Native American Renaissance" is the name the critic Kenneth Lincoln gave, in a 1983 book, to the surge of fiction and poetry by Native American writers that followed N. Scott Momaday's novel *House Made of Dawn* (1968), which won the 1969 Pulitzer Prize for Fiction. Key works include James Welch's *Winter in the Blood* (1974), Leslie Marmon Silko's *Ceremony* (1977) and Louise Erdrich's *Love Medicine* (1984). These books bring oral story, ceremony and tribal history into the modern novel, often through a character returning home damaged and seeking healing.

## How It's Best Learned
Read the opening of *Ceremony*, where poems about Thought-Woman and the power of stories are set between prose chapters, and ask why Silko breaks the page this way. Then trace how the returning-veteran plot works in *Ceremony* or *House Made of Dawn*. Finish by reading two linked chapters of *Love Medicine*, each told by a different narrator, and discuss what the shared family story gains from many voices.

## Common Misconceptions
- Native American written literature began in the 1960s. (Earlier writers include Samson Occom and William Apess in the eighteenth and nineteenth centuries, Zitkala-Ša in the early twentieth, and D'Arcy McNickle, whose novel *The Surrounded* appeared in 1936.)
- "Native American literature" describes one shared culture. (The writers come from distinct nations with different languages and traditions: Momaday is Kiowa, Silko is Laguna Pueblo, Welch was Blackfeet and Gros Ventre, and Erdrich is a citizen of the Turtle Mountain Band of Chippewa.)
- These novels simply record traditional tales. (They adapt oral material inside modern forms such as fragmented time, multiple narrators and stream of consciousness, and they address war, alcohol, poverty and life off and on reservations.)

## Explainer
In 1969 the Pulitzer Prize for Fiction went to *House Made of Dawn*, the first novel by N. Scott Momaday, a Kiowa writer and scholar. Its main character, Abel, returns from the Second World War to his home pueblo in New Mexico and cannot find his place there or in the city. The novel moves back and forth in time, mixes prose styles, and draws on ritual, song and the land itself. Its success opened a door. Over the next fifteen years, publishers and readers paid new attention to Native American writers, and in 1983 the critic Kenneth Lincoln called the period the "Native American Renaissance."

Several books from these years are now taught as American classics. James Welch's *Winter in the Blood* (1974) follows an unnamed Native narrator through a flat, darkly comic stretch of life on a Montana reservation, as he slowly learns his family's history. Leslie Marmon Silko's *Ceremony* (1977) tells of Tayo, a Laguna veteran who returns from the war sick in body and spirit. A mixed-blood healer named Betonie leads him into a ceremony that must be adapted to the modern world. Silko sets traditional stories in verse between the prose sections, so the novel itself works like a ceremony. Louise Erdrich's *Love Medicine* (1984) tells the history of linked Ojibwe families in North Dakota through chapters spoken by different narrators across five decades.

Common features appear across these works. Time is often nonlinear, because memory and story do not follow a calendar. A damaged character returns home and must reconnect with land, family and tradition. Oral storytelling is treated as knowledge, not decoration. The books also face hard realities directly, including war trauma, alcoholism and the history of land loss and forced schooling.

The label has limits. Native authors were writing long before 1968, from Samson Occom's eighteenth-century sermons to Zitkala-Ša's essays and D'Arcy McNickle's novel *The Surrounded* (1936). Oral traditions never ended. The writers also come from many distinct nations. When you read these novels, ask how each one carries oral forms into the novel, and what the return-home plot says about identity, place and survival.
