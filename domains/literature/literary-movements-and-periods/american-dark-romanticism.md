# American Dark Romanticism

## Core Idea
Dark Romanticism is a modern critical label for the mid-nineteenth-century American writers who shared the Romantic interest in emotion, imagination and the individual but doubted the Transcendentalist faith in human goodness. Edgar Allan Poe, Nathaniel Hawthorne and Herman Melville are its central figures. Their fiction explores guilt, sin, obsession, madness and the limits of what a person can know, often through Gothic settings, symbols and deliberate ambiguity.

## How It's Best Learned
Pair a passage from Emerson's *Nature* with Hawthorne's "Young Goodman Brown" (1835) and ask how each text pictures a person alone in the woods. Then read Poe's "The Tell-Tale Heart" (1843) and track how the narrator's voice reveals what he denies. A third step is Melville's "Bartleby, the Scrivener" (1853), where the darkness is quiet and comes from an office, not a haunted house.

## Common Misconceptions
- Dark Romanticism was a movement its writers joined and named. (The term is a later critical grouping. Poe, Hawthorne and Melville did not see themselves as a school, though Melville admired Hawthorne and wrote about him.)
- These writers rejected Romanticism. (They kept its emphasis on feeling, imagination, nature and the individual, but they turned it toward the mind's darker capacities.)
- Every Dark Romantic story is a horror story with monsters. (Many are moral or psychological studies, such as *The Scarlet Letter* (1850), whose subject is hidden guilt in a Puritan town.)

## Explainer
In the 1830s and 1840s, Emerson and Thoreau told Americans to trust the self and find God in nature. Some of their neighbors were not convinced. Nathaniel Hawthorne, who knew the Transcendentalists and lived for a time at their Brook Farm community, wrote stories in which the self is exactly what cannot be trusted. Critics later grouped him with Edgar Allan Poe and Herman Melville under the label "Dark Romanticism."

These writers are still Romantics. They value emotion over cool reason, the strange over the ordinary, and the inner life of a single person. But where Emerson saw a "transparent eye-ball" in the woods, Hawthorne's "Young Goodman Brown" (1835) sends a young Puritan into the forest at night, where he sees, or seems to see, his whole respectable town at a witch-meeting. He comes back a bitter man. The narrator asks whether Brown only dreamed it and never answers. The story's point is what the vision does to him.

Poe brought a craftsman's method to this material. Reviewing Hawthorne's *Twice-Told Tales* in 1842, he argued that a tale should aim at one effect and that every sentence should serve it. His stories put the reader inside disordered minds. The narrator of "The Tell-Tale Heart" (1843) insists he is sane while describing a murder and the beating heart he still hears under the floor. Poe's narrators are rarely reliable, and the reader learns to read past their words.

Melville took the darkness to sea. In "Hawthorne and His Mosses" (1850), he praised Hawthorne's "great power of blackness," which he tied to the old Calvinist sense of original sin. A year later *Moby-Dick* (1851) showed Captain Ahab's hunt for the white whale destroying almost everyone aboard the *Pequod*. In "Bartleby, the Scrivener" (1853), a copyist who answers every request with "I would prefer not to" unsettles his employer without a single supernatural event.

Hawthorne's *The Scarlet Letter* (1850) gathers the main concerns: secret guilt, public shame, and symbols that change meaning as the story goes on. When you read these works, ask what each one suggests about the human heart, and how the narrator controls what you are allowed to know.
