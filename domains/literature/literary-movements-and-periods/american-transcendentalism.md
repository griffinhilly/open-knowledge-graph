# American Transcendentalism

## Core Idea
Transcendentalism was a New England movement of the 1830s to 1850s, centered on Ralph Waldo Emerson, Henry David Thoreau and Margaret Fuller. Its writers held that each person can reach spiritual truth directly through intuition, and that the divine is present in nature and in the individual soul. From that belief came their main themes: self-reliance, distrust of inherited institutions, close attention to nature, and moral resistance to an unjust state.

## How It's Best Learned
Read the "transparent eye-ball" passage from Emerson's *Nature* (1836) and a few paragraphs of "Self-Reliance" (1841), and mark each claim about where truth comes from. Then read Thoreau's "Where I Lived, and What I Lived For" from *Walden* (1854) to see the ideas tested in a single life. Ask what the writers reject as well as what they affirm.

## Common Misconceptions
- Transcendentalism was a church or a formal organization with a fixed creed. (It was a loose circle of writers and reformers, many from a Unitarian background, who disagreed with one another on many points.)
- Self-reliance meant selfishness or simple economic independence. (Emerson meant trusting the inner voice that he believed connects each person to a universal spirit, rather than conforming to custom.)
- The movement was only about enjoying nature. (It also drove social criticism: Fuller's *Woman in the Nineteenth Century* (1845) argued for women's equality, and Thoreau's "Resistance to Civil Government" (1849) defended refusing to support slavery and the Mexican War.)

## Explainer
In 1836 a group of New England writers and ministers began meeting to talk about religion, philosophy and literature. The group became known as the Transcendental Club. The name came loosely from the German philosopher Immanuel Kant, whose ideas had reached them through Samuel Taylor Coleridge and Thomas Carlyle. For these Americans, "transcendental" meant knowledge that goes beyond the senses and comes to us by intuition.

The same year, Ralph Waldo Emerson published *Nature*. Its most famous passage describes standing on bare ground and feeling the "currents of the Universal Being" pass through him: "I become a transparent eye-ball; I am nothing; I see all." The claim is large. Spirit is present in the woods, in the sky, and in each person who pays attention, as much as in any church or creed.

If that is true, then the individual does not need to borrow truth from authority. Emerson made this argument in "The American Scholar" (1837) and "Self-Reliance" (1841). "Trust thyself," he wrote, and "a foolish consistency is the hobgoblin of little minds." Self-reliance here means listening to the inner voice that Emerson believed linked every soul to a universal spirit.

Other members carried these ideas in different directions. Margaret Fuller edited the movement's journal, *The Dial*, from 1840, and in *Woman in the Nineteenth Century* (1845) she argued that women must be free to develop their full nature. George Ripley founded Brook Farm in 1841, an experiment in communal living. Henry David Thoreau built a cabin at Walden Pond on land owned by Emerson and lived there from 1845 to 1847. *Walden* (1854) reports that experiment in deliberate, simple living. In "Resistance to Civil Government" (1849), Thoreau defended his refusal to pay a tax to a government that supported slavery.

Transcendentalism shaped American writing well beyond its own circle. Walt Whitman's *Leaves of Grass* (1855) drew Emerson's praise. Other writers pushed back. Nathaniel Hawthorne and Herman Melville doubted its optimism about human nature. When you read a Transcendentalist text, ask where it locates truth, and what it asks the reader to stop obeying.
