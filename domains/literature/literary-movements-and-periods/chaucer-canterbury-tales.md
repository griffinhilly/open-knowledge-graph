# Geoffrey Chaucer and the Canterbury Tales

## Core Idea
The Canterbury Tales (late 14th century) represents the culmination of medieval narrative art through its diverse character types, realistic dialogue, and social observation. Chaucer's frame narrative allows him to juxtapose different social classes and genres, with each character's voice revealing identity. The work established English as a literary language and demonstrated literature's capacity to capture social complexity.

## How It's Best Learned
Read the General Prologue portrait of the Wife of Bath, then the opening of her own prologue, using a Middle English text with a facing translation. Read a few lines aloud to hear the verse. Compare how the narrator describes her with how she presents herself. Then read how the Miller interrupts to answer the Knight's Tale, and note how the frame lets tales argue with each other.

## Common Misconceptions
- The narrator's opinions of the pilgrims are Chaucer's own direct judgments. (The narrator is a persona, a genial pilgrim who often praises people his portraits quietly expose. He approves of the worldly Monk's views, for example.)
- The Canterbury Tales is a finished work that follows its announced plan. (The Host proposes that each pilgrim tell several tales, but far fewer survive, and the work is unfinished.)
- The tales are a loose anthology with no connection between them. (The frame links them. Pilgrims respond to, quarrel with, and answer each other's stories.)

## Explainer

The Canterbury Tales stands at the culmination of medieval narrative art, bringing together traditions that had developed over centuries and transforming them through Chaucer's genius for characterization and realistic observation. Medieval literature had perfected many forms—the courtly romance, the religious allegory, the saint's life—but these forms often emphasized ideals and abstract patterns over the messy reality of actual human beings.

Chaucer's innovation was to use a frame narrative—pilgrims journeying to Canterbury Cathedral, telling stories to pass the time—as a vehicle for social observation and psychological realism. The journey brings together people from different classes: a Knight, a Miller, a Prioress, a Merchant, a Wife of Bath. These characters are not idealized types but recognizable individuals with particular personalities, perspectives, and motivations. More importantly, Chaucer differentiates their voices. Each character speaks in a way that reveals their education, their personality, their values. The rough Miller does not speak like the courteous Knight; the clever Pardoner deploys rhetorical tricks.

This careful attention to how people speak was revolutionary. Medieval literature had not typically valued realistic dialogue as a way of revealing character. But Chaucer understood that what people say and how they say it reveals who they are. By juxtaposing different voices and classes within a single work, Chaucer demonstrated literature's capacity to capture social complexity—not by telling us how society works, but by showing us how different people within a society see things differently.

The Canterbury Tales also established English as a literary language. Before Chaucer, serious literature was written in French or Latin; English was provincial. By creating his masterwork in English, Chaucer proved the language capable of sophistication, range, and depth. The work's influence was immense: it demonstrated that literature could be both technically accomplished and deeply humane, both formally sophisticated and attentive to social reality.
