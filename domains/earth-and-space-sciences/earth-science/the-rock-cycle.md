# The Rock Cycle

## Core Idea
The rock cycle describes how rocks change from one type to another over long periods of time. Igneous rocks form when melted rock cools and hardens. Sedimentary rocks form when small pieces of rock pile up and get pressed together. Metamorphic rocks form when heat and pressure change an existing rock without melting it. Any rock type can become any other rock type given enough time and the right conditions. The rock cycle has no beginning or end — it keeps going.

## How It's Best Learned
Draw a triangle with the three rock types at the corners and label the arrows between them with the processes that cause each change (melting, cooling, weathering, cementing, heat, pressure). Use real rock samples so students can handle igneous, sedimentary, and metamorphic rocks while tracing how one could become another. Crayon shavings can model the cycle: shave crayons (weathering), press shavings together (sedimentary), warm and flatten them (metamorphic), melt and cool them (igneous).

## Common Misconceptions
- The rock cycle always follows one path (igneous to sedimentary to metamorphic). In reality, any rock type can transform into any other — there are shortcuts everywhere.
- Rocks are permanent and never change. All rocks are slowly changing, just on timescales too long for us to watch.
- The rock cycle destroys rocks. It transforms them, but the material is conserved — the same atoms get recycled over and over.

## Explainer
You already know that rocks come in three main types — igneous, sedimentary, and metamorphic. The **rock cycle** is the big idea that connects them all. It explains that rocks are not permanent. They are constantly being changed from one type to another by natural forces, just very slowly.

Think of it like water recycling through the environment. Water evaporates, forms clouds, falls as rain, flows into rivers, and eventually evaporates again. Rocks do something similar, but instead of taking days or weeks, it takes millions of years. A granite mountain gets worn down by wind and rain. The tiny pieces wash into rivers and settle on the ocean floor. Over time, those layers get pressed and cemented into sandstone — a sedimentary rock. If that sandstone gets buried deep enough, heat and pressure can change it into quartzite — a metamorphic rock. And if it gets hot enough to melt completely, the magma could cool into a brand new igneous rock.

The key insight is that the cycle does not follow a fixed order. A metamorphic rock does not have to melt before it can become sedimentary — it could be exposed at the surface, weather into pieces, and form sedimentary rock directly. An igneous rock does not have to become sedimentary first — it could be buried and metamorphosed right away. Every shortcut is possible because what happens to a rock depends on which natural forces reach it first: wind and rain at the surface, burial pressure underground, or volcanic heat from below.

Two engines drive the whole cycle. The Sun powers weathering and erosion by heating the atmosphere and driving the water cycle — rain, rivers, and glaciers break rocks apart and carry the pieces to new locations. Earth's internal heat powers the other half — it drives volcanic eruptions, pushes tectonic plates, and creates the temperatures and pressures that metamorphose and melt rocks deep underground. As long as the Sun shines and Earth's interior stays hot, the rock cycle will keep running.
