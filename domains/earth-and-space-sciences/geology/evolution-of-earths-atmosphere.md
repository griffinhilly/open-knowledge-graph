# Evolution of Earth's Atmosphere and the Great Oxidation Event

## Core Idea
Earth's early atmosphere, released mostly by volcanic outgassing, was rich in water vapor, carbon dioxide, and nitrogen and had almost no free oxygen. Water vapor condensed into oceans, and much of the CO₂ dissolved in seawater and was locked into carbonate rocks. Oxygen built up only after cyanobacteria evolved oxygen-producing photosynthesis, and it first rose to lasting levels in the Great Oxidation Event, about 2.4 billion years ago. The rock record of this change, including banded iron formations and red beds, shows that life and Earth's surface have changed each other over time.

## How It's Best Learned
Build a timeline from 4.5 billion years ago to today and place the evidence on it: the oldest zircons, the oldest stromatolites, banded iron formations, the first red beds, and the rise of animals. For each piece, ask what it says about oxygen at the time. Compare a pie chart of today's atmosphere with an estimate for the early atmosphere to make the change concrete.

## Common Misconceptions
- Earth's atmosphere has always been about 21 percent oxygen. (Free oxygen was nearly absent for roughly the first two billion years and reached modern levels much later.)
- Oxygen rose as soon as cyanobacteria appeared. (Oxygen was first used up by reactions with dissolved iron, volcanic gases, and other reduced materials, so a long delay passed before it could accumulate in the air.)
- The early atmosphere was much like Venus or Mars today in every way. (Earth's early atmosphere was CO₂-rich, but liquid water oceans removed CO₂ into carbonate rocks, a path that took a very different course on Venus.)

## Explainer
You have learned the geologic time scale and the makeup of today's atmosphere: about 78 percent nitrogen, 21 percent oxygen, and less than 1 percent argon, with a small but important amount of carbon dioxide. That mix is the product of more than four billion years of change.

Earth formed about 4.54 billion years ago. Any first atmosphere of light gases was probably lost early. The atmosphere that lasted came mainly from **outgassing**, the release of gases from the interior through volcanoes. These gases were mostly water vapor, carbon dioxide, and nitrogen. Impacts of comets and asteroids may have added some water and other volatiles. As the surface cooled, water vapor condensed and fell as rain to form oceans. Zircon crystals from Western Australia, about 4.4 billion years old, contain chemical signs that liquid water existed very early.

The young Sun was about 30 percent dimmer than today. Earth still had liquid water, probably because its atmosphere held much more CO₂ and other greenhouse gases. Over time, CO₂ dissolved in the oceans and reacted with rocks, and it was stored in carbonate sediments. This slow transfer is part of the long-term carbon cycle.

Oxygen came from life. **Cyanobacteria** evolved oxygenic photosynthesis, using sunlight to split water and release O₂. Stromatolites, layered mounds built by microbial mats, appear in rocks about 3.5 billion years old, though not all early mats made oxygen. At first, oxygen was consumed as soon as it formed. It reacted with dissolved iron in seawater, and the resulting iron oxides settled into **banded iron formations**. It also reacted with volcanic gases and other reduced minerals.

Around 2.4 billion years ago, oxygen sinks could no longer keep up, and oxygen began to accumulate in the air. This is the **Great Oxidation Event**. Evidence includes red beds on land, the loss of easily oxidized minerals from river sediments, and a change in sulfur isotope patterns that requires an oxygen-poor atmosphere before and an oxygenated one after. Oxygen also allowed an ozone layer to form, shielding the surface from ultraviolet light.

Oxygen stayed well below modern levels for a long time. A second rise in the late Neoproterozoic, roughly 800 to 540 million years ago, came before the spread of large animals. The history of the atmosphere is therefore a story of coevolution: Earth's surface shaped life, and life reshaped Earth's surface.
