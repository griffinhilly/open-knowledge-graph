# Burial, Compaction, and Diagenesis

## Core Idea
As sediments are buried, they lose water and consolidate through mechanical compaction (grain rearrangement) and chemical cementation. Temperature and fluid chemistry control mineral dissolution and precipitation, transforming sediment to rock. Diagenetic changes (grain contact pressure solution, stylolites) record burial depth and duration.

## How It's Best Learned
Track porosity loss with depth in well logs. Examine diagenetic textures in thin section to infer burial history.

## Common Misconceptions
- Cementation always requires external fluid source.
- Compaction is purely mechanical with no chemical change.
- Diagenesis stops at shallow depths.

## Explainer

From your study of sedimentary rocks, you know that sediments accumulate in layers at Earth's surface — sand on a beach, mud on a lake floor, shells on the ocean bottom. But loose sediment is not rock. The transformation from unconsolidated grains to solid stone happens through **burial, compaction, and diagenesis** — a suite of physical and chemical processes that begin the moment sediment is deposited and continue as it is progressively buried under newer layers.

**Compaction** is the most intuitive process. As overlying sediment accumulates, its weight presses down on deeper layers. Water occupying the pore spaces between grains is squeezed out, and grains rearrange into tighter configurations. Imagine stacking wet sponges: the bottom sponge gets flattened by the weight above, expelling water. In nature, muddy sediments can start with 60–80% porosity (mostly water) at the surface and compact to 10–20% porosity at depth. The rate and extent of compaction depend on grain size, shape, and sorting — well-sorted sand grains resist compaction better than platy clay minerals, which deform and realign under pressure.

But compaction alone cannot turn loose sand into sandstone. That requires **cementation**, the chemical side of diagenesis. As pore water circulates through buried sediment, it carries dissolved minerals — silica, calcite, iron oxides — that precipitate in pore spaces, binding grains together like mortar between bricks. The specific cement depends on the chemistry of the pore fluid and the temperature at burial depth. Calcite cement is common in marine sediments; silica cement dominates in deeply buried sandstones where temperatures drive quartz dissolution and reprecipitation. At grain contacts under high pressure, a process called **pressure solution** dissolves mineral material where grains press against each other, and that dissolved material reprecipitates nearby as cement. This creates distinctive sutured grain contacts and features called **stylolites** — jagged, dissolution surfaces that record significant compaction.

Diagenesis is not a single event but a continuum that spans from the surface to the boundary with metamorphism. Early diagenesis occurs at shallow depths and low temperatures — bacterial activity, oxidation of organic matter, and precipitation of carbonate concretions. Late diagenesis occurs at greater depths where temperatures reach 150–200°C, driving mineral transformations like the conversion of smectite clay to illite. The textures and cements preserved in a rock tell its burial history: what temperatures it experienced, what fluids passed through it, and how deeply it was buried. Reading these diagenetic signatures is essential for reconstructing basin history and for practical applications like predicting reservoir quality in petroleum geology, where porosity and permeability determine whether a rock can store and transmit fluids.
