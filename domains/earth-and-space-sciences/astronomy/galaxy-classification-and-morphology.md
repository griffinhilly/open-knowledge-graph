# Galaxy Classification and Morphology

## Core Idea
Galaxies are classified morphologically as elliptical, spiral, lenticular, or irregular based on their observed shape and structure. Hubble's sequence organizes galaxies by morphology in a way that correlates with color, gas content, and star formation rate. Galaxy morphology results from the interplay of gravity, angular momentum, and interactions with other galaxies. Understanding morphology provides insights into formation, evolution, and the distribution of matter in the universe.

## Explainer

When you look at images of galaxies, the diversity of shapes is immediately striking — tight spirals with sweeping arms, smooth featureless ellipses, and chaotic irregular blobs. **Galaxy morphology** is the systematic classification of these shapes, and the framework most widely used traces back to Edwin Hubble's 1926 classification scheme, often called the **Hubble tuning fork**. Understanding this system is your entry point into how galaxies form, evolve, and differ from one another.

The tuning fork divides galaxies into three main types. **Elliptical galaxies** (labeled E0 through E7, where the number indicates elongation) are smooth, rounded systems with little gas, dust, or ongoing star formation. Their stars are predominantly old and red, orbiting in random directions rather than in an organized disk. At the other end are **spiral galaxies**, which have a central bulge surrounded by a flat disk with prominent spiral arms. Spirals are further divided into normal spirals (S) and **barred spirals** (SB), depending on whether a bar-shaped structure of stars cuts across the center. Within each branch, the letters a, b, and c indicate how tightly wound the arms are and how large the central bulge is: Sa galaxies have tight arms and large bulges, while Sc galaxies have loose, open arms and small bulges. Sitting at the fork's junction are **lenticular galaxies** (S0), which have a disk and bulge like spirals but lack prominent spiral arms and have little gas — they appear to be transitional between ellipticals and spirals.

Morphology is not just about appearance — it correlates strongly with physical properties. Elliptical galaxies tend to be redder (older stellar populations), gas-poor, and found more often in dense galaxy clusters. Spirals tend to be bluer (active star formation in their arms), gas-rich, and more common in less crowded environments. **Irregular galaxies**, which fit none of the neat categories, are often small, gas-rich, and actively forming stars; the Large and Small Magellanic Clouds, visible companions to our Milky Way, are classic examples.

What determines a galaxy's morphology? The answer involves the interplay of **angular momentum**, gas content, and interactions with other galaxies. Galaxies that formed from gas clouds with high angular momentum tended to flatten into rotating disks — spirals. Those that formed through violent mergers of multiple smaller systems had their organized rotation disrupted, producing the random stellar orbits characteristic of ellipticals. Galaxy collisions and mergers can transform spirals into ellipticals, strip gas from spirals passing through dense clusters (turning them into lenticulars), and trigger bursts of star formation that reshape morphology. The Hubble sequence is thus not an evolutionary sequence from left to right, as Hubble himself initially speculated, but a snapshot of the diverse outcomes of billions of years of gravitational assembly and interaction.
